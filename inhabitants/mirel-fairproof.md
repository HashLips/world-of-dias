# Mirel Fairproof

## Name

Mirel Fairproof. Her boards are "Fairproofs," or "a proper board." In most of Nauw nobody bothers with either word: "a board" means one of hers.

## Overview

**Mirel Fairproof** is a Lineborn block-cutter and printer in the outer ring of Veloria City who makes the standard **Valhop Board**: the common face shown on the Plate of the Valhop Board, cut so that every circle, box, and block agrees with the street [`Valhop rules`](../rules/valhop-rules.md). Most boards unfolded on steps and benches in Nauw, Wabet, and Sorel came out of her workshop, or were printed from blocks cut by people she taught. Plenty of people own other boards. Most people still play on hers.

What she is: a working craftsperson with a narrow shop, a hand press, two apprentices, and a reputation for being faithful to the rules down to the width of a gouge. What she is not: the inventor of Valhop, the owner of its rules, or a judge of anyone's game. She did not design the board's layout. She made the **fair copy** of it, and she is careful to say so. Who printed the *first* board is still argued between Veloria's copy rooms and Secondfire's block-cutters (see Valhop Origins and Spread), and Mirel does not settle it. Her blocks are not Resonant Artifacts. An old Fairproof feels friendly in the hand for the ordinary reason that people have handled it a great deal.

## Description

### Look

Tall and narrow, as Lineborn usually are, with an outline as clean as a fresh cut and a side-on profile that nearly vanishes when she bends over the cutting bench. The shading inside her frontal plane runs in close diagonal crosshatch, a little darker toward the left shoulder. Children at the Softfruit Table Hall back bench love to tell her she looks like the hare block. She tells them the hare block looks like *her*, which is not the same thing, and then changes the subject. Her fingertips are black to the second joint whatever she does with soap. She wears a leather thumb-guard on her holding hand, a canvas apron with a row of gouge loops across the front, and a hank of undyed tie-strips over one shoulder, so that she can tie a board wherever she happens to be standing.

### The workshop

Her shop is a guild-door workshop on a cobbled lane behind the outer-market sheds, a few minutes' walk from the door of Softfruit Table Hall. It is outer-ring building of the ordinary kind (timber beams, lime plaster, flagstones, one wide window), and it has no sign. Instead, a folded and tied board hangs in the window, and the drying lines inside are visible from the lane: rows of freshly printed faces pegged up like washing, all identical, all drying black on pale. Neighbors say you can tell a good printing week from the lane by how evenly the sheets hang.

Inside: a cutting bench under the window, where the light comes in low and sideways and every edge shows; a flat hand press of wood and iron; tall stacks of pale kraft paper for the common run, which is most of what she prints, and bolts of plain undyed cloth bought at the outer market for the dearer cloth run; a tray of river pebbles sorted by size; and a bowl of fruit-pit dice for proofing. On the back wall hang her three spoiled first blocks, each with its fault scraped and initialed rather than planed away, because she trained in a copy room and believes in Scrape Not Erase Custom. The middle one has a bear block nearly the size of the moth's. Apprentices are shown it on their first morning.

### How she came to standardize the board

Mirel began as a junior in a Veloria copy room, where Lineborn are common and the first lesson is *every mark copied, nothing added*. She played Valhop at the Table Hall back bench after shifts, in the days when printed boards were still new and charcoal cloths were still everywhere. She noticed that most quarrels at the bench were not between the players. They were with the board. One sheet had nine circles on a row. One had a bear block as large as the moth's, so twenties fell like rain. One had hare hatching so faint that a die in it could be argued into the scenery. A Secondfire-cut board and a copy-room board, laid side by side, disagreed about the wolf.

So she did what copy rooms do with a disputed text: she went back to the source. For one First Fruit season she carried a slate to the back bench, to convoy drivers resting outside the market gates, and to anyone who would answer, and she wrote down the rules as players **said them aloud**, keeping only the parts every bench agreed on. Then she cut a block from the slate, not from any existing board. She spoiled three blocks before the bear came out small enough. The first run she gave away at the back bench. The second run she sold. By the third convoy season, Fruit-Road drivers were carrying her boards west on the empty return, and by the time the children who had learned on them were teaching their own, the Fairproof face had simply become the face.

She still cuts every new master block from a fresh copy of that rules slate, never from a worn block. "Copy the source," she tells apprentices. "A copy of a copy learns bad habits."

### Why hers is the standard

A Fairproof has nothing on it that the rules do not need, and everything the rules ask for is where a player expects it:

- **Two rows of exactly eight circles**, Team Moon's ending in the crescent and Team Sun's in the small rayed sun, under the big sun.
- **The big double-lined center box**, completely empty, so nothing printed inside it can be mistaken for a block.
- **The Switcheroo square** under the two looping arrows, sized so the spare pebble sits in it without overhanging.
- **The blank gap** below the big box, left wide enough that the fold never touches a printed line.
- **Six creature blocks** in the plate's two rows of three, in the right size order, with the silhouettes standing on their top edges and the marks hand-cut beside them.
- **Crisp hatching on the hare block.** Bold, even diagonals, cut deep so they never fill with ink, and spaced so the hatching still reads all round a die resting inside it. The 80% call depends on seeing the block's edge, and Mirel will not make it harder.
- **Busy scenery that never hides anything printed for play.** Mountains, river, waterfalls, castles, the empty boat, the ruined castle at the foot, and no people, exactly as the plate shows.
- **A blank back**, left entirely to the owner.
- **A pouch.** Every Fairproof leaves the shop folded once, tied, and packed in a plain undyed pouch holding the Switcheroo pebble and one unwaxed score rock. There is no die in it, because the die is the owner's own, made from whatever their region makes dice from, and the first rub of wax on the rock is the owner's too. The bench joke is that a Fairproof "comes with everything but luck."

### The cat, the wolf, and the hare

The rules say two things about the bottom row that sound, to new players, like a contradiction: the higher the multiplier, the smaller the block; and the cat's block (5x) and the wolf's (4x) are close to the same size. Mirel keeps both halves. She cuts the cat block smaller than the wolf block by **one gouge-width**: plain against a straightedge, invisible to an eye at bench height. When someone complains that the two look the same, she says, "They are close to the same size. The rules say so. The cat's is smaller. The rules say that too." Then she offers them a straightedge, which nobody ever takes.

The frog's block is the widest on the sheet, the moth's a little smaller, then the wolf's and the cat's, and the bear's the smallest. The **hare block** is not on that ladder at all, because it carries no multiplier. Mirel sizes it by its own measure, a middling block that is neither as wide as the frog's nor anywhere near as small as the bear's, and she says the hare "does not stand in the queue." It sits where the plate puts it, between frog and moth on the top row, and nobody has ever successfully argued that it should be bigger or smaller. Several have tried.

### Signature habits

- **The proofing ten.** Every board is proofed before it is folded. Mirel or an apprentice stands at the sun end and drops a fruit-pit die from above the sun ten times. The test is not the score. It checks that the sheet lies flat, that the ink has dried matte so a die does not skid on it, and that no cockle in the paper or cloth tips a die off the big box. A board that fails goes on the seconds pile. Seconds get one corner scraped as a mark and are given away, mostly to children at the Table Hall and to waystation keepers on the Eastbound Fruit Road, who say a scraped corner plays exactly as well and feels more honest.
- **The bear gauge.** A small slate square cut to the exact bear block hangs from her apron. Every print's bear block is laid against it. Fair hosts have asked her for a bigger bear so that crowds see more twenties; children have asked; one very persuasive Soft-Bear enthusiast from the canopy approaches asked twice. The answer has never changed: "If it were easy, it would be a frog."
- **The first fold.** She folds every new board herself, once across the gap, and ties it. "The first fold is mine. Every fold after belongs to whoever won."
- **The maker's mark.** Because the face is the common face and the back belongs to the owner, she prints her mark on neither. Instead, a small square holding **three diagonal strokes**, a scrap of the hare's hatching, is **blind-pressed** without ink into the lower-left corner notch beside the little star, where it shows only when light slants across the sheet. The same mark is inked on the pouch flap. Players tilt a strange board toward a lamp to "find the hare's thumbprint," and that is how a Fairproof is told from a copy.
- **Two runs, one face.** Most of what hangs on her drying lines is the kraft run: pale unbleached kraft, the cheap board, the one most people buy and most children learn on. A smaller cloth run is printed from the same blocks for people who would rather pay more. She proofs, folds, ties, and blind-presses both the same way, and will not say which is better. "Same face," she says. "One of them costs more to spill on."
- **Worn boards welcome.** People bring her kraft boards gone soft as old letters: white-cracked at every fold, corners rounded, tea-ringed, taped and stitched along the gap, the ink faded to a ghost of a bear. She is quietly prouder of these than of anything on her drying lines. She tilts each one toward the lamp to find the hare's thumbprint, checks the hatching, and offers to print a fresh one at the kraft price. She never insists, and first-board keepers nearly always say no, which she expects. "Keep it," she tells them. "It has better luck than anything I print. I only made the paper." A jar of gummed paper strips sits by her door for exactly these customers, free for the taking.
- **No edge on her own lines.** At the back bench, Lineborn are often asked to "give it the edge" on a close 80% call. Mirel always declines when the board is one of hers. "I cut that line. I'm not neutral about it."

### The rivalry with Secondfire

Secondfire's block-cutters, who learned their trade on seals and stamps, say the common face was theirs first. Mirel has never disputed it. She says only that if it was theirs, she has copied it faithfully, and this annoys them far more than an argument would. Secondfire calls Fairproofs "clerk boards," on the grounds that they look as if they are waiting to be stamped. Mirel calls Secondfire's prints "warm boards," because their heavier, rounder line is handsome and spreads a little in kiln-yard heat. Both names are said fondly, and in each other's hearing.

The running joke is **the hair letter**. Every Hearth Laughter Week a Secondfire board reaches her by carter with exactly one thing wrong: a bear block one hair too large, a ninth circle hidden among the pines, the crescent at the wrong end, once a wolf with its head down. Mirel finds the fault, scrapes a correction in the margin, initials it, and sends the board back with a new Fairproof that has nothing wrong with it and a note that says only "One hair." Secondfire's reply arrives the following season and says only "Two." Nobody in Veloria knows what "Two" means. Mirel believes Secondfire knows. Secondfire believes she does. The Kiln Yard Valhop Players believe the whole thing is the best game of Valhop ever played, and that nobody is winning.

## Beliefs About Them

The Table Hall Valhop Benchers say a Fairproof rolls kinder; Mirel says that is the flat press, not her. Copy-room clerks call her the only junior who ever left a copy room and made *more* paper. Sorel players say a Fairproof is too clean until it has been to Sorel, and they stamp its back until, in their words, "it isn't a Fairproof any more, it's a Sorel." Wabet hush players say nothing at all about her, which in Wabet is a compliment. Children believe she is made of the hare block, and she has stopped correcting them.

## Narrative Role

Use Mirel for warm Veloria craft scenes: a shop where everything hangs in rows, a maker whose pride is in being exact rather than original, an apprentice's first spoiled block, a traveler who wants to know why every board in F432 looks the same. She is a gentle authority on whether a *board* is right and a firm non-authority on whether a *throw* is. Her comic thread is the hair letter. Her rule is the rule she copied. The question of who printed first stays open.
