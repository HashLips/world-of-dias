# Valhop Board

## Name

Valhop Board. On steps and benches it is "a Hare sheet," "your board," or, when someone is already unfolding it, "go on then."

## Overview

A **Valhop Board** is the folding sheet that **Valhop** (street name *the Hare*) is played on. It is one printed sheet of paper or cloth (most often pale kraft paper, because cloth costs more), folded once and tied, kept in a small pouch that holds a die, a score rock, and the spare pebble. Two people spread it on a step, a bench, or a flat stone and play for ten to fifteen minutes.

It is a household and pocket object. It is not a sporting good, not a trophy, and not a stake: nothing is wagered on a Valhop Board. It is not a Resonant Artifact in the archive's sense either. Like everything in Dias it carries the ordinary echo of being handled, and an old board feels friendly in the hand for that reason only. Full play: [`Valhop rules`](../rules/valhop-rules.md).

The common pattern described below is the **standard board**. It is cut and printed in Veloria's outer ring by the Lineborn block-cutter [`Mirel Fairproof`](../inhabitants/mirel-fairproof.md), whose workshop (and the shops she taught) makes the boards most people own and most games are played on. Her boards keep every circle, box, and block faithful to the rules. People also own other kinds of board, from pocket quarter-boards to kiln-tile boards; those are described in [`Valhop Board Variants`](valhop-board-variants.md).

## Description

### The face

The face is printed in black on a pale ground from a carved block, so every board in the common pattern shows the same layout:

- **Top band.** A big rayed sun sits in the middle. To its left runs a row of eight circles that ends, at the outer edge, in a crescent. To its right runs a row of eight circles that ends, at the outer edge, in a small rayed sun. The crescent row is **Team Moon's**; the small-sun row is **Team Sun's**. The sun and the crescent are the game's own marks. The board makes no claim about the sky.
- **The big center box.** A large double-lined square, empty, under the sun. Every throw must touch it first.
- **The Switcheroo square.** A small square to the right of the big box, under two arrows that chase each other in a loop. The spare pebble lives here.
- **The gap.** An empty band of ground below the big box. This is where the board folds.
- **Six creature blocks** in two rows of three, each topped by a silhouette and a mark: **frog 2x**, **hare -x** (the hatched block), **moth 3x**; then **cat 5x**, **bear 20x**, **wolf 4x**. The higher the multiplier, the smaller the block. The bear's 20x block is the smallest on the sheet; the cat's and wolf's blocks are close to the same size.
- **Scenery** around everything: mountains, a winding river running down toward the big box, cliffs and waterfalls down both sides, pines, castles and a tower, a small sailboat, islands, and a ruined castle on an island at the foot. The scenery is decoration. A die resting on a waterfall scores only its top number.

The common pattern shows no people. It is printed and judged by eye; it is not a measuring instrument.

### The back

The back is the owner's. People stamp and mark it with their name and their own signs: a carved name-stamp, a kiln seal, a pressed leaf outline, a pattern of dots, a fruit-stain thumbprint, a small drawing of a favorite step. Some add the marks of places the board has been unfolded. Two boards with the same face never have the same back, and a lost board is usually returned because the back says whose it is.

### Fold and tie

A board is folded **once**, across the empty gap below the big center box, so the board's real crease never runs through the big box, the circles, or a block. A crease through a block would tip dice and start arguments. (Paper boards folded smaller for a pocket get palmed flat before play; see Paper, cloth, and years of folding below.) The folded sheet is tied with a cord or a cloth strip. By custom the winner of the last game folds and ties it, even when the board belongs to the other player.

### The pouch

A small pouch of cloth, leather, or woven fiber goes with each board. It holds:

- **The owner's die**, a six-sided die. Regional makers use local material: **fruit pits** in Nauw, **kiln clay** in Sorel, **quiet timber** in Wabet (offcuts and fallen wood, never fresh-cut for a toy, in keeping with the Wabet reciprocity harvest rule).
- **The owner's score rock**, a tiny stone rubbed with **tree wax or sap** so it sticks to its circle and does not fall off when the sheet shakes. A rock that has stopped gripping gets a fresh rub before the game.
- **The spare pebble**, the Switcheroo pebble, which sits on the Switcheroo square at the start of every game.

The second player brings their own die and score rock. That is why so many people in Nauw, Wabet, and Sorel carry one die and one slightly sticky rock in a pocket even when they own no board.

### Paper, cloth, and years of folding

Most boards are **kraft paper**. The common run of the standard board is printed on pale, unbleached kraft, closer to cream than to brown, stout enough to take a deep-cut block and cheap enough that nobody thinks twice about owning one. **Cloth boards** carry the same face on plain undyed cloth. They are quieter under a die, they survive a spill better, and they cost more to buy. That is the whole of the difference. Nobody reads anything into which kind a player unfolds: a cloth board is a board someone paid a little more for, or was given.

**The pocket square.** The pouch is how a board is sold and how it is kept at home. For daily carry, plenty of paper owners leave the pouch in a drawer. They give the board its proper fold across the gap, then keep folding until it is a square about the size of a palm, and slip it into a pocket beside the die and the sticky rock. The Switcheroo pebble rides tucked in the middle of the square, where it cannot wander off. The gap fold is still the board's real fold, and the winner still makes it and ties it; the extra folds are the owner's business afterwards. Those folds do cross blocks, so a pocket board is always **palmed flat** before the first throw: both players smooth it from the sun downward with the flats of their hands, and nobody minds the wait. A die that plainly tips on an old crease is thrown again, like any bumped die.

**How a paper board ages.** A new kraft board is crisp, smells of ink, and crackles when it opens. Give it some years of pockets and steps and it changes character. The paper goes soft and wrinkled, like a letter that has been read too often. The folds turn supple and a little fuzzy, and along every crease the black ink cracks into a fine white line, so an old board wears a pale grid over its mountains and waterfalls, a record of every pocket it has lived in. The corners round off. The cream darkens toward toast. Pebbles leave small grey smudges on the Switcheroo square, and the big box takes on faint dimples from a great many dropped dice. There are tea rings from Table Hall bowls, a pink fruit-juice bloom where someone's softfruit leaked at a convoy rest, a dusting of kiln grit that never quite brushes out. When a fold starts to part, owners patch it on the back with strips of gummed paper tape, or stitch it through with a few loose stitches of thread, keeping the stitches in the gap where no die will mind them. The ink fades from black to a soft charcoal. The circles and blocks still read, and that is all a game asks.

People play on these boards gladly. A worn board lies flatter than a new one, opens without crackling, and has opinions about which way a die should roll that its owner claims to understand.

**First boards.** Some players believe that the first board they ever owned is lucky for *them*, and stays lucky only while they keep playing on it. They will not replace it. They patch it, re-stitch it, and unfold it on steps where everyone else's board is newer, looking pleased. Others carry a worn first board in one pocket and a newer board in the other, play on the new one, and pat the old one before a difficult throw. It is a personal habit, not a rule of the game and not something anyone can prove, and it means nothing to anyone but the keeper. The bench joke that a new board "comes with everything but luck" gets a reply from first-board keepers: the luck comes later, with the tea rings. Benches tease the habit gently and respect it completely.

## Cultural Meaning

Unfolding a board on a step is an invitation to anyone carrying a die. The back of the board is a small autobiography, and reading someone's back marks is a polite way to start talking. The loser's custom of pouring the first bowl or handing over the first fruit makes losing a form of hospitality, never a debt.

People read the silhouettes as familiar animals. The frog is usually called a choir-frog (Verdant Choir-Frogs). The hare is a Softlane hare (Softlane Hares), which suits a block that makes you give way. The moth is a Banner Moth (Banner Moths), the cat a Lap-Cat (Veloria Lap-Cats), and the small bear with the biggest number a Soft-Bear (Canopy Soft-Bears). The wolf is a plain wolf. It is not drawn from any particular pack, and players do not give it one.

## Usage

Everyone plays: children learning to count, workers at the end of a shift, walkers resting, neighbors after supper. It is a casual pastime, not an organized sport. Familiar circles meet at Softfruit Table Hall in Veloria, on the Verdant Choir Meadows in Wabet, and at Kiln Laughter Yard in Secondfire.

Care is simple. Keep the sheet dry, or at least drier than the tea. Re-wax the score rocks. Put the pebble back on the Switcheroo square before folding. Make the board's real fold across the gap, never through a block; extra pocket folds are palmed flat before play.
