# Valhop Board Variants

## Name

Valhop Board Variants. On the street nobody uses the phrase. People say "a proper board" or "a Fairproof" for the standard one (with "a kraft" or "a cloth" if the material matters, which it rarely does), and name every other kind by what it is made of or where it lives: "a quarter," "a timber," "a tile," "a fair cloth," "a slate."

## Overview

**Valhop Board Variants** describes the standard **Valhop Board** and the other kinds of board people play **Valhop** on. The standard is the folding printed sheet made in Veloria by **Mirel Fairproof** (and by shops she taught), whose face matches the Plate of the Valhop Board. It is the board most people own, the one most people learn on, and the one most games are played on. Variants exist because people carry games to odd places, build with what they have, and like making things.

What it is: a field guide to the board kinds in ordinary use, and to how they differ from the standard. What it is not: a second rulebook. Every board here is played by the same street [`Valhop rules`](../rules/valhop-rules.md). A variant may change the size of the sheet, its material, or how it is carried. A board that changes the rows of eight, the order of the block sizes, the big box that every throw must touch first, or the hare and its pebble is not called a variant on the street. It is called "something else," and circles politely fold it up.

## Description

### The standard board

The Fairproof: one sheet of pale kraft paper in the common run (or plain undyed cloth, in the dearer cloth run), printed black on pale from a carved block, folded once across the blank gap below the big box, tied, and packed in a pouch with the Switcheroo pebble and a score rock. The face is the common face: two rows of eight circles under the big sun, the empty double-lined box, the Switcheroo square under the looping arrows, six creature blocks in the right size order, the hatched hare block, scenery with no people. The back is blank for the owner's marks. A small blind-pressed square of three diagonal strokes in the lower-left corner notch, visible only in slanting light, tells a Fairproof from a copy.

Secondfire's block-cutters print the **same face** from their own blocks. Their line is heavier and rounder and their sun a little fuller, but the layout and sizes are the common ones, so most players treat a Secondfire print simply as a board. Mirel calls them "warm boards" and, from her, "faithful enough" is high praise.

### Common variants

| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Quarter-board** | Convoy pockets on the Eastbound Fruit Road, Driftfall Piers crews, anyone short of pocket room | A small printed sheet, about a quarter of the standard face, folded once like any board and carried in a palm-sized pouch. | **Kiln-tile board** | Some Sorel yards and bench-tops, Hearth Laughter Week | The face is pressed into a set of clay tiles, fired with a matte glaze so dice do not skate, and set into a bench or a low wall. | **Fair cloth** | Ring-Fair Lights tables and First Fruit glut benches in Veloria | A large cloth, printed from a second, bigger block at the same proportions so the size order holds, and laid on a trestle so a crowd can see the blocks. ### Owner's marks across variants

Every kind of board still gets marked by whoever owns it. Cloth and paper boards are marked on the back, as with the standard. Quiet-timber boards are marked by burning a small sign into the underside of one panel. Tile boards carry their makers' seals on the edges. Fair cloths carry the host's mark. Quarter-boards are so small that owners often mark the pouch instead, and a lost quarter is identified by its pouch more often than by the sheet.

### Kraft, cloth, and worn boards

The standard comes in two runs from the same blocks. The **kraft run**, on pale unbleached kraft paper, is the cheap one, and it is what most people own and most games are played on. The **cloth run**, on plain undyed cloth, costs more to buy and goes longer between patches. That is all anyone means by it; a cloth board says nothing about its owner except that they paid more for a board. Secondfire's warm boards are printed on kraft as well, and with age their heavy line fades to a rusty grey that Sorel players consider handsome.

Material shows up a little by region. On the Verdant Choir Meadows, where the hush prefers a board that does not crackle, more players than elsewhere save for a cloth board, and a kraft board on the meadow is laid on a folded coat to quiet it. At Kiln Laughter Yard, paper boards brown faster on the warm stones, and nobody minds.

Kraft boards are the ones folded down into a palm-sized pocket square for daily carry, the pouch left at home (see Valhop Board), and they are the ones that age most visibly: soft and wrinkled, cracked into white lines at every fold, corners rounded, pebble-smudged, ringed with tea and fruit juice, patched with gummed paper tape or stitched along the gap, the ink faded to charcoal. A worn kraft board is **not a variant**. It is the same standard face, it settles a close call as well as a fresh one, and when two standards meet at a bench, politeness often lets the older one be unfolded.

Some players hold that their **first board** is lucky for them. They will not replace it, only patch it, and a few carry it folded beside a newer board they actually play on. Benches treat this as a personal habit, not a variant and not a rule.

## Cultural Meaning

Players like variants and play on the standard. The variant says where a game lives: on a pier, in a rainy timber house, in a kiln yard, at a fair. The standard says the game is shared, so that a driver from Wabet and a stall cook from Nauw can sit down at a strange bench and already know which block is the bear's. Most people own one Fairproof whatever else they own, and a well-marked Fairproof is still the board people ask to be passed down.

## Usage

- **For everyday play,** the standard: cheap (in kraft), light, folds once, and needs no explaining.
- **For travel with no room,** a quarter-board, accepting more rethrows.
- **For bad weather at home,** a quiet-timber or tile board, depending on the region.
- **For a crowd,** a fair cloth.
- **For teaching a child to count circles,** a slate, until the child wants a real one.

When two players with different boards sit down together, the custom is to play on the **standard** if either of them has one. If neither has, they play on the larger of the two boards, where a close call is easier to see.

## Notes

All variants are played by the shared [`Valhop rules`](../rules/valhop-rules.md); none adds pieces, stakes, or new blocks. The "something else" judgment on boards that change the rows, block order, box-first touch, or the hare is street opinion, not a law. Kiln Yard and Choir Meadow everyday play stays on folded sheets, as their entries describe. Standard board: [`Valhop Board`](valhop-board.md).
