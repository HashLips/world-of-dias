# Chalk on the Bell Board

## Premise

Lark stands in The Peel and Bell and chalks the strip and the orchard onto the board. She does not make a speech. The room understands it can go, and the going is already a loss.

## Story

The Peel and Bell was mid-morning thin: peels, wet wood, the bell unringing, a few bodies who had not gone to the shed and a few who had come back to recover their ordinary voices. Bram was wiping cups. Hullwater was not pouring itself. The ticket still tacked to the Peel Bell Board looked like a joke that had already collected.

Lark walked in with fuel on her coat and Wabet mud on her boots. She did not order. She took the chalk from the cup. Her hand knew tallies. Nia's hand would have recognized the letters.

She wrote, high enough to read from the benches:

*Lent Strip. Yarrow Bine's lend. West of the ditch.*

Under it:

*Mix orchard past the stones. Orrow. The glass tree. One fruit still on it.*

Under that, smaller, because smaller was harder to turn into a banner:

*I will not fly it out quiet.*

The chalk squeaked. That was the loudest thing she did. She set the chalk back in the grit. She turned around. Kett's mouth was open and, for once, late. Puck's blink, even without Ox-Patience, went long. Nia stood up so fast her bench complained. Yarrow was not there. Yarrow was the person the words were going to walk toward.

"You don't get to take that back by being sorry in a pretty way," Nia said. Not a question.

"I'm not taking it back," Lark said.

Bram looked at the board a long time. He could wipe a paid debt. This was not paid. This was a place, and places on his slate became roads. He rang the bell once. Not for a round. For the moment a room becomes one room and cannot pretend it missed the slate. People drifted to the board. A man who had cheered under a blimp yesterday swore softly, already calculating feet, days, a look at glass. Another said Sella's name wrong and Kett corrected it, having just learned it, protective and too late.

"Wagons will go," Nia said. "Curious is a kind of convoy. They will skip rests if you let them, and I will not let them, and the delay will sit on honest fruit. You did that. The shed did that. Don't stand there like the chalk was weather."

"I know," Lark said.

Dain was not beside her. She had landed on the public pad and he had left the stair as a pressure and a ruined cuff, into a city that could not invoice what it could not catch. A cup near the end of the counter tipped. No hand. Beer ran. Bram righted it and did not chase. There was nothing to grab unless the man forgot and brushed a sleeve through a puddle. He did not. The worse unseen had made him careful and furious somewhere between the door and the street. His absence was not peace. It was a debt walking.

Hess came in with his box and saw the board and sat down as if the box had gained weight. He did not open it. Ilen, passing the door, read the chalk, closed his eyes, and kept walking toward a counting room that would want a person to yell at. The person was not reliably visible. Lark stayed on her feet because sitting would look like a tale's ending, and she had not earned an ending. She had earned a field full of strangers and a pilot's name next to a lane.

Bram did not pour her a forgiving drink. He poured water. She drank it. The board dried at the edges of the letters, already permanent enough. Outside, someone called for a cart. The call was ordinary and it was the cost, starting, without a speech to soften the wheels.
