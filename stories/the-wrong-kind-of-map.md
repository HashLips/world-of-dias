# The Wrong Kind of Map

## Premise

Tamsy Kell comes west with a bag of slates. At the long table in the Letter House, Maren lays three records side by side: the original letter's margin columns, the survey ledger's float timings from the fourth day, and Tamsy's counts from Wetwake to now. The margins are not a position. They are what the water did in the hours before a Long Slack, and the same shape shows in Tamsy's slates before the fleet's slack. If it holds, the current announces its own rest a countable time ahead. And Tamsy's newest slates show the shape beginning again. It is a map of when, not where. It is only a guess. And it tells Maren the one place she might stand.

## Story

Tamsy came late in Hushlean, eighteen days out of Saltjaw, walking down the dune path into Sallow Strand behind a salt-cart. She had a canvas bag over her shoulder that clacked when she walked, and she was so thin and wind-browned that Maren did not know her until she was close.

"Brann sends love," Tamsy said, "and says the posts are still standing. And Bastien says if you need anything measured, he's got nobody to measure for, and to write." She swung the bag down onto the cookhouse step. It clacked. "And I brought these. All of them. I didn't know which ones you'd want, so I brought everything since Wetwake."

Slates. Two dozen of them, wrapped in rags, each one covered edge to edge in Tamsy's quick small strokes and dots: groups and rests, day after day, from the jetty and the skiff and the lighter's bow, every count she had taken since the morning Ansel told her to write down what she saw.

Maren looked at them for a long moment. Then she said, "Odile. I need the letter down."

---

They worked at the long table under the window for three days.

Odile brought the original down from the high shelf in its box and laid it on its cloth, letter side up, and nobody touched it but Odile, who turned it when they asked. Beside it Maren opened the survey ledger to the fourth survey day, where Ansel had written out the float timings in columns, timed float by timed float, and beside them, *Irregular set SW of shelf edge. Pulses in groups. Group pattern drifts. Counted first by T. Kell by eye; confirmed by float and glass. Cause unknown. Do not guess cause.* And on the other side of the table, in rows, Tamsy laid out her slates in order of days.

The two Songripe coasters came, because they had been arguing about the margins since Brightpass and would not have forgiven anyone who solved it without them. Wick came, and sat in the corner, and said nothing, and handed people things.

"First rule," Maren said, before anyone began. She put a hand flat on the ledger. "His rule. *Write what you measured. Write what you guessed. Never let the second look like the first.*" She looked round at them. "Whatever we think we find, we write it in two columns."

---

The margins came first.

Ansel had started counting, the letter said, to stay awake. He had started at the top of the left margin of the letter side and gone down it, and then down the right, and then turned the leaf over and gone down both margins of the drawing side, in the fog and the dark, by feel and by the faint blue light he had written about. Strokes for pulls. Dots between them where he had marked a short rest. A dash for a long rest. Groups set apart by a little space.

*6 6 6. 6 6 5. 6 5 5.* At the top of the left margin, the groups ran sixes and fives, the pattern shifting a little each cycle, exactly as the ledger said the current had run on the fourth day. Tamsy put her finger on the ledger column and then on the margin and said, "That's the same water," and nobody argued.

Down the left margin the fives began to drop out. *6 6 6. 6 6 5. 6 6 6.* By the bottom of it, a seven: *6 6 7.* At the top of the right margin, more sevens. *6 7 7. 7 7 7.* The dots between the groups were getting further apart. Then they were dashes. The long rests were getting longer.

On the drawing side, down past the sketch of the cliff, the groups were sevens, all sevens, with a dash between every one. Then two dashes. Then three. And at the very bottom of the right margin of the drawing side, under the last group, Ansel had drawn a single long dash, the whole width of the margin, and stopped. No group came after it.

"That's when he stopped counting," said one of the coasters.

"That's when he stopped to write," said Tamsy. Her voice had gone strange. "Look how long the rests were getting. By dawn the water was hardly counting at all. He was sitting in a rest that long when he put the pencil down and started the letter." She looked up. "He counted the water right up to the edge of a long slack and didn't know what it was. It must have come while he was writing, or just after. He wrote *I am putting this into the outward water*, and the outward water was the slack. The run. That's when the canister went. The morning after the strike."

Odile, who had kept the letter for half a year and never understood its margins, sat down very slowly on the bench.

---

Then they laid out Tamsy's slates, from the middle of Fruitcarry, when she had started telling Bastien the groups were stretching, through to the morning the fleet went out.

It took them most of the second day. The slates were not as tidy as Ansel's margins; they were counted on different days, at different hours, from different places, and some were smudged and one had been sat on. But when they laid them out in order, and the coasters tied what they read into knotted cord the way Songripe tide-readers did, and Maren ran her finger down the rows the way she ran it down a tag board, the shape was there.

Sixes and fives, drifting. Then the fives dropping out. Then the first seven. Then the sevens coming in, and the rests lengthening, and the dashes. And then a slate from the lighter's bow, the morning before the fleet went out, that said in Tamsy's hand *rests long, long, long* and then *flat*.

"It's the same," said the coaster who talked less. "Same turn. Same order. Fives go, sevens come, rests stretch, flat."

"How long?" said Maren. "From the first seven to the flat. In his. In hers."

They counted. They counted again. The coasters counted on their cords, and Tamsy counted on her fingers, and Maren counted in her notebook in two columns.

In Ansel's margins, from the first seven to the long line, there were a certain number of groups. It was a single night's counting, and the night had been short.

In Tamsy's slates, from the first seven to the flat morning, the groups were spread over nearly four weeks. Sevens came on her slates in the last days of Fruitcarry, and the fleet's slack did not come until early Hushlean.

"That's not the same," said the young man from the Basin, who had come to watch and been allowed to stay if he kept quiet. He had not kept quiet.

"No," said Maren slowly. "The calendar's not the same. It never is. Two or three times a year, Tamsy says, and never at the same distance twice. If you count days you'll never catch it."

"But look at the groups. Not the days. Count the groups." She put her finger on Tamsy's slates. "From the first seven to the flat. How many groups."

They counted.

It was close. Not the same, but close. Within a handful.

---

"Two columns," Maren said, when the light was going and the table was covered in slate dust and knotted cord. She opened her notebook and drew the line down the middle of a clean page.

In the first column she wrote: *MEASURED. In two records (his margins, the night after the strike; T.K.'s slates, Fruitcarry into Hushlean), the counting current ran the same shape before a long slack: fives drop out, sevens come in, rests lengthen, then flat. In both, the count of groups from first seven to flat is close. Not equal. The days between are not close at all.*

In the second column she wrote: *GUESSED. That the current announces its own long rest. That once the turn begins, the slack comes in a countable number of groups, whatever the calendar says. That this would let someone who counts know a slack is coming some days ahead. Two cases only. The withdrawal broke the water and may have changed it. Do not trust this.*

Then she stopped writing, because Tamsy had picked up the last slate in the bag, the one she had not laid out yet, and was holding it very still.

"This is from the jetty," Tamsy said. "The day before I left Saltjaw. The water's been broken since the fleet. White, angry, no shape to it for weeks. Then it started counting again. Sixes and fives." She turned the slate toward them. "And then, the last three days before I came away. I counted on the morning I left, too, before the cart. Look."

*6 6 6. 6 6 5. 6 6 6. 6 6 7.*

The fives were dropping out. There was a seven.

Nobody said anything for a long time.

"If it's true," said Odile at last, side-on at the end of the bench, almost invisible in the dusk, "then there's a long slack coming. And you know when, near enough."

Maren had already done it, in the margin of the notebook, in pencil, small: the groups in Ansel's turn and the groups in Tamsy's, the pace of the water on Tamsy's last slates, the eighteen days on the road. If the shape held, and the pace held, and nothing the island had done had broken it, the water off the shelf would go flat within ten days. Perhaps a dozen. She rubbed the pencil out with her thumb before anyone else could read it.

"And the run goes out on it," said Odile. "West."

"If it's true," said Maren. She looked at the two columns in her notebook. "It's a map, Odile. It's the wrong kind. It doesn't say where anything is. It says when the water stops. That's all."

"That's a great deal," said Odile, "to somebody with a boat."

"Yes," said Maren. She closed the notebook. "Which is why it doesn't leave this house. Not the shape. Not the count. Not the *when*. You heard my rule."

"I heard it." The young man from the Basin spoke, quietly for once. "I'm keeping it."

---

Later, when the others had gone and Odile had put the original back in its box on the high shelf, Wick Hollen came out of his corner for the first time in three days.

"The run," he said. He had a slow voice, Wick, the voice of a man who said every word on the shingle as softly as if Glasswake were listening. "The outward water. When it comes in, I know where it comes in. Things off the Sorel side, they come ashore east of the rock, a quarter of a morning's walk. Same cove every time. That's where I found his canister. There's an old wreck bell in the shallows at the mouth of it, fallen off some boat nobody remembers. At the lowest tides it knocks." He looked at Maren. "We call it Low Bell Cove."

Maren sat very still.

"I'm not telling you to go there," said Wick. "I'm telling you that if I were waiting for something to come off that water, that's where I'd wait."

## Notes

Chapter 29 of **The Island Saga**. The margins of [The Kestry Letter](../artifacts/the-kestry-letter.md) are read for the first time. They record **the counting current's turn toward the Long Slack** on the night after the strike, ending in a single margin-wide dash: the rest Ansel was sitting in when he stopped to write. Compared with the [Saltjaw Shelf Survey Ledger](../artifacts/saltjaw-shelf-survey-ledger.md)'s day-4 float timings and Tamsy's slates before the fleet's slack, they show the same shape (fives drop out, sevens come in, rests stretch, flat) and a similar *group* count from the first seven to the flat, even though the *calendar* spacing differs widely. This is written up explicitly as **hypothesis**, in two columns, following [Shelf survey measurement constraints](../rules/shelf-survey-measurement-constraints.md).

Tamsy's newest slate shows the turn beginning again. The finding is a map of time, not place. Under Maren's rule no "when" leaves the house. [Low Bell Cove](../regions/low-bell-cove.md) is named here as the cove where [Wick Hollen](../inhabitants/wick-hollen.md) found the canister, where things from the outward run come ashore.
