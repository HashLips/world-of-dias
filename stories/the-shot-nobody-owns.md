# The Shot Nobody Owns

## Premise

Hadrin Stell takes a bow from a frightened bowman to put a ward-bolt into the boom, which is the procedure the writ calls "placing a ward." The bolt goes where bolts go on a trembling morning. In less time than a single long count, the fleet and the island do what neither wanted. Boarding hooks go onto the boom. A Driftfall cutter rides up on a timber and turns over, and a Ward oarsman drowns. A second, panicked bolt kills a young islander at her oars. Tavi Corran is hooked and goes into the water, heavy as iron. Ansel, who floats, goes in after him. Afterward, nobody will say whose shot it was.

## Story

Maren never got to whistle.

The breath was in her, and her lips were shaped for it, when Hadrin Stell said, "Give it here," and took the bow out of his bowman's trembling hands.

He did not shout. That was what everyone remembered afterward, when they remembered anything in the same way. He did not shout or make a speech or call the fleet to arms. He took the bow the way a foreman takes a pick from a young miner who's holding it wrong, and he looked along the bolt with its little grey ward-pennant tied below the head, and he said, in an ordinary voice, as if reading from the writ, "Hazard ward, placed," and he loosed it at the boom.

It was the procedure. Ward contingents marked obstructions that way at bleed sites and on reef edges: a bolt with a grey pennant into the thing itself, so that the next boat would know. Hadrin had done it a hundred times. He meant to put it into a timber of the ring forty strokes to the left of Nessa's boat. Perhaps he believed that was all he was doing. Perhaps he believed that once the ward was placed the writ was answered and the rest could be talked about. Perhaps he was simply a frightened man who had come a long way to put a mark on something and could not bear to go home without one.

The bolt went where bolts go on a flat, shadowless morning when the man loosing them has a scar across his chin and a reason to be angry. It struck the timber. But it struck it a hand's width from the narrow boat's bow, and the grey pennant whipped across the young rower's face, and Tavi Corran stood up in the boat with a roar that the cliffs threw back three times.

---

Afterward Maren could never put it back in order. Nobody could. The Concord marshals tried, that night, and their four books said four different things.

What she remembered was this.

Nessa raising one hand, palm out, toward the Ward cutter. Not a threat. A gesture that meant *stop*.

The swing-gap in the boom opening. Not for Nessa's boat. For three more narrow boats coming out from the harbour fast, boom crews with long hooked poles they used for mending joints, and to frightened men on a Ward cutter long hooked poles looked like spears.

Hadrin shouting, at last, but too late, and nobody afterward agreed on what: *hold*, or *hooks*, or both.

Severin on the lighter's rail, standing up on it, which a Choirglass should never do on a boat, his voice ringing with that second overtone so that it seemed to come from two places at once: "*Hold! Hold! Cases on! Hold!*"

Two Driftfall cutters, whose crews had signed on for marks and had no idea what they were in, swinging toward the boom because the Ward cutter had, oars clashing. Ward boarding hooks going out from the lead cutter onto the timbers, to hold the cutter off them or to pull it in; no one ever said which. One of the Driftfall cutters, driven in too fast, riding up onto a timber at a joint exactly as the Patient Lead had, in fog, on a hard pull, the season before. It hung there for a heartbeat, tilted, and then rolled off the far side and turned over in the quiet water.

A Ward oarsman in grey, thrown from the cutter's waist, going under between two timbers and not coming up. The timbers knocking together over the place where he had been. *Tok.*

And a second bolt. From the lead cutter, from somewhere along the rail; not from Hadrin, whose bow was empty. A young bowman's bolt, loosed at a boom crew's hooked pole because it was coming toward him. It went past the pole and took the young rower from Nessa's boat in the chest, the girl with the plain face who had rowed beside Tavi, and she sat down on her thwart very suddenly, as if she were tired, and slid sideways into the water.

---

Tavi went in after her. He did not dive; he stood up and stepped over the side, and a Ward boarding hook that had been swung at the boom caught him across the back of the shoulder as he went, and opened him, and he went down into the quiet water like a dropped anvil.

Kharad bodies are dense. Dorrit Vash had once said, laughing, that she sank in a bath, and Dorrit was in the ground above the Saltjaw outfall now. Tavi was Kharad, or so near it that the water did not care about the difference. Out there past the shelf there was no bottom on the lead.

And from the harbour, a figure with a crest went off the edge of the stones.

He did not dive either. He could not; he had never been able to. He jumped feet-first, the way children jump off the Saltjaw jetty, and came up at once, as he always came up, riding high, his feathers holding air, unable to sink if he tried. The second narrow boat at the harbour, his boat, the one that should have taken him home, was already moving with two rowers in it and he did not wait for it. He swam, badly, the way he had always swum, splashing and slow, toward the place where the water still boiled from Tavi going under.

Maren saw him reach it. She saw him put his face in the water and reach down with both arms, and come up with nothing, and do it again, and on the third time come up with a fist in the cloth of Tavi's shirt and Tavi's great iron head breaking the surface beside his. She saw him hold it there. Not swimming. Not pulling. Just floating, the one thing his body knew how to do, with all of Tavi's weight hanging from him and his own face going under on every pull and coming up again, until the narrow boat reached them and six hands hauled Tavi in over the side and two more hauled in Ansel.

The same body that had carried him away from Brann and Tamsy on the timber. The same body that had made him useless to Dorrit. He had found the one thing in the world it was for.

---

It had taken less than a single long count. Less than a hundred breaths. Afterward Tamsy said she had been counting under her breath the whole time without meaning to and had got to eighty-seven.

The Ward cutter had backed off the boom. The overturned Driftfall cutter floated bottom-up, its crew clinging to it, all but the Ward oarsman who had been aboard, and Bastien was bringing the lighter around to them, shouting for lines. Brann was already over the side into the skiff, rowing for the nearest man in the water. Severin had come down off the rail and stood on the deck with his hands open at his sides and his colour gone white all through, which Maren had never seen a Choirglass do and never wanted to see again.

Nessa's boat was moving back toward the swing-gap, slow, the old woman alone at the oars now, rowing with her eyes on the lead cutter. In the bottom of the boat lay the young rower, very still, her face covered with Nessa's undyed coat. The other narrow boats were falling back with her. The boat with Tavi and Ansel in it was already at the harbour stones.

On the harbour, Maren could see people carrying Tavi up the steps. She could see a figure with a crest walking beside him with one hand on the litter, soaked, feathers plastered flat. She could not see whether he looked back.

The swing-gap closed behind the last narrow boat. *Tok.*

---

Nobody owned the shot. That began at once and never stopped.

"I placed a ward," Hadrin Stell said, on his cutter, to no one, to everyone, to the Concord marshal writing in his book. "Under writ. Into the obstruction. As written. I did not loose on any person." His voice was steady and his hands were not. "The second bolt was not mine. I gave no order."

"I heard *loose*," said the young bowman who had loosed the second bolt, and was sick over the rail, and said it again. "I heard someone say loose."

The backers' agent said nothing at all. He had gone below.

The four Concord marshals wrote in their four books. *A bolt with ward-pennant loosed by the Ward captain into the ring of timbers*, one wrote. *Bolts loosed*, wrote another. *Ownership of the second disputed*, wrote the third. The fourth, an old woman who had kept the marshal's book at Span Toll for thirty years, wrote *one of ours drowned, one of theirs killed, and nobody will say who began it*, and underlined it twice.

Up on the island's seaward terrace, very faint across the water, so faint that at first Maren thought it was the blood in her own ears, someone had begun to count.

## Notes

Chapter 25 of **The Island Saga**. The clash is **brief, costly, and the exception**: fewer than a hundred breaths. The dead: one Ward oarsman drowned between timbers, and one Aumeri rower, the young woman in Nessa's boat, killed by a second, panicked bolt. Tavi is opened by a boarding hook. "Ward-bows" are ordinary bows; placing a ward means a grey-pennanted bolt shot into an obstruction, which is standard Ward practice at reef and bleed sites.

The shot nobody owns: [Hadrin Stell](../inhabitants/hadrin-stell.md) fired the first bolt as "procedure" and denies firing on people. The second bowman says he "heard *loose*." The four [Nauw Concord](../cultures/nauw-concord.md) marshals' books disagree. Ansel's buoyancy, which separated him from his crew in chapter 5, is what saves [Tavi Corran](../inhabitants/tavi-corran.md). He does not fly or dive. He floats. The count beginning on the terrace leads into chapter 26 and the [Aumere withdrawal resonance rule](../rules/aumere-withdrawal-resonance-rule.md).
