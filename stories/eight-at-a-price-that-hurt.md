# Eight at a Price That Hurt

## Premise

Eight fruits go for prices that hurt. Hess buys and will not eat. On the way out, the Brindle Crawler proves it is a slow real train and not the joke rail.

## Story

The numbers started as theater and became pain so gradually that Ludo did not notice the change in his own mouth until it ached.

"One," he said, and the first Vitrin — a whole fruit, not a wedge, glass-heavy, ringing when the bowl kissed it — left the table for a woman from the basin who paid with a stack and a face like a person signing a house she meant to fear. The room made the sound money makes when it is too large for pockets. VEL marks, counted twice, Ilen's slate eating figures. Dain's voice, still unhoused in a face, said "good" the way a man says good to a dog that has fetched something expensive.

Hess went in on the third. He had told himself he would watch. His knee betrayed him into a raise. Ludo saw the face, as Ilen had warned, and priced the face. Hess met it because not meeting it would have meant sitting in his body while someone else carried glass out of the room. He wrote a number he had practiced only as a joke about buying a narrow house with a good window. The joke died. The fruit was his. He did not take the bite Dain offered as a flourish. He put the Vitrin in a box with both hands shaking and the lid on before the want could become a mouth.

"You have to taste it," the unseen said, delighted, fraying.

"I have to get it home," Hess said. "Tasting is how you stopped having a face."

A laugh from the back, thin. Ilen bought the fifth for the counting room, historic and budget colliding on the slate until the budget limped away and hid. He looked ill in a tidy way. Yarrow did not bid. When Ludo's eyes snagged on him he shook his head once, a holder refusing to buy back a trouble that had grown on the edge of his lend. Eight fruits. Eight wounds in various purses. Dain announced the arithmetic as if it were modesty.

"The tree gave about ten," he said. "I ate one so you would not think I was selling a rumor. I keep one. You have the rest that were ready. If you wanted a ninth you should have been the farmer."

The farmer was not named. Lark felt the omission like a strap pulled too tight.

They spilled into the day afterward, rich and frightened, a procession that did not know how to walk. Wagons still occupied the stone. Nia looked at the boxes and did not ask. Above, the blimp had begun to drift off-station, Cress done with the hire, the painted peel turning its bite away. Hess, Ilen, and a cluster of the merely shaken ended up at the siding because the walk to the inward rings felt too open, as if an unseen man might lift what was left of their dignity for sport.

The Brindle Crawler waited with its modest stack already smudging the air. Soot, a shop-bell, wooden slats, a visible track. Jory Milt waved from the footplate with a kettle at his elbow.

"Depot," he said. "Not a tour of your feelings. Climb up if you're coming. We're slower than that man's ox if the grade is rude. I like the grade rude. It tells the truth."

A buyer, giddy with having survived beauty, said, "And if we buy the wrong ticket, do we get the same platform back? I hear there's a rail that—"

Jory's wave stopped. "Don't," he said, pleasant as a brake. "That story is a pier joke about doors that open where you started. I have two platforms. They are different. I will show you the stain on the depot one if you are bored. I do not stamp dreams. I do not hum you into another band. I take crates and frightened people a short way, and I arrive."

The engine sneezed black. The train crawled. Hess held his box on his knees and watched the first platform leave and the second approach, ordinary iron, ordinary lateness of four minutes which Jory announced like a psalm. Nothing shimmered. The fruit in the box did not ferry him. It only weighed, and rang once when the car rocked, a private bell, and Hess put his palm over the lid until the ringing agreed to be a secret he was already tired of keeping.
