# Blue in the Fog

## Premise

The drift carries Ansel into the blue bank by morning. The sun sits where his bearings say it cannot. The water is too quiet to be the sea. A real shore rises out of the mist, cliffs and terraces and walls, and between him and it lies a line of dark timbers that he recognizes and does not want to recognize.

## Story

The blue was colder than the fog.

He felt it before he saw it: a change on the skin of his face where the feathers stopped, a damp that was finer than fog damp, almost dry, like the air in a deep drift after rain has been through the rock. Then the grey around him took on colour, the way a lamp shows through a tent wall, and he was inside it.

It was not dark. That was the first thing that unsettled him. A fog bank should dim the day, and this one did not. The light inside the blue seemed to come from everywhere at once, low and soft and even, so that his hands on the canister strap, which was empty now and lay slack across his chest, cast no shadow on the water. When he breathed out, his breath did not show. When he moved his fingers through the mist, the droplets were so fine that he could not see them separately at all, only feel them as a coolness. It smelled of nothing. Not salt, not rot, not vent. Of stone, perhaps, if stone had a smell when it was clean.

*Not Vent Haze,* he thought, because Vent Haze was sweet and gritty and stank of eggs. *Not ordinary sea fog. Not anything I have a word for.* He did not try to find one. He had learned long ago that the quickest way to stop seeing something is to name it too early.

The sea underneath had gone quieter still.

He had thought the water after the strike was quiet. This was another order of quiet. There was no swell at all. The surface lay like oil in a lamp-dish, and when he moved an arm the ripples went out from it in perfect rings and did not come back. He could hear his own heart. He could hear the creak of the canister strap. Far away and very faint, so faint that he could not be sure he was not inventing it, he could hear something like a slow, deep knocking, wood against wood, from somewhere ahead.

He tried to fix his position. It was a stupid thing to try and he tried it anyway, because it was what he was. The sun was a pale disc through the blue, and he lifted his hand and measured its height above the water with his fingers, and its bearing against the line of his body as he lay. Then he thought about where the sun should be at this hour of a Wetwake morning, off Saltjaw, a half-day's drift south and west of the shelf edge.

It was wrong. Not a little wrong. The sun was too low and too far round. Either it was much earlier than he thought, which it was not, because he had counted through the whole night, or he was much farther south and west than he had guessed. Days farther. A distance he could not have drifted in one night.

He lay on the water and made himself say it aloud, quietly, the way he said the groups. "I don't know where I am."

It was, in its way, a relief. The guess on the letter had been wrong. He had marked it GUESS. He had not lied.

---

The shore came out of the blue slowly, like a face coming up out of sleep.

First a paleness, low and long, that he took at first for a brighter patch of mist. Then edges in the paleness. Then a cliff, not high, perhaps twice the height of the Saltjaw headframe, pale grey stone with the faint green of lichen on it, rising straight from a narrow strip of shingle. And across the face of the cliff, level as a mason's line, the terraces: shelves cut into the rock one above another, some of them green, some of them with low walls along their edges. Above the highest terrace, set back from the brink, there were shapes that were not rock. Squared shapes. Roof-lines.

He had drawn this. He had drawn it on the back of the letter with a wax pencil and the side of his thumb, from twenty breaths of seeing it at dusk. He had drawn it badly. It was real.

It was very quiet. No gulls. No surf on the shingle; the water simply lay against it. A thread of smoke went up straight from somewhere behind the roof-lines and did not bend. Nothing moved on the terraces that he could see.

And between him and the shore, perhaps two hundred strokes off, lay the line.

He saw it as the drift brought him closer: a long low row of dark timbers lying in the water end to end, so low that only their backs showed, like a string of sleeping seals. They were lashed together with heavy rope at their joints, and from some of the joints ropes ran down into the water, taut, as though something below held them. Every so often, between the timbers, a rounded block of pale stone sat half-awash, bound in a cradle of rope. The whole line ran away into the blue to the left and right further than he could see, curving gently, as if it went all the way round.

As he watched, a slow pulse went along it. One timber lifted a little and settled. Then the next. Then the next. *Pull, and rest.* The Counting Current was moving the line, or the line was moving with the current, and where it settled each timber knocked against its neighbour with a deep, dull sound, wood against wood and stone. *Tok.* Far down the line. *Tok.*

He knew that sound.

He had felt it come up through the keel of The Patient Lead in the fog. He had seen a timber like this one, long and dark and roped, lying in the water with Tamsy kneeling on it and Brann's face in his arms.

He did not let himself finish the thought. He was too cold and too tired, and if he finished it he thought he might stop floating out of pure grief. *Measure,* he told himself. *By eye. Once. You don't know yet. You don't know.*

The drift brought him against the line. His shoulder touched a timber; it was slick and cold and solid as a wall. He put his arms over it because there was nothing else to do, and lay there half on it and half in the quiet water, looking at the shore.

There were people on the cliff.

He had not seen them arrive. They were simply there, on the edge of the second terrace, three or four figures standing very still, looking down at him. They were too far away to see faces. One of them lifted an arm, not waving: pointing, perhaps, or signalling to someone he could not see. Below them, at the foot of the cliff, a low boat came out from a cleft in the rock that he had taken for shadow, and two figures in it began to row, unhurried, toward the line.

He wanted to call to them. He found that he could not think what to say. All the words he had were for Maren, and he had put them in a canister and given them to the sea.

So he did the only thing he had left. He took one arm off the timber, made a fist, and knocked twice on the wet wood. *Tok. Tok.* And he tried to whistle, rising, and his lips and beak were too cold, and only air came out.

The boat kept coming.

## Notes

Chapter 7 of **The Island Saga** (Act II, *The Shore Nobody Charts*). First full view of [Aumere](../regions/aumere.md) and [the Stillmouth](../regions/the-stillmouth.md). The sun-bearing error is a measurement fact, not magic: Ansel has drifted much farther than one night could account for at any rate he knows, and [Shelf survey measurement constraints](../rules/shelf-survey-measurement-constraints.md) explain why dead reckoning in fog on that water fails. How the Counting Current moves things that far stays unexplained.

The line of roped timbers is the [Aumere Turning Boom](../artifacts/aumere-turning-boom.md). Ansel suspects it struck The Patient Lead and refuses to conclude it. Confirmation comes in chapter 9.

The blue mist casts no shadow and shows no breath. Its properties are recorded in [Stillmouth Blue Mist](../phenomena/stillmouth-blue-mist.md) and are not explained. It is not Vent Haze, not Blue Litter, and not a bleed sky.
