# Learning the Shore

## Premise

Three weeks on Aumere. Ansel learns what the islanders eat, how their houses keep heat, how their days run, and the small dawn ritual of the Tide-Count. When a cistern gallery starts to weep, his mining knowledge turns out to be the one thing the island did not already have.

## Story

He learned the island the way he had learned Saltjaw: by being useless in it first.

For the first days he could do nothing but sleep and eat and sit by the warm stones. The Aumeri let him. Nobody asked him to work. Nobody, apart from Nessa and occasionally Tavi, asked him anything at all. They came and went past the door of the stone house on their own business and looked in at him with the same long quiet look he had first seen from the cliff, as if he were weather they had not yet decided how to dress for.

By the fourth day he could walk the length of the high terrace without stopping. By the sixth he could go down the steps to the harbour and back up again. By the tenth Nessa said he was no longer stupid with cold and could start being stupid in the ordinary way, and handed him a basket.

---

The food was plain and very good, and none of it was fruit.

He had not known how much of Sorel's eating came from the west until it was gone. In Saltjaw, even in a hard Hushlean, there were Wabet apples in the kettle market and banana on festival days, carried down the long roads from somewhere far away. Aumere had none. What it had was the sea and the terraces. Limpets prised off the cliff foot at low water and stewed with a bitter green leaf that grew in the cistern channels. Flat bread made from the meal of a small hard bean and baked directly on the hot stones of the house pits, so that every loaf carried the faint pattern of the stone it had cooked on. Dried fish, thin as paper. The silver-leaved crop from the terraces, which turned out to be a kind of salt-green, crisp and sharp, eaten raw with everything. Water from the cisterns, so clean and cold it hurt his teeth.

They ate together in the evenings, at long tables in the lee of the rock spine, forty or fifty people, children running between the benches. Nobody served anybody. Bowls went down the table and came back empty. It reminded him so much of the Saltjaw kettle under the hull ribs that on the second such evening he had to get up and walk to the terrace edge and stand there for a while looking at the blue, until Nessa came and stood beside him without saying anything, and then they went back.

The houses were built for the wind that never came inside the quiet water but did come, Nessa said, in the Hushlean storms that broke the boom. Thick stone walls, thicker at the bottom than the top. Turf roofs over a frame of driftwood and cut stone, grown so deep with grass that from the high terrace the village looked like a hillside. Doors that were short tunnels through the wall, so that the wind could not blow straight in. And in the middle of every floor a pit of loaf-sized stones over a slow peat fire, which they kept going day and night and which heated the stones until the whole house was warm from the inside out. Children were taught to lay the stones before they were taught to read.

The days had a shape. Dawn: the count. Then work, which meant terraces, cisterns, boats, the boom. Midday: everyone stopped, ate, and slept for an hour in the warm, even the children, even Tavi. Afternoon: work again. Evening: the long tables, and singing, which was mostly arguing set to a tune.

And the boom. The boom was always being mended. There was a rota for it, written in strokes on a slate by the harbour, and every few days a narrow boat went out with three people and coils of rope and tools, and came back hours later wet and tired and arguing about joints.

---

The count was the first thing every morning, and it was very small.

At first light, anyone who was awake went to the edge of the seaward terrace. Not everyone; there was no bell and no order. Old people, mostly, and children, and the boom crew for the day, and whoever happened to be up. Each person took a small pebble from a basket at the top of the steps. They stood along the low wall at the terrace edge and looked out at the water and counted the pulls of the current under their breath, all together, a soft murmur of numbers like rain starting.

*Six. Six. Five.* The line of the boom lifting and settling below them in the blue.

When the long rest came, everyone stopped counting at once, and each person set their pebble on the top of the wall in front of them, in a row with yesterday's pebbles and last week's and last season's. Then they went to work.

That was all. Nobody explained it to him. He watched it three mornings running before he took a pebble himself and stood at the end of the wall and counted with them, and set his stone down when they set theirs down. A small girl beside him looked at where he had put it and moved it a finger's width to the left, into the right place in the row, and said, "There," and went to her breakfast.

He walked the wall afterward. The rows of pebbles ran its whole length, hundreds of them, thousands, grouped by little gaps, some of the old ones worn smooth and sunk into the stone. It was a record, he understood. The count of every morning for as long as anyone had stood there. Children learned their numbers from it. The boom crews read it before they went out, to know whether the pulls were running long or short.

"It's a ledger," he said to Nessa.

"It's the count," she said. "It is also a ledger. Most good things are two things."

---

On the nineteenth day the north cistern gallery began to weep.

The Aumeri had cut their great cisterns into the hillside behind the village, chambers in the rock that filled with rain from channels on the terraces, and the largest of them was fed by a gallery: a tunnel, chest-high, running back into the spine of the island to tap a seep. Tavi came for Ansel in the middle of the afternoon with his iron face set very hard and said, "You know rock. Inside rock. Come and look."

The gallery was dark and wet and cold, and it smelled exactly like Second Drift. Ansel went in on his knees behind Tavi's lamp and felt his whole body settle, the way it had not settled once since the strike. Here, at least, he knew what he was doing.

The water was coming through a crack in the roof a little way in. Not much. A thin sheet running down the wall and pooling. The Aumeri had already set a timber across under it. It was the wrong place.

He took a stone from the floor and tapped the roof in a line, the way he tapped a face at Saltjaw, head tilted, his good eye toward the rock. Sound. Sound. Sound. Then, a hand's width past the crack, toward the back of the gallery, the tired answer. And again on the left side, low.

"It's not the crack," he said. "The crack is just where the water's getting out. There's a plate up there"—he showed Tavi with the stone—"that's let go behind. It's sitting on that seam and it wants to come down toward the back. Your timber's in front of it. When it moves, it'll go behind your timber and fill the gallery, and your cistern loses its feed. Maybe for a season."

Tavi stared at the roof. "How do you hear that?"

"I don't know," said Ansel, truthfully. "I've listened to two thousand of these. Write down every one you tap and what it does after. Your ears will learn faster from a ledger than from me." It was what he had told the Kharad girl in Third Drift. He heard himself say it and had to stop and breathe for a moment.

They propped it the Saltjaw way: two posts and a cap set under the bad plate, not under the crack, and a short relief channel cut in the floor to lead the seep-water away so it did not soften the footings. It took them three days. Tavi worked like two men and asked questions like ten. On the third evening, when the cap was set, Ansel tapped it twice, because that was the rule, and the stone answered sound.

They came out of the gallery into the blue evening light, soaked and filthy. Tavi stood on the terrace with his hands on his hips looking at the cistern, and then he looked at Ansel in a new way.

"We know open stone," Tavi said. "We cut terraces. We've never been good inside it. Nobody here has ever worked a mine."

"Nobody in Saltjaw has ever built a door in the sea."

Tavi laughed, which Ansel had not heard him do before. It was a surprisingly light sound for someone made of iron.

That night at the long tables someone passed Ansel the first bowl instead of the last. He noticed. He ate. He sat afterward at the terrace edge with the pebble wall at his back and the blue in front of him, and thought of Maren on the step with the half-built room behind her, and counted until he could stop.

## Notes

Chapter 10 of **The Island Saga**. Daily life on [Aumere](../regions/aumere.md): sea and terrace food with no fruit (the absence of the Wabet fruit roads is felt), thick-walled turf houses with warm-stone pits, a midday rest, a rota for mending the boom. The small ritual is the [Aumeri Tide-Count](../cultures/aumeri-tide-count.md): counting the current at dawn and setting a pebble on the count-wall at the long rest. It is a ledger as well as a rite; it has no power of its own in this chapter.

Ansel's mining craft ([Saltjaw Mine Safety Practices](../cultures/saltjaw-mine-safety-practices.md): prop under the bad plate, not the visible crack; tap the cap twice) saves the north cistern feed. This is where he becomes useful and liked, which makes the next chapter's conflict harder.
