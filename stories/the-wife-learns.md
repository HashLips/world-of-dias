# The Wife Learns

## Premise

The search for The Patient Lead finds Tamsy and Brann on a strange roped timber and nothing else. After three days Bastien Crale declares Ansel dead. Maren will not let his tag be moved. Six weeks later a rumor comes down from the Westfold road, then a letter of two lines from a Letter House in Wabet: the sea has brought her something, and it has her husband's name on it beside a shore that cannot exist.

## Story

On the night of the strike, Maren rang the bell until her hands bled.

She had stood at the bell-stand from the evening call-up into the dark, watching the path down to the jetty, waiting for a knock and a rising whistle that did not come. When full dark came and The Patient Lead had not, she did not ring the call-up, because there was nobody underground to call. She rang the sea-call, which Saltjaw had not rung in four years: one long stroke, a wait, one long stroke, over and over, which meant *boat overdue* and brought every skiff on the shore down to the water with lamps.

They searched the shelf all night and found nothing. In the morning, a little before the midday kettle, a Saltjaw fishing skiff came back round the point with its oars going raggedly and two people in the bottom of it under blankets.

Tamsy Kell and Brann Oake had been found clinging to a timber in the open water just inside the shelf edge, far to the northeast of where the boat had gone down. They had been on it all night. Tamsy had kept Brann's head out of the water for the last hours of it, because he had stopped trying. Neither of them could stand when they were lifted out. Brann did not speak for a day.

Tamsy spoke at once. She spoke at the jetty, through chattering teeth, with Maren holding both her hands, and she said the same thing again at the kettle that evening to Bastien and the crew leads and anyone else who would listen, and she said it so many times over the following weeks that people began to look away when she started.

"It wasn't rock," she said. "We were off the shelf. There's no rock off the shelf. It was wood. Something big under the water, wood and stone together. It came up under the keel and broke her at the seam. And the thing we were on, it wasn't from our boat. It was a log as long as the boat, and it was roped. With proper rope, thick, tarred. Knots I've never seen. And there were ropes going *down* from it, cut off short, like it had been tied to something on the bottom."

"Driftwood," Bastien said, at the kettle, kindly. "Raft timber. Something off a wreck."

"I know what Driftfall knots look like, Bastien. I've tied them since I was four. These weren't Driftfall." She turned to Brann, who sat with a blanket round him staring at the fire. "Tell him."

Brann looked up. For a long moment he said nothing. Then he said, very quietly, "Dorrit pushed me to it. She was going down and she pushed me to it." And he put his face in his hands and that was all anyone got from him.

"And Ansel?" Maren said.

Tamsy looked at her. "He floated. Like he does. He was swimming to us. The current kept taking him. It came in groups, Maren, the counting one, and every group took him further. It didn't take us. We were low on the log and it went under us. It took him because he was high." Her face crumpled. "He was alive. When I lost him he was alive and swimming and calling. Southwest. Into the fog."

---

The search went on for three days. Saltjaw skiffs and two hired from Driftfall Piers quartered the shelf and the water beyond it as far as anyone dared go. They found the stub of The Patient Lead's mast. They found Dorrit Vash's body on the second day, on the shelf, and brought her home, and Saltjaw buried her on the slope above the outfall with her skipper's squint closed and a lump of verdigris iron in her hands because she had never once lost a sack. They found nothing of Ansel. Not a feather.

On the morning of the fourth day, Bastien came to the tag board.

Saltjaw had a practice for this, as it had for everything. It was painted on the plank by the tag board under the bell rule, in the same repainted letters. *Three days on the in side, below ground or on the water: the tag goes on the sea nail.* There was a single iron nail at the end of the board, a little apart from the others, rusty, with nothing on it. Every tag that had ever hung on it in Saltjaw's history had belonged to someone who did not come back.

Bastien put out his hand to Ansel's tag. The little drawn bird on it was dull with salt.

Maren put her hand over his.

"No," she said.

"Maren." Bastien did not take his hand away. His voice was tired and gentle and absolutely certain. "It's been three days. He went into the open sea off the shelf edge in fog. He was cold before the boat broke. There's nothing out there but water till Wabet. I am so sorry. But it's the practice, and the practice is there so that we don't—"

"So that we don't what? Hope?"

"So that you can grieve. So that the crews can. So that the backers know the shelf survey's done and what it cost, and I can tell Dorrit's people and the hands what's owed." He looked at her. "So that it's finished."

"It's tidy," said Maren. "That's what you mean. It's tidy for the mine if he's dead. A drowned surveyor and a wrecked boat on a bad shelf. You can put it in the book and close it. You don't have to listen to Tamsy talking about roped logs and knots nobody's seen."

Bastien's face went very still. "That's unfair."

"Maybe." She did not move her hand. "Leave his tag where it is, Bastien. On the in side. I'll keep the bell for him. If I'm wrong, I'll move it myself, and you can watch me do it."

He looked at her for a long time. Then he took his hand away, and the tag stayed on the *in* side, and stayed there for the rest of Wetwake, and the crews learned to hang their own tags around it without touching it.

---

The rumor came down the road in the sixth week, and Brann brought it.

He had begun going to the Salt Ledger in Driftfall on rest days, walking the coast path north and inland, because it was the one place on the shore where nobody asked him anything. He sat at the back with one cup for an hour and watched the door. It was there, on a grey afternoon in the last days of Wetwake, that a fruit-runner back from the west, a Hearthvale man who had been driving empty carts home through Westfold Toll, sat down two benches away and told the room a story he had heard at the crossing.

A drowned Sorel surveyor, he said. A letter in a tube, washed up somewhere on the Wabet coast, in the south, in the desert country. A letter to his wife, with a picture of an island on the back where no island was. A blue island. A shore that wasn't on any chart.

The Salt Ledger listened the way it always did, without much ceremony. Someone said it sounded like the Lumens boy's letter, the one he wrote to himself as a deckhand off these very piers and then went off and vanished after. Someone else said that was a different thing entirely and the Lumens boy had at least had the manners to vanish on land. Someone asked the surveyor's name.

The fruit-runner did not know it. He knew the wife's. He had heard it said at Westfold, because the story was that the letter was addressed to her. Maren something. At a mine.

Brann put his cup down and walked out of the Salt Ledger and back down the coast path to Saltjaw through the dusk without stopping, and it was full dark when he came up the slope and knocked on the door of a hull-rib house with half a room built beside it.

He knocked twice. He did not know why. He had heard Tamsy talk about it.

Maren opened the door so fast that it banged against the wall, and then she saw who it was, and her face did something that Brann would remember for the rest of his life.

"I'm sorry," he said. "I'm sorry. I didn't think. It's me. Listen. Maren, listen."

---

The proper word came nine days after that, by the long way: a slip of paper in a narrow hand, passed from a glasswort carter to a Keep runner to a toll clerk at Westfold to a Hearthvale wagon to a Driftfall pier-boy who had been told to find the bell-keeper at Saltjaw Mine and had found her.

*The sea has brought a letter for Maren Kestry, bell-keeper of Saltjaw Mine. It is kept at the Letter House of Sallow Strand for her. O. Brask, keeper.*

Maren read it standing at the bell-stand, with the rope in one hand. She read it four times. Then she went to the tag board and touched the little drawn bird on Ansel's tag with one finger, and went to find Bastien.

He was at the kettle. She put the slip of paper down in front of him, beside his bowl.

"He wrote to me," she said. "From the sea. With a picture of somewhere that isn't on any chart." She did not raise her voice. "Tell me again that it's finished."

Bastien read the slip. He read it again. He looked up at her, and for the first time since the strike he did not look certain of anything at all.

## Notes

Chapter 16 of **The Island Saga**. Tamsy's testimony describes the [Aumere Turning Boom](../artifacts/aumere-turning-boom.md) timber accurately (roped, tarred, non-Driftfall knots, ropes cut short going down); Saltjaw has no way to read it. The *sea nail* practice is in [Saltjaw Mine Safety Practices](../cultures/saltjaw-mine-safety-practices.md). Bastien's declaration is honest by the practice and convenient for the mine; Maren names both.

The news arrives by two channels, both ordinary: rumor via a Hearthvale fruit-runner returning through [Westfold Toll](../regions/westfold-toll.md) to [The Salt Ledger](../regions/the-salt-ledger.md), and the Letter House's word by the long relay (carter, Keep runner, Westfold clerk, Hearthvale wagon, Driftfall pier-boy). The Lumen hook is talk only: Salt Ledger regulars compare the letter to [Letter to Myself at the Crossing](../artifacts/letter-to-myself-at-the-crossing.md); nothing connects the two.
