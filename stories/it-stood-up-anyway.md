# It Stood Up Anyway

## Premise

On the basin road the stolen color stands up. The Lean Echo offers a wonderful elsewhere and will not say how to reach it. The three follow the real road south.

## Story

They were a mile past the last shed that knew Veloria's name when Joss stopped because the rag had gone light in his arms, not brighter — emptier, as if the color had stepped out and left the cloth holding a habit.

The stone sat in his palms gray for the first time since the shop. The color was in the air in front of them.

It stood at a person's height only if you were being polite. Its edges drank the morning. Inside the drinking, amber and chalk-gray and a thread of blue moved like hearttide weather trapped in a figure that had not decided on a face. It did not cast a shadow the road could use. When it turned its attention, Rill felt a bell under her teeth, pressure without sound.

Harl made a noise and stepped back into a cart-rut. A fruit wagon coming the other way — basin traffic, not their business — rolled through the space the figure occupied and did not swerve. The driver touched his hat to the three living children and did not touch his hat to the color. Rill understood, cold, that the road was not obliged to see what they had taken.

"You followed," the figure said. The words were almost words. They arrived as place-feeling: a hearth, a wide light, a south that loved you. Under the kindness, a thinner cold, the kind of cold that happens in a corner when you look at it too long. Rill had heard porters scare each other with that cold and call it the null band, the abyss frequency, F500 if they wanted to sound like they had seen a ledger. She had never heard anyone claim a road to it. She did not hear one now.

"Where?" Joss asked. His voice was awed and already loyal. "Where are you going?"

"Where it is wonderful," the echo said. "Where what you felt can be the whole of the air. I can find it. Walk."

"How?" Harl said. He had found his counter-voice, thin but present. "If it's a band, there isn't a ferry. Everybody jokes about the ticket and then gets off at the same dock. So how?"

The figure's colors warmed, Joss's amber leading, as if reward lived in not asking. "Knowing is not a thing I put in your mouth. The road is already going. Stay with me. You will see the light change."

"Sorel," Rill said, testing. "Hearthvale. The second beginning. Is that the light?"

A pause. The cold corner of it flickered, interested, and then the warmth returned, thicker. "You will like what you call it when you arrive. Do not make me a map. Maps are how people sell brittle things."

It used Pim's idea without Pim's refusal, and Joss heard only the promise. "It knows," he told them. "It stood up because we got it out. If we go back now we put it in a case and it dies into gray. I'm not doing that to it."

"It isn't a pet," Harl said. But the echo had already begun to walk, half a step ahead, south, on the actual road, ruts and dung and a milestone with Nauw's mark still readable. Real. That was the cruelty. A lie that floated off the path would have been easier to leave. This one used the path the basin already believed in.

Rill looked at the gray stone in Joss's hand, dull as a river rock, and at the being wearing their colors. She thought of the ribbon on the hook, which had not followed them, and of the sentence about ease. The echo had not offered a method. It had offered company and a direction. She hated that company was enough to move her feet.

"First lie, we turn," she said again.

The Lean Echo, though she did not have that name for it yet, inclined its head as if a condition were a kind of music it could walk through. They followed it into the basin's long south, and the carts kept not seeing it, and the sky over Veloria grew smaller behind them without ever agreeing to explain what they were walking toward.
