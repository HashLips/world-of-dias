# The Sea Crew

## Premise

Four survey days on The Patient Lead with Dorrit Vash, Tamsy Kell, and Brann Oake. The shelf is mostly sound. On the fourth day, past the shelf edge to the southwest, Tamsy notices that the floats do not drift. They come back, in counts.

## Story

The Patient Lead was not much to look at, which Dorrit said was the point of her.

She was a broad, shallow cutter, tarred black, with a single mast that could be unstepped and a sounding well cut through her floor amidships so the lead could go down without anyone leaning over the side. Dorrit had built her from the keel of a lighter that had come apart in a Hushlean storm and the deck of a Driftfall fishing boat that had not, and the two halves had never entirely agreed. She leaked a little at the seam. She rowed heavy. She did not tip.

"I named her for what you need in this work," Dorrit said, the first morning, stepping down into her with a weight that made the whole hull settle a finger's width. "Not the lead. The patience."

The crew was four. Dorrit at the tiller. Ansel in the well with the lead line, the sand-glass, and the ledger in its oilcloth wrap. And on the oars, two hands Dorrit had picked herself, because she did not row with strangers.

Brann Oake was a human man in his forties with a broad back and two fingers missing from his left hand, which he had lost to a hawser in Claimscar Yard in some crew fight he did not talk about. He had left Claimscar for Saltjaw because, he said, a mine at least tells you which way the danger is. He rowed like a machine, spoke rarely, and had a habit of humming the same four notes of a Driftfall work song over and over until someone begged him to learn a fifth.

Tamsy Kell was nineteen, from a fishing family down the shore, small and sun-browned and very fast. She had been rowing since she could hold an oar and counting since before that. She counted strokes. She counted waves. She counted the gulls on the drying post every morning and announced the number to anyone nearby. Her mother said she had been born adding things up and had never found a reason to stop.

"Why does it matter how many gulls?" Ansel asked her on the first day.

"Because one day it'll be a different number," said Tamsy, "and then I'll know something happened."

He wrote that in the back of the ledger. He liked her at once.

---

The first survey day was the inner shelf, close under the point, in a flat grey calm. They ran the first rib of Ansel's fan out from the jetty toward the shelf edge, stopping every forty strokes for a sounding. Ansel let the lead down through the well hand over hand, felt it touch, read the marks on the line, hauled it up, and turned the tallow to the light. Sand. Sand. Green dust. Green dust. Shell. Green dust. He sang the numbers out and Dorrit repeated them back and he wrote them down, and between soundings he dropped a blue-banded float and Tamsy timed it on the glass while Brann held them steady.

The floats went west. They always went west. The Shelf Set took them along the coast like a slow hand, and by the time the glass had run twice they were specks.

That night he walked up the slope with his feathers stiff with salt and knocked on his own door, twice, and whistled, rising, and heard Maren laugh on the other side before she opened it.

The second survey day came four days later, after a blow. They ran the second and third ribs of the fan. The plate held under the lead, sounding after sounding, a long sound table of green-stained rock with sand drifted over it in hollows. At one sounding the tallow came up black and smelling of eggs, and Ansel marked it with a small circle and wrote *vent-warm seep? sulfur on tallow. guess, not measure* beside it. Dorrit looked at the circle and said nothing, but she steered wide of the place afterward.

Brann learned a fifth note on the third survey day, mostly to stop Tamsy threatening him. It did not help. The work song now had five notes and he hummed those over and over instead.

"It's a better song," he said, when challenged.

"It's the same song with an extra note."

"That's what makes it better."

It rained. They ate cold fish and kiln-bread under the thwarts. Dorrit told them about the storm that had broken the lighter whose keel they were sitting on, and how she had come ashore on its wreckage with a crate of ore in her arms because she had refused to lose a sack, and how Bastien's father had paid her for the sack and not for the lighter. Tamsy told them about a shark that had followed her uncle's boat for a whole morning off the eastern beaches, close enough to touch, and done nothing at all, which had been worse than if it had attacked. Ansel told them, because they asked, that he did not know where he came from. He had been found on Driftfall Salvage Shore at about four years old, wrapped in a split crate after a wrong-sky night, by a salvage couple named Kestry who had kept him because nobody else came for him. The Salt Ledger crowd said he was from F120, because he wrote everything twice. He thought he wrote everything twice because he had been trained by a careful man.

"Does it bother you?" Tamsy asked. "Not knowing?"

"It used to." He hauled the lead and looked at the tallow. Green. "Now I know where I live. That's most of it."

---

The fourth survey day took them to the end of the fan, the long southwest rib that ran out past the shelf edge, where the green table broke off and the bottom dropped away beyond the reach of the lead.

It was the day Dorrit liked least. The point was low behind them. The headframe was a thumbnail. Only Sprout Vent's plume, standing up off the land, gave Ansel a clear mark, and he took his sightings on it carefully, three times each, because if the vent stopped smoking he would be guessing.

He let the last float go at the shelf edge and Tamsy turned the glass.

The float went west, as floats did. Then it stopped. Then, while they watched, it came back toward them, a little, and stopped again, and went west again.

"That's wrong," said Tamsy.

Ansel dropped a second float. It did the same. Out, and back, and out. Not drifting. Being moved and let go.

"Brann," Tamsy said, "row nothing. Hold her. Just hold her."

Brann held the boat. Tamsy knelt in the bow with her chin on the gunwale and watched the water and counted under her breath. Ansel had seen her count gulls. This was different. Her lips moved and her fingers tapped her knee, and after a while she said, "Six. Then six. Then five. Then nothing for a long time. Then six again."

"Six what?"

"Pulls. It pulls, and lets go, and pulls. Like breathing, but it can't count properly. Six, six, five, and then a long rest." She looked up at him, and her face was not frightened. It was delighted. "It's counting, Ansel. The water's counting."

Dorrit, at the tiller, said: "It's a current off the edge. Edges do strange things."

"Edges do strange things the same way every time," Tamsy said. "This isn't the same. Watch. Six, six—" she waited "—six. It was five last time. It's six now."

Ansel watched. He could not see it as she did. He had to drop floats and time them on the glass, one after another, and set the times out in columns. It took him the rest of the light. When he had done it he looked at the columns and his chest went tight in a way that was not pleasure.

Six, six, five, long rest. Six, six, six, long rest. Six, five, five, long rest. Each group was almost the group before. None was the same.

He wrote it in the ledger exactly as he had measured it, timed float by timed float. Beside it he wrote: *Irregular set SW of shelf edge. Pulses in groups. Group pattern drifts. Counted first by T. Kell by eye; confirmed by float and glass. Cause unknown. Do not guess cause.*

Then, after a moment, under it, smaller: *Tamsy calls it the counting current.*

"We go home," Dorrit said. "Now. Before the light's gone and the vent's dark."

They went home. Brann rowed and hummed his five notes and nobody stopped him. Tamsy sat in the stern facing backward with her hand trailing in the water, counting until the point hid the shelf edge. Ansel knocked on his own door that night, two and a rise, and told Maren about the water that counted, and she said it sounded like Tamsy had found the only thing in Saltjaw more stubborn than she was.

He laughed. He meant to go back to the shelf edge and measure it properly. He thought he had time.
