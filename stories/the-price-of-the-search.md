# The Price of the Search

## Premise

Maren goes west again, the long overland way, carrying the ledger and the fair record and the knowledge that a girl she never met is dead because people came looking. At Sallow Strand the letter-keepers split. Some want to read the margins, find the island again and tell the coast where it is. Some want to stop. Some walk away east to sell a new story. Maren tells them what the search has already cost and lays down a rule of her own: whatever they learn, no position leaves Sallow Strand. They are looking for him, not for it.

## Story

She left Saltjaw in the second week of Hushlean, and this time she did not pretend to herself that she was coming back.

There was very little to pack. The plank fund was gone; the clay pot sat empty on the shelf. The frame of the second room stood on the slope beside the hull-rib house, posts only, the tarp over its floor gone grey with weather. She left the tarp where it was. Brann said he would see to the posts. They both knew he meant that he would leave them standing exactly as they were, so that there would be something on the slope that was still waiting.

She gave up the bell. The second bell-keeper took it, with her mended arm and an expression that said she knew exactly what she was being handed. Maren showed her the sea-call one more time, long stroke, wait, long stroke, though the woman had known it for years. Then she said, "The bell stays at the bell," which was the oldest rule at Saltjaw, and walked away from it.

She went with the survey ledger in oilcloth against one side of her ribs and the fair record against the other.

---

The road west took nineteen days, and she knew every stage of it now.

The salvage wagon from Driftfall Piers, with a driver who had heard the fleet's story five different ways at the Salt Ledger and wanted to know which was true. ("All of them," Maren said. "And none. Someone loosed a bolt and two people died. That's the true part.") The long cart road across Sorel's interior, past yard camps and Freebands posts where someone had already painted a grey pennant and a ring on a board and written under it *THE HOUSES' WARD*, which was not a compliment. The Hearthvale crossroads. Westfold Toll, where the clerk looked at her for a long moment and then, without being asked, did not charge her. "You've paid enough," he said, and stamped her slip.

In the dune country a Keep runner met her on the path and walked beside her for half a day without speaking. Then, at a place where the marker stones said one thing to Keepers and another to strangers, he said softly, "The Keep has sent word to every cove from Glasswake to the Coral side. No boat goes out from Lumira to look for that water. Not from our sand." He glanced at her. "We thought you would want to know that before you came down."

"Yes," said Maren. "Thank you. I did."

---

Sallow Strand had heard. Everything on that coast heard eventually; the copies had taught it that. The cookhouse knew there had been a fleet and an island and a vanishing. It did not know that there had been a girl at her oars with a bolt in her chest. Maren told them.

She told them on her first night back, at the long table in the Letter House, with Odile sitting side-on at the end of the bench and every letter-keeper in the room and some who had stopped coming and came back to hear. She told it plainly, the way she had told the tag board's count at the end of a shift: who was in, who was out, who did not come up.

She told them about the shore standing up out of the blue, and the fair record held against it, and the drawing being exactly right. She told them about Nessa Orvay in her undyed coat refusing the fleet and offering words, and offering Ansel. She told them about the man on the harbour stones with his hood in his hand. And she told them about the ward-bolt, and the grey pennant whipping across the young rower's face, and the second bolt, and the girl sitting down on her thwart as if she were tired. And the Ward oarsman between the timbers. *Tok.*

"She was perhaps Tamsy's age," Maren said. "I don't know her name. Nobody does. We'll never know it. She's dead because people came looking for an island that didn't want to be found, and some of the people who came looking were looking because of a letter that's on the shelf above your heads." She looked round the room. "Because of copies of it. Because of us talking about it. Because of me, going to Veloria's own envoy and saying *he's a person, not a hazard*, and getting myself put on a boat that brought the bolts closer."

Nobody spoke.

---

The split came anyway. Maren had known it would. It came over the fish stew, three nights later, in voices the room had to keep asking to come down.

The young man from the Basin was still there. He had grown quieter since Brightpass, but he had not changed his mind. "It's real," he said. "We know that now. You saw it. A whole Ward contingent saw it. There's a shore off the Sorel shelf that comes and goes, with a ring of timbers around it that breaks boats, and people on it. Every boat on that coast has a right to know where and when. If those margins say when—" he looked at the shelf "—then keeping them here is keeping a reef secret. The next crew that drowns on that boom is on us."

"The next crew that goes out to that boom because we told them when," said the fisher from Sorel's south shore, "might bring bows. Or might be dare-crews with a painted copy that says *the way through*. You heard her. That's what came last time."

"So we warn them off. We tell them when *not* to go."

"Telling someone when not to go is telling them when to go," said Odile from the end of the bench. She did not raise her voice. "You learn that young, keeping letters. Every warning is a map to someone."

There was a third group too, and it was the one that left. Four of the seekers who had come in Brightpass looking for the door had stayed through the summer arguing, and now they had a new story and they believed it. The island had gone through. The people had gone through and taken the surveyor with them. Next long slack, it would open again, and anyone who counted right would see the shore. They called it the shore nobody charts, and they had already started painting it: the cliffs, the terraces, the blue, and now the ring of timbers too, and a crested figure on the harbour stones holding out his hand.

"That's not what I saw," Maren said, when one of them showed her.

"It's what it means," said the seeker.

They left for the east the next morning with their paintings rolled under their arms. Maren watched them go up the dune path, walking loudly, and knew she would meet their version on every road in Dias for the rest of her life.

---

The ones who stayed were nine. Odile, Wick, the fisher, the two Songripe coasters, a grieving woman whose husband had been lost off the Outer Rim, and two others. And the young man from the Basin, who had argued all night and in the morning had not left.

Maren sat with them at the long table on the fourth night, with the fair record under its glass and the survey ledger closed beside it, and said what she had been carrying since the fleet.

"I'm going to keep looking," she said. "I'm not going to stop. He wrote *don't let anyone tell you I'm finished because it's tidy*, and I won't. But I'm going to tell you what the looking is for, and if you can't keep to it, you can go after the others with my blessing.

"We're looking for him. Not for it. Not for the island. Not for where it is or where it went or how to get there. Them, the people on it, Nessa and the iron-skinned man and the girl's family, they asked one thing of the fleet: no landing, no measuring, no mark. That's their answer. It's the same answer the Keep gives when it walks you softly past its silences. I'm going to give them the courtesy the Keep gets.

"Whatever we learn from those margins, no position leaves this house. No bearing. No *when*. Not to the Houses, not to the Ward, not to the coast, not to the road. If I learn how to find a shore, I'll burn that page myself. If I learn how to send a word to him, I'll send the word and nothing else."

The young man from the Basin looked at her for a long time.

"And the reef?" he said at last. "The boom. The boats."

"Vexra Coil's left it out there as its own ward," said Maren. "The water round it is broken white from the point. Nobody needs a map to know not to row into white water." She held his eyes. "That's the price. Some boat may still go out and drown there, and we might have stopped it, and we won't have. I know. I've rung the sea-call. But I've also seen what comes when people go looking with the Houses' grade in their hands, and I'll pay this price before I pay that one again."

He did not answer. But he did not leave, either, and in the morning he was the first one at the Letter House with the broom.

---

That night Odile came and found her on the dune above the hamlet, where Maren was sitting with her knees drawn up, facing south and east, toward Sorel and the shelf and the white water and the empty sea beyond it.

"You said *send a word*," said Odile.

"Yes."

"Not *go*."

"No." Maren picked up two stones from the dune and turned them in her hand. "Nessa said he was going home by a way of their own, and walking to me from a far shore. His canister went out on the same kind of water and came ashore here, east of Glasswake. Everything we ever lost off the point went west. If they were going to set him down anywhere, it was on sand like this." She knocked the stones together once, softly, and then again. "I'm not going to go looking for an island that doesn't want me. But I can stand where things come ashore."

## Notes

Chapter 28 of **The Island Saga**. The price of the search is told without euphemism: the unnamed Aumeri girl, the Ward oarsman, the plank fund, the bell given up, the unfinished room left standing. The Keep closes the Lumira coast to boats going out to look ([Silent Dune Keep](../cultures/silent-dune-keep.md), [Lumira Silence Law](../cultures/lumira-silence-law.md)).

The [Letter-Keepers of Sallow Strand](../cultures/letter-keepers-of-sallow-strand.md) split three ways: those who would publish a position as the coast's right to know; those who stay under Maren's rule; and four seekers who leave east carrying the myth [The Shore Nobody Charts](../myths/the-shore-nobody-charts.md). Maren's rule: **looking for him, not for it.** No position or "when" leaves the house; she will send a word and nothing else. Odile's line, "every warning is a map to someone," becomes letter-keeper doctrine.
