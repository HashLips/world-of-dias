# Fruit In, and Not Fruit Out

## Premise

At Mercer Shed, Kett Rind watches the freighter unload. Fruit had gone into the hull in Wabet. What comes out under cloth does not look like fruit, and something is carried away anyway.

## Story

Kett Rind knew the shed by its bruises.

Mercer Shed at lamp-time was a sweet, vinegary room: outer-ring doors open on stone and on the last ox of somebody else's day, inner wall bright with third-ring lamps and the loader arm waiting on its rail like a polite insect. She had been sorting Softfruit since the gilding faded. Her scarf was losing. Her eyes were not. A Softfruit either slumped honestly or it had been packed by a liar. She put the liars in a different crate and did not give them speeches.

The Late Bruise landed on the hired pad beyond the doors, not on a street, because even Dain Mercer could not ask Veloria to be a field. Opal Mire came in first with the public count. Crates. Labels. The smell of Wabet grass still in the rope. The loader arm took what it was allowed to take, metal hand careful, more careful than some men. Ordinary fruit became distribution. That part was so normal Kett could have done it in her sleep, and some nights did, dreaming in tallies.

Then the other weight.

Two of Mercer's inside hands carried a crate that had been under the rest. Cloth, undyed, rope crossed tight. It did not vent the way Softfruit vented. It did not stain the cloth the way market banana stained anything that loved it. When a corner bumped the doorframe there was a sound Kett had no stall-word for: a small ring, as if someone had tapped a cup under a napkin. Not juice. Not a tool-rattle. A note.

"That's not fruit," she said, to Opal, because Opal was the only person in the doorway who had been in the hull.

Opal's hand stayed on a coil of rope. "It's weight," she said. "I load weight."

"Fruit went in," Kett said. She had not been in Wabet. She had heard the slate. She had seen this plane leave empty of secrets often enough to know when a return was wrong. "I was told fruit. I am looking at a thing you are not letting the arm touch."

The hands took the crate through the side door into the office end, where Dain's desk lived and the resonant tallies slept their dumb sleep. No voice came out of the machines. A man followed, and Kett saw Dain himself for a moment: clean shoes, a smile he put on when he passed labor, the smile of a host who had already decided the room was his. He did not look at the sorters. The door shut. The ring-sound did not repeat.

Kett stood with a Softfruit in her palm going soft the way Softfruit does when you forget it is alive. She set it down gently, angry at herself for the bruise she had just made. Opal went back to the hull to count the honest crates a second time. She would not add a sentence. Her silence had a shape, and the shape was confirmation.

On the dock a third-ring lamp hummed. Out on the stone an ox driver swore at a wheel in medieval words that did not care what year the lamp thought it was. Both Velorias were in the same hour, which was how the city always worked if you were not romantic about it. Kett wiped her hands. She had a scarf, a true eye, and a story that was already too big for a shed.

She did not know the bar would be the place. She knew she was not going to keep a ringing crate behind her teeth. Fruit in. Not fruit out. Something carried. If she said it badly, the city would correct her by repeating it worse. That was what cities were for. She blew out her own small lamp at the sorting bench and left the bright ones for men who could afford to be seen.
