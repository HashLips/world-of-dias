# A Bird That Only Clicks

## Premise

Farstock opens again. The three are given a click-bird, not the stone. The shop becomes talk of the town. Adults grow curious and do not receive a map. Whether the echo truly knew a way is left unanswered.

## Story

Pim opened the door at the hour he always had, which was the hour the loop was already loud. The bell gave one small ring for the apprentices who had come to see a death or a treasure and were half-believing, and then it settled, unimpressed by the size of the crowd. The crowd was not a crowd yet. It was eight people and a story growing between them, which in the outer ring is the start of talk of the town.

The stone was not on the counter. The case was on the shelf, cloth at the lip, ribbon on the hook again. Gray, if anyone had been allowed to look, which they were not. Pim sold a twist of gilding candy to a child who had been pushed forward as a scout, and the candy was candy, and the child looked betrayed in a way that would heal by the next sweet thing. Someone asked if the shop had moved a relic south. Someone asked if Sorel had bought him. Someone asked, lowering their voice as if the null band might be eavesdropping from a pear stall, whether it was true a color had offered a way out of the realm and been wrestled back by a keeper who knew the words.

"I know the words for prices," Pim said. "I don't know a way out of the realm. If I did, I would not put it in your mouth, and I would not put it in theirs." He meant the three, who stood inside the counter's end like staff and felt like thieves still, which was accurate and incomplete.

He took a basket from under the pencils. Palm-sized wooden birds, rough-carved, painted with a thumb of color that did not shift when Rill touched one. He set three on the wood.

"These are not wages and they are not a moral," he said. "They are what I can give you without lying. Set it down."

Rill set hers on the counter. It clicked once, a wooden tongue, a landing, and then it was a toy. The shop's talk dipped in disappointment so pure she almost laughed. Joss set his down. Click. He waited for a second click, for a lean, for amber. The bird declined. His shoulders did a strange thing: they loosened without the stone's help, a small real loosen, the kind you get when an object refuses to be your life. Harl's clicked, and Harl said, "That's all," in the tone he used for correct change, and put the bird in his pocket as if pockets were for birds now.

"Take them home," Pim said. "If you want to tell the loop you walked to the Long Gate and stole a thing that worked and were glad to give it back, tell them that. If they ask how the color would have carried you into the abyss, tell them you don't know. I don't know. The watcher on the gate doesn't know. Not knowing is not a door left ajar for you to sell. It is the truth we have."

The apprentices lingered. Curiosity is a civic weather, and it had arrived. They would come back. They would bring aunts. They would try the string, the spoon, the marble that only knew rain. Some of them would be better for a shop that did small true things. Some of them would be angry that the famous part was not for sale. Pim seemed prepared to be a disappointment, which was its own outer-ring courage.

Rill stayed after the others were nudged out, Joss and Harl waiting on the step because they did not yet like separating. The case on the shelf was quiet. She touched the click-bird's back. It did not advise her.

"If it knew," she said, "we didn't deserve the knowing. If it didn't know, then it used Sorel and the cold the way a stall uses a shout. I can't tell which is worse."

"Worse isn't the work for today," Pim said. "The work for today is that the ribbon held and you are in a shop and not in a pocket of air. Let the question be a question. Questions that get answered too fast turn into tickets. You have seen a ticket."

She had. She put the bird in her pocket. On the step the bell did not ring, because they were not arriving, they were leaving, and belief was a more ordinary thing than it had been: a door that stuck, a keeper who locked it like a house, a south that was a real country they had seen and not entered as neighbors. The market loop took them back without asking for a map. Above the rings the Sky Drifters kept their own counsel. In the pocket, against a message slip and a bit of chalk dust, the wooden bird was silent, having already done the one small thing it was for.

Rill did not decide about the echo. The city did not decide. Farstock stayed one street off the loop, talked about, visited, refused, and open.
