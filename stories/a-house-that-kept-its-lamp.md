# A House That Kept Its Lamp

## Premise

They come down near the lent strip in the dark. A wooden skiff passes the lane a handspan above the ruts, and Willa will not say what holds it. Sella's lamp stays lit.

## Story

Wabet at night was a country that had not asked for engines.

Lark put The Late Bruise down on the lent grass by memory and by a stubborn moon, and the wheels found the old bruise of the morning's — yesterday's — strip. The noise slapped the trees and came back. Somewhere a bird objected in a voice that had never had to include metal. She killed the engines as fast as she dared. Fuel ticked. The case with the kept fruit was a small cold idea under Dain's unlocatable arm.

Moth Kear should have been asleep. Children are not contracts. The child appeared at the stake with a blanket around the shoulders and the same offended eyes, smaller in the dark.

"You said fruit," Moth whispered, as if the plane might be embarrassed. "It's night."

"Stay off the wheels," Lark said. "Go home."

Moth did not go home. Moth followed at the hedge-distance of a person who had been told no by experts. Along the lane toward the trees, a different shape moved: The Stillwood Skiff, pale wood, a handspan above the ruts, pole tapping. Willa Plank stood in it as she would have stood in a boat, straight, unmagical in the face, utterly unexplained in the hull. No bag of gas. No fan. The weeds bowed only where the pole touched, not where the keel would have been if it had agreed to be a keel.

Moth forgot the plane for a whole breath. "What holds that?"

"I don't know," Willa said. She did not stop. She did not sell the sentence. "It doesn't leave the lane. It isn't a Drifter. Drifters don't take a pole, and they don't come down here to impress you. It isn't a ferry. If someone told you wood can buy another frequency, they wanted your errand or your money. I want neither. Gate's shut at the fork. I'm going around."

She went around. The skiff's lift remained a fact without a lesson. Moth looked at Lark as if the adult world had developed a second rudeness.

"And yours is the machine," the child said.

"Mine is the machine," Lark said. "Hers is a boat that forgot the water and won't discuss it. Go home. Tell your cousin I was here if you have to. Don't follow me to the house."

The house had a lamp. Orrow House sat low in the timber way, one square of light on the lane, hearth-smoke behaved. Inside the square, Sella moved, and Hob's shadow crossed with a bowl. Stew. The smell reached the hedge: onion, apple, something green. A share-pot night. Dain's voice arrived at Lark's shoulder from the wrong side.

"Predictable," he said. "Stay with your machine."

"I'm not staying," she said.

He did not argue with sound. He went, a pressure along the path of flat stones, sometimes a shoe, sometimes the case knocking once against his knee with that small ring. Lark followed because she had told Opal she wanted to be in the room. The grass she had raked yesterday was already confused again. Yarrow's stakes watched like blunt teeth. At the tree-line the mix orchard held its ordinary dark and one point that was not ordinary: high on the younger tree, a last glint, small, green-glass, not part of any count Dain had spoken aloud in the shed. She saw it and stored it and hated the storing.

Sella's door was latched against weather. It was not latched against a man the lamp could not find. Lark heard the latch lift anyway, carefully, from the outside. Hob laughed at something inside, young, unfinished. The lamp did not go out. That was the house's whole bravery, and it would have to be enough, because the night had already decided to enter a kitchen.
