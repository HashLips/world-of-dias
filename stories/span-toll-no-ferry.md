# Span Toll, No Ferry

## Premise

At Span Toll they pay like travelers. A joke ferry ticket is nailed up as a warning. The Lean Echo will not turn the joke into a way, and Harl stops asking it how.

## Story

Span Toll was a working bridge with shade stalls and a chalk mind. The hill above it carried the joke-name everyone used when they meant the road marshal's patience, but the gel-helmed man himself was not on the span that hour. His discipline was. Tallies. A board for labor owed. A clerk with ink on a knuckle who looked at three dusty teenagers and did not look at the color standing just behind Joss's shoulder.

"Destination," the clerk said.

"South," Harl said. "Through. We're not selling and we're not a convoy."

"South through is still a use of the span." The clerk tapped a rate that was fair and unromantic. They paid in the coins Harl had refused to call emergency until now. The stone in the satchel did not lighten the fee. Rill was glad. A toll that ignored wonder was a kind of safety.

On the stall post, where comedy went to be useful, someone had nailed a cheap ticket. Cheerful boat, cheerful lie, and a no-ferry mark struck through the promise so hard the card had scarred. Fine print, read by Harl because Harl read fine print the way other people read faces: destination, nowhere you do not already stand. A second line in clerk-chalk beside it: *Laugh, then walk. Bands are not a dock.*

Joss glanced and dismissed it. The echo's edge drank the chalk dust and gave nothing back. Harl, who had asked *how* once and been stroked for the asking, touched the ticket with two fingers.

"If you know a way," he said, quiet, to the color, "it isn't this. So what is it? A gate under the span? A word? A corner you stare at until it opens? People say staring tunes you. I want the actual thing, or I want you to say you don't have it."

Travelers eddied around them. A mule sneezed. The echo came close enough that Harl's chalk-gray rushed up its middle like a blush of honesty it did not want.

"I may know," it said. The bell-pressure in the teeth, almost kind. "I will not put the knowing where a toll book can copy it. Books make doors that are not doors. Walk to where the light changes. You will not need a ticket when the place recognizes you."

"That's not an answer," Harl said.

"It is the only answer that does not become a thing you can sell," the echo said, and for a heartbeat it sounded like Pim, which made Rill's stomach turn. Pim had used refusal to keep a stone on a cloth. The echo used refusal to keep a method in its mouth. They were not the same no. She held onto that. She did not say it well enough to stop Joss, who was already showing the paid chalk-mark on his wrist and calling the conversation a delay.

They crossed the span on foot. Water under them was ordinary water. No second river of frequency showed between the pilings. Harl looked until his eyes stung, hunting a hidden stair, and found bolts, moss, and a lizard doing lizard business. He laughed once, ugly, relieved.

"There isn't a ferry," he told Rill on the far side. "I needed to see the joke nailed up next to the rate. If it knew a stair, the stair would have been a temptation right there, in the shade, with an audience. It didn't even look at the water."

"It said it may know," Rill answered.

"May is a word adults use when they want a child's feet." He adjusted his pack. Ahead, the march aimed them toward the long southern crossing, and the echo was bright again, done with questions. "I'm still here," Harl said. "That's the part I hate."

Rill nodded. The toll's chalk dried on their wrists. Somewhere behind, a clerk turned the joke ticket so the next fool would see the strike-through first. The road remained a road. Whatever the echo may have known, Span Toll did not learn it, and neither did they.
