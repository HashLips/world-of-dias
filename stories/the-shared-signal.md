# The Shared Signal

## Premise

A burst pump line fills Second Drift with water-noise and dark. Maren, keeping the bell, finds Ansel in it with the private call they have used for nine years: two knocks and a rising whistle. It is not a power. It is a habit two people made so they could find each other where nothing else carries.

## Story

The pump line in Second Drift went in the fourth hour of the afternoon shift, and the first thing anyone at the surface knew about it was the sound.

Saltjaw Mine was never quiet. The hand-pumps on the low levels groaned all day, the ore carts rang on their rails, picks and wedges kept up a broken drumming that carried up the adit like weather. People who had worked there long enough stopped hearing it, the way people on Driftfall Piers stop hearing the sea. What Maren Kestry heard from the bell-stand, standing at the rope with her hands in the red-marked places, was a change in it: a deep wet roar from somewhere below, and then the drumming stopping in pieces, crew by crew, as people put their tools down to listen.

She looked at the tag board. She did not need to. She knew whose tags were on the *in* side the way she knew the weather. Two crews in Second. Bastien's nephew on the carts. And a brass tag with a little drawn bird on it.

She rang the call-up: three strokes, three strokes, three strokes. Everyone in the mine knew it. *Come up. Come up. Come up.* Then she gave the rope to the boy who carried water for the stand and told him to keep ringing it until she told him to stop, and she took the bell-hammer off its nail and went down.

Bell-keepers were not supposed to go down. That was in the safety practices, painted on a plank beside the tag board in letters that had been repainted every Wetwake for twenty years: *The bell stays at the bell.* The reason was good. If the bell-keeper went under, who would count the tags? She went anyway, as far as the timber sets at the head of Second, which she told herself afterward was still more or less the bell.

The drift was black. A lamp had gone, or several. Water was coming up the slope in a sheet over the rails, ankle-deep and cold, and the noise in that narrow place was enormous: the burst line spraying somewhere below, the trapped echo of it coming back off the plates, men and women shouting to each other and every shout breaking apart against the stone into ten shouts. She could hear people. She could not tell where any of them were.

Two of the crew came past her out of the dark, holding on to each other, and she shoved them up toward the light. A third. A fourth. She counted them against the tags in her head. Bastien's nephew came up with a cart-lamp swinging and said the low crew was out by the ore chute and the surveyor had gone down the side cut to find where the line had split, because he was the only one light enough to stand on the old staging over the sump.

Of course he had.

Maren stood at the last timber set with the water around her ankles, and she did the thing she had done perhaps a dozen times in nine years and never once in front of anyone who mattered.

She struck the timber twice with the bell-hammer, hard, flat. *Tok. Tok.* Then she put two fingers to her lips and whistled, one note that started low and climbed. She had never been able to whistle properly. It came out breathy and cracked at the top, more like a kettle than a bird. It was hers.

Under the roar of the water, something answered.

*Tok. Tok.* Wood or stone, she couldn't tell, struck twice somewhere off to the left and down. And then the whistle came back up the dark: the same rising note, but clean, perfectly clean, a sound that went through the noise instead of around it. Nobody in Saltjaw whistled like that. Nobody in Saltjaw had a beak.

She did it again. He answered again, closer. She kept doing it, two and a rise, two and a rise, and he came up the side cut toward her by it, the way you walk toward a fire on a beach at night, until the cart-lamp found him: soaked to the skin, his feathers plastered flat and dark, holding a broken length of pump hose in one hand like a dead snake and looking extremely pleased with himself.

"It split at the second joint," he said. "Someone let the staging rot. I've marked it."

"You went down the side cut."

"I'm the only one the staging holds."

"The staging is *rotten*. You said so."

"It held me," Ansel said, reasonably, and she hit him on the arm with the bell-hammer, not very hard, and then held on to the wet feathers of his sleeve all the way up.

---

Bastien was at the tag board when they came out. Every tag had gone from *in* to *out* except one, and Maren took the bird off its hook herself and hung it on the right side and only then let go of her husband.

"The bell stays at the bell," Bastien said.

"The bell was ringing," said Maren. "I left a boy on it."

Bastien looked at the boy, who was still ringing, white-faced and diligent, three and three and three, though there was nobody left below. "You can stop now," Bastien told him. Then, to Maren, more quietly: "What was that you did? At the sets. The knock."

"Nothing. A habit."

"It wasn't the call-up."

"It isn't for everyone," said Ansel, and Bastien, who had run a mine for long enough to know what was and was not his business, nodded and let it go.

---

That night they sat on the step with the half-built room behind them and Ansel's feathers fanned out to dry, which made him look half again his size and very foolish, and Maren laughed at him until she stopped.

She had come to Saltjaw nine years ago from Hearthvale, from a kiln family in Secondfire who had not understood why anyone would leave a place built for second beginnings to go and dig holes on the wrong side of Driftfall. She had come for work. She had stayed, she sometimes said, because of a fog.

It had been her first Wetwake. A sea fog had come in off the shelf so thick that the settlement disappeared at arm's length, and she had been caught out on the shingle below the outfall with no idea which way was up the slope. She had called out and the fog had eaten it. Then somewhere near her someone had knocked twice on a piece of driftwood, *tok, tok*, and whistled, rising, the way you would to ask a question. She had tried to whistle back and made that cracked kettle noise, and the stranger had laughed, and knocked again, and whistled again, and she had walked toward it until she nearly fell over a young man with a feathered head sitting on an upturned crate, waiting for the fog to lift, because he could not see in it either and had decided the sensible thing was to stay where he was and let lost people come to him.

"Why two knocks?" she had asked him, once she had stopped being frightened.

"One sounds like a wave hitting something," he had said. "Three sounds like the mine. Two sounds like someone."

It was still the truest description of it she knew. They had never made it into anything grand. It was not a code; it carried no message except *it's me, I'm here, come this way*. They used it in fog and in the mine and once, embarrassingly, across a crowded Flagweek Strip when she had lost him in a parade. It worked because nobody else knew it and because each of them could tell the other's version from any imitation: her cracked rising note, his clean one.

"You shouldn't have gone down the side cut," she said now.

"I know."

"You'll do it again."

"Probably." He folded his feathers down, which took a while. "But you'll knock, and I'll come up."

"That isn't a plan, Ansel."

"It's worked every time," he said.

## Notes

Chapter 2 of **The Island Saga**. Introduces [The Two and Rise](../symbols/the-two-and-rise.md): two flat knocks and one rising whistle, Maren's cracked and Ansel's clean. It is a private habit, not a code, not a resonance, and not a power. It works only because two people know each other's sound.

Saltjaw's public bell calls (three-and-three for *come up*) and the rule that the bell stays at the bell are in [Saltjaw Mine Safety Practices](../cultures/saltjaw-mine-safety-practices.md). Maren breaks that rule here in a small way; the saga will ask her to break larger ones and she will refuse some of them.
