# What Pim Would Not Sell

## Premise

Joss tries to buy the Heartlean Colorstone. Pim lets them hold it over the counter, then takes it back. The refusal is a kept sentence, not a speech about their futures.

## Story

They did not leave. That was the first mistake, and it felt like manners.

Pim set the stone on the linen again and kept his fingers on the case beside it, a plain wooden box lined in undyed cloth. The Refusal Ribbon ticked against the hook when someone on the street passed heavy. Joss put both hands on the counter as if a counter were a place arguments were won.

"I have marks," he said. "Stall marks. I can get more if it's a guild thing. Is it a guild thing?"

"If it were a guild thing there would be a sign, and you would be speaking to a person with a cord," Pim said. "You are speaking to me. The stone is not for sale."

Harl, who distrusted miracles and trusted prices, leaned in. "Then why is it on the counter where hands go?"

"Because hands are how it tells the truth for a moment, and then it is put down." Pim looked at Joss. "You may hold it. Over the wood. Both of you can watch. When I say down, it comes down."

Joss took it.

The amber returned so fast Rill felt it in her own wrist, a sympathy she did not want. It was not only color. The day's crate-ache in Joss's shoulders, the bit he carried from being the boy who laughed first so nobody could call him slow, loosened. He stood easier. The stone had not erased the week. It had made the week step back half a pace, and half a pace felt like a life.

"See," he whispered. "It works."

"Put it down," Pim said.

Joss did not. Harl reached, not to steal, to see, and the amber muddied toward chalk-gray where his fingers joined the hold, a tally color, honest and unlovely. Rill put one fingertip on it because she could not be the only one who only watched. Workshop blue threaded the gray, oil and message-slips and the particular attention of streets. For a breath the stone was all three of them and none of them. It was beautiful. It was also crowded, like three people trying to wear one coat.

"Down," Pim said, and this time his hand was there. The stone left Joss's palm. On the linen it cooled. The colors thinned. They did not snap out like a trick. They ebbed, the way a room ebbs when a argument remembers it has neighbors.

Joss's ease ebbed with them. The week stepped back in. He looked at Pim as if the man had taken a meal off a table.

"That feeling," Joss said. "You can't keep that in a shop and not sell it. People need it. I need it. I'll pay more than it's worth."

Pim turned a card that had been lying face-down by the pencils. The hand was his, the sentence was not dressed up. Rill read it upside down and then right side up, because some lines do not let you pretend.

*Ease you can purchase is the brittle kind.*

"I copied that from holding-cloth talk," Pim said. "I do not own the coves, and I do not sell their weather. The stone leans. Hearttide lean, if you want the word the poets use. It shows a color that is already yours for as long as you are willing to set it down. The moment you buy the ease, you are buying the brittle part. I will not help you do it."

Harl tried for a side door. "Sell us something else, then. The compass. The one that points at what you want."

"The compass points once and sticks, and it will not point at a dream you have not admitted," Pim said. "You have admitted this one too loudly already. No."

He put the stone in the case. The ribbon stayed on the hook. A damp-studio bottle on a high shelf threw a little Averran color on the wall and did not offer to repaint anybody's life.

Rill said, "We should go. I have a cooper waiting."

Joss nodded. It was not agreement. It was the nod of a person who has started doing math that does not involve marks.

On the step, the bell did not ring. They were leaving, and belief was no longer the interesting part. Want was.
