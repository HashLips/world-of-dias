# Myth on the Market Loop

## Premise

Back on the loop, adults laugh Farstock into a children's story. The three plan a return anyway. Harl's chalk will not write the lie that they are only going to look.

## Story

By afternoon the loop had them again. Pears, rope, a woman selling cups of tea with the window already half latched against the dusk she expected later. Rill delivered the cooper's slip late and took the scolding without offering a door as an excuse. Excuses from that street sounded, even in her own mouth, like something a child would say to get out of work.

They met behind the stall where Joss owed an hour. He had spent the hour badly. His hands kept opening and closing on nothing.

"It's a trick of light," Harl said, for the fourth time. "Averran pigment in the bottle. He lets you touch a warm stone. Your eyes do the rest. I have seen worse at fair week."

"You felt your hands change," Rill said.

"I felt embarrassed. Embarrassment has a color. Apparently it is chalk."

A porter they all knew, a man who had walked the outer ring long enough to have opinions about every door, stopped to buy a candy he did not need and heard enough.

"Farstock," he said, pleased. "Still telling that one? Pim likes his quiet. Lets children dare each other up the step so the street feels famous. There is no stone that keeps a feeling. If there were, the second ring would have taxed it and the third ring would have put it in a box with a light."

He laughed, not unkindly, and went away eating gold paper and air.

Joss watched him go with his jaw set. "That's the trick they do. They laugh it until you're ashamed of having hands. I'm going back when the loop shuts. The case isn't locked to a guild. The door sticks. Sticking isn't the same as locked."

"That is theft," Harl said.

"It is borrowing a thing he is too stubborn to price. We'll bring it back when we've— when we've had enough of it to be all right. A night. Two. Then it's his gray rock again."

Rill heard the shape of the lie and hated that it fit her own mouth so easily. She had spent the day hearing, in every adult laugh, the same door closing: wonder was a phase, and the city after the phase was pears and scolding. She did not want the stone's ease as badly as Joss did. She wanted the street to be real after the adults were done with it. Those were not the same want, and they led to the same step.

"If we go," she said, "we don't pretend we're only looking. I'm tired of sentences that make the hands clean."

Harl took a white stick from his pocket, bought that morning because he bought anything that claimed to be a tool. Chalk That Waits. He crouched and wrote on a crate slat, mouth moving.

*Only looking.*

The chalk dusted. No line.

He stared at it. "It needs the words aloud."

"Say them," Joss said.

Harl opened his mouth. The stick hovered. Dust again. He could not put the lie into the air and make the mark come, and he would not put the other sentence out where the stall could hear it. He put the chalk away as if it had insulted him.

"Fine," he said. "We go after the shutters. We look at the latch. If it's truly locked I am going home and you can both be thieves without a counter."

"And if it isn't?" Rill asked.

The loop bell marked the turn toward closing. Somewhere above the rings, pale clusters drifted, too high to recruit into a plan. Joss smiled for the first time since the counter, and the smile was the old one, the one that made hard ideas sound like afternoons.

"Then the myth can do something useful," he said.

Rill did not smile. She tied her hair again, badly, and stayed.
