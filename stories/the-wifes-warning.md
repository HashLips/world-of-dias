# The Wife's Warning

## Premise

Tamsy's letter reaches Sallow Strand by the long relay. Maren leaves the original with Odile and carries Odile's fair record east instead, overland and fast, through Westfold, Hearthvale and Driftfall, spending the last of the plank fund and the letter-keepers' pooled marks. She reaches Saltjaw the evening after the fleet has gone out to wait at the shelf edge. Brann's skiff carries her out on the first flat water. She boards the lighter and tells the envoy, and anyone else who will listen, that the man they are looking for is a person and not a hazard.

## Story

The letter came to Sallow Strand in the hand of a glasswort carter, twelve days after Tamsy wrote it, folded small and grubby from four other hands: a Driftfall pier-boy, a Hearthvale wagon-driver, a clerk at Westfold Toll, a Keep runner on the dune paths. Maren read it standing in the cookhouse doorway with a ladle still in her other hand.

*They're going.*

She read it again, then went up the dune to the Letter House and gave it to Odile, and watched Odile read it the way Odile read everything, side-on and then full-face, all the edges first.

"Sevens," said Odile. "She says the groups are sevens."

"And that was twelve days ago." Maren was already counting on her fingers, and hated what they told her. "The relay took twelve. If I go back the way I came, it's twenty. Eighteen if I don't sleep. The slack comes before the middle of Hushlean, she thinks. Maybe sooner."

"You won't make it."

"I'll make it if I don't stop at the toll to argue." Maren looked at the high shelf. "Odile. I can't take it with me."

"No," Odile agreed. "You can't."

---

They had talked about this, in the long evenings at the table by the window, without either of them saying what they were talking about. The original was the only thing in Dias that said exactly what Ansel had written. If it went out on the water with Maren, it could go into the water. If it went out on the roads, it could be taken at a toll or a pier by anyone with a House stamp and a reason. Here, on the high shelf, under Odile's keys and the Keep's silences, it was as safe as anything in Dias could be.

So Maren went up the ladder, and took the box down, and opened it, and looked at the leaf one more time: the red crescent, the square shaking hand, the two dots and the rising stroke. She did not touch it. Then she closed the box and handed it to Odile.

"It's to you to keep," she said. "Until I come back for it. If I don't come back for it, it's still to you to keep. Don't give it to the Houses. Don't give it to anyone who wants it to say something."

"I wasn't going to," said Odile. She put the box back on the high shelf, and then took the fair record from under the glass on the long table, the one she had made in three nights with *Not the original. Every mark copied; nothing added* written at its foot, and folded it the way Ansel had folded his, in three and then in half, and gave it to Maren.

"That's the one people sit with," Maren said.

"People can sit with the drawer," said Odile. "Take it. If you have to show the envoy what was written, show him that. Every mark's there. If you lose it in the sea, I'll make another."

---

The letter-keepers pooled their marks that night without being asked. Wick Hollen brought a fistful of VEL from his glasswort money and put it on the table and went away again before anyone could thank him. The two Songripe coasters, who had come back a third time to argue about the margins, gave her a length of knotted cord and showed her how to tie a group-count into it with one hand, in case she had to count in the dark. The fisher from Sorel's south shore gave her a dried fish and a blessing she did not understand.

Maren sewed the marks into her coat beside what was left of the plank fund, and the fair record into an oilcloth packet against her ribs, and left before dawn.

She went the way she had come, backwards and faster. She went up the dune paths with a Keep runner who did not speak and did not need to. She paid at Westfold Toll without a word, and the clerk who had recognized her in Brightpass looked at her face and stamped her slip and did not make her wait in the queue. She rode Hearthvale fruit wagons by day and walked by night when the wagons stopped, and slept in the back of carts with her arm through the strap of her pack. She did not go to Secondfire this time either. At the Hearthvale crossroads she paid a salvage driver twice the going rate to take her east with no stops but water, and he did, rattling across Sorel's interior through yard country and salvage camps under the Freebands' signs.

Every night she took out the knotted cord and tied the day's count into it: days gone, days left, Tamsy's guessed slack. Every night the numbers got closer together.

On the sixteenth day she came down into Driftfall Piers in the late afternoon. The pier-ends were empty where the Ward's brass listening pipes had stood. A Concord marshal was sitting on a bollard with his book shut on his knee, and when she asked he told her: the Saltjaw current-reader had called the groups gone to long rests three days ago. The fleet had moved down to Saltjaw to wait. That morning it had gone out.

---

She reached Saltjaw on foot along the coast path at dusk, running the last of it, and found Brann at the jetty with the skiff already in the water and two oars across the thwart, as if he had been waiting every evening for a week. He had.

"They went at first light," he said. "Five hulls. Tamsy's aboard Bastien's lighter with the envoy and the Operators. Hadrin Stell's got the Ward on the lead cutter. They're going to lie at the shelf edge tonight and go on when the water's flat. Tamsy says tomorrow. She says it's slackening now." He looked at her. "You've got that look."

"What look?"

"The look Ansel had before he told me we were going out on the fifth day." Brann put out his good hand and steadied the skiff. "Get in. Sleep in the bottom. I'll wake you when the water stops."

---

He woke her in the grey before dawn with his hand on her shoulder, and she sat up and knew at once, from the way the boat sat, that the counting had stopped.

The sea was flat. Not calm, the way the inshore water was sometimes calm on a windless morning, ruffled and alive. Flat. The skiff sat on it like a cup on a table. Brann's oars went in and came out and the water closed behind them without a ripple. All around them the morning was grey and soft, and very, very quiet.

"Long slack," said Brann. His voice came out too loud and he dropped it. "Nobody fishes out here. Nobody's ever seen it but Tamsy and the dead."

They rowed southwest along the survey line, past the shelf's ribs to the edge, where the bottom fell away under the lead, on a bearing Brann held in his head, and they found the fleet at the shelf edge an hour after sunrise. Five hulls lay together on the flat grey water. The Ward cutter was in the lead with its grey pennant hanging limp. Bastien's lighter was broad and ugly among the slim Driftfall cutters. The Operators' brass drum winked on its gimbal amidships.

Someone on the Ward cutter saw the skiff and shouted, and bows came up out of their cases. Brann stopped rowing.

"Bell-keeper from Saltjaw," Maren called across the water. Her voice carried, the way voices carry over flat water. It was the bell voice, the one that reached the bottom of Third Drift. "Maren Kestry. I want the envoy."

---

Severin Ault came to the rail of the lighter, tall and translucent in the grey light, and looked down at her for a long moment. Beside him Tamsy's face went through four things at once.

"Let her come aboard," Severin said.

Across the water, on the Ward cutter, Hadrin Stell said, "This is a ward expedition under writ. Civilians—"

"The writ gives the envoy authority to speak to anyone there is to speak to," said Severin, without raising his voice, "and she is someone. Let her come aboard, Captain."

Brann brought the skiff alongside, and Bastien reached down his rope-burned hand and hauled her up over the rail, and Brann tied the skiff astern and came up after her.

She stood on the deck of the lighter, salt-stiff and road-filthy, with the oilcloth packet against her ribs, and every face on the five hulls was looking at her: the Ward with their bows half-raised, the Driftfall crews, the two Operators by their drum, the four Concord marshals with their books, the backers' agent in his good coat at the lighter's stern, facets turning.

"You're going to find something today," Maren said. She said it to Severin, but loud enough for the whole flat sea to hear. "I don't know what. He saw a shore. He wrote that he measured it by eye, once. He wrote that something long and heavy, wood and stone together, broke his boat. Someone made that. If there's someone out there who made it, they're not a hazard and they're not a door. They're people." She took a breath. "And if he's out there, he's not a hazard either. He's a person. He's my husband. He floats and he can't fly and he counts when he's frightened and he whistles badly. If anyone on these boats points a bow at a shore because the Houses gave it a grade, they'll be pointing it at him."

Nobody said anything. The water lay flat. Far ahead, low on the southwestern horizon, there was something in the grey that was not quite grey: a faint blue haze, sitting on the water like breath on glass.

Tamsy saw it first. Then Maren. Then everyone.
