# Latch After Market

## Premise

After the loop shutters, the three go back to Farstock. The door sticks and then gives. They take the Heartlean Colorstone. The Refusal Ribbon stays on the hook.

## Story

Veloria's outer ring at shutter-time smelled of quenched coals and fruit that had not sold. The Sky Drifters were a paler rumor overhead. Rill kept her message bag on as if she might still be a person on an errand. Harl carried the chalk he would not use. Joss carried the plan, which was not long enough to deserve the name.

The street one off the loop was empty in the way of streets that have decided they are not a show. No bell rang. Rill noticed, and wished she had not. Performed belief was not what they had brought.

The old door stuck. Joss set his shoulder the way he set it against a crate, and the door gave with a sound like a throat clearing. Inside, the slit of light was gone. The kettle was cold. The listening spoon did not hum. A night-latch thread on the back shutter hung dull red, uninterested, because the hands at the front door were not testing a stranger's house in the way the thread understood strangers. They had been invited, once, by daylight. The shop was not built to call that a lock.

The case sat where Pim had left it, on the shelf behind the counter, linen visible at the lip. The Refusal Ribbon hung on the hook above, red-brown, knotted through the iron as if the no were a separate object from the yes they wanted.

"Don't," Rill said, and heard how late the word was.

Joss opened the case. The stone was gray in the dark, a gray that remembered amber. He lifted it. Color climbed his fingers at once, brighter for the lack of other light, gilding without a sun. He laughed once, short, frightened of the laugh and pleased with it.

Harl whispered, "The ribbon."

"It's on the hook," Joss said. "We're not taking the hook."

He did not hear himself. Rill did. She watched the ribbon stay while the stone left the linen. Shelf-quiet cloth showed the print of where a palm-weight had been, a pause with nothing left to pause over. She thought of putting her hand on Joss's wrist. She put her hand on the door instead, holding it for them, which was the same crime in a kinder shape.

They were in the street again before the stone's light could become a lantern someone official might notice. Joss wrapped it in a stall rag. The amber bled through the weave and made the rag look expensive. Harl pulled them toward the basin side of the ring, away from the loop's last porters, through a cut where flower pots made the dark smell green.

Behind them the door stood ajar the width of a mistake. None of them went back to close it. The ribbon turned, once, in a draft that did not ring the bell, and remained on the hook.

At the ring's outer walk, where the medieval stone gave way to the road camps and the basin's darker fields, Joss unwrapped a corner to be sure he had not dreamed the weight. The color held. It did not ebb. There was no counter under it, and no voice saying down.

"It works better out here," he said, soft with relief. "He was keeping it small."

Rill looked at the rag and at her own fingertip, which had not even touched it this time and still felt blue at the nail, a trick of eyes or something worse. "Or it works worse," she said, "and we haven't stood still long enough to know."

Harl counted their exits the way he counted coins. Loop behind. Shop behind. City still around them, huge, ringed, uninterested. "We said a night," he said. "We find a place to set it down and see if it goes gray. If it goes gray, we take it back before he opens."

Joss nodded, already walking, the rag against his chest like a second heart that had agreed with him. The stone did not cool. The city let them pass, because cities are good at mistaking theft for someone heading home.
