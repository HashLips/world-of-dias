# Her First Failed Voyage

## Premise

Before she will go west to a letter, Maren goes out to the sea it came from. With Bastien's survey ledger, Tamsy's counts, Brann at the oars, and a borrowed fishing skiff, she retraces the southwest survey rib to the shelf edge. A shark, a haze-front, and the counting current turn her back. She comes home knowing that a map of where someone was is not a map of where they are.

## Story

"You want the ledger," Bastien said.

"I want the ledger," said Maren.

He gave it to her. He did not argue. Since the slip of paper from Sallow Strand he had argued with her about nothing, which she found more unsettling than when he had argued about everything. He took the heavy waxed book from the shelf in the mine office and put it in her hands, and said only, "The last day isn't in it. He had loose leaves on the fifth day. They went down with him."

"Or they went into a tube," said Maren, "and the sea brought them to Wabet."

Bastien said nothing to that either.

She read the ledger at the table by the green window over three nights. She did not understand all of it. She understood more than she had expected to. She had kept the bell for nine years, counted tags, timed shifts; she knew what it was to set things out in columns so that someone else could trust them. Ansel's small square hand went down the pages: soundings, tallow, *green dust, green dust, shell*. The fan of survey ribs drawn out from the point. And on the fourth survey day, at the end of the southwest rib, the columns of floats that would not drift, and his note in the margin, *Cause unknown. Do not guess cause*, and under it, smaller, *Tamsy calls it the counting current.*

She put her finger on the place where the southwest rib crossed the shelf edge. *There,* she thought. *That's where it starts.*

---

Tamsy's mother would not let Tamsy go back on the water. Tamsy came to the house the night before instead, with a slate covered in strokes.

"These are my counts from the fourth day and the fifth," she said. "As near as I can remember them. Six, six, five. Then sixes. Then six, five, five. It was going back to sixes when we went over." She put the slate down on the table. "It'll be different now. It changes every time. But if you get out there and it's counting, you'll know you're in it."

"And then what?"

Tamsy looked at the slate for a long time. "I don't know," she said. "That's the trouble. I can count it. I can't tell you where it goes."

Brann came for her at dawn with a skiff borrowed from Tamsy's uncle, a clinker-built fishing boat half the size of The Patient Lead, with a lug sail and two pairs of oars. He had not said yes when she asked him. He had simply turned up. Maren did not ask why; she thought she knew. He sat at the oars with his maimed hand bandaged against the wood and did not hum.

The morning was clear and bright, the first real Brightpass morning of the year. Sprout Vent smoked well on its shoulder inland, a long pale plume. Maren took the ledger in its oilcloth, and Tamsy's slate, and a sand-glass from the mine office, and a bag of painted pegs she had made herself the night before, blue-banded, because that was what floats looked like.

They went out along the southwest rib.

---

It took all morning. Brann rowed steadily and Maren sat in the stern with the ledger open on her knees, matching the shore to his drawings: the headframe, the drying post, the vent. She had never been so far off the point. Saltjaw grew small behind them and then very small. The water went from green over the shelf to a darker, deeper colour.

She knew when they reached the edge because the sea changed under the boat. Not dramatically. A slow lift, and a stillness, and a lift.

"Brann."

"I feel it."

She dropped one of her pegs. She turned the sand-glass. The peg went west, a little, and stopped, and came back toward them, and went west again. She counted under her breath, the way Tamsy had shown her. *One. Two. Three. Four. Five.* Long rest. *One. Two. Three. Four. Five.* Long rest. Fives. It was running fives now. She wrote them on Tamsy's slate under Tamsy's sixes.

"It's here," she said. Her voice did not sound like her own. "It's still here. He was here."

"He was here," Brann agreed. He was looking past her, to the southwest. "Maren. Look at the water."

She looked. Off to the south, low on the sea, there was a brownish smear across the horizon, thin and spreading. It was not fog. It was the wrong colour. As she watched, the wind changed, swinging round from off the land to off the sea, and it brought a smell with it, faint and sweet and rotten, like old fruit and eggs.

"Haze," said Brann. "Vent Haze. Coming back off the water."

She knew what Sorel Vent Haze was; everyone in Driftfall did. It came in the sprout seasons, sweet-rot and gritty, and you shut the windows and tied wet cloths over your mouth and waited. She had never thought of it out at sea. It had leaned coastward on the wind for days, Brann said, and now the wind was bringing it back again, low across the water, thick enough to hide a boat.

And thick enough to hide the vent. She turned and looked for the plume over the land, and it was already a smudge, and in the time it took her to understand what that meant it was gone.

"We can't see the mark," she said.

"No."

"Ansel had the vent and lost it in fog. That's how it started."

"Yes," said Brann. "That's why we're going back."

"*No.*" It came out of her too loud. "No. We're here. We're in the counting. If we go a little further, if we follow it, if—"

Something bumped the boat.

It was not hard. A nudge against the planks under the stern, a flat heavy push that rocked them sideways. Maren grabbed the gunwale. Brann had shipped one oar and was half on his feet. And beside the boat, close enough to touch, a long grey shape slid by under the surface, turning slowly, and came up, and a fin cut the water a hand's width from her fingers and went past, and turned, and came back.

Saltjaw's eastern beaches were shark country. Everyone knew it. Tamsy had told them all about her uncle's shark that followed his boat for a whole morning and did nothing. This one did nothing either. It circled the skiff, slow and patient, and bumped it again, and circled.

"It's curious," said Brann, very quietly. "It's not hungry. Don't put your hands in the water. Don't splash. We're going back now, Maren. We're going back on the bearing while I can still remember it, and you are going to sit in the stern and keep the ledger dry and not argue with me, because I am not losing another one out here."

His voice broke on the last words.

She sat. She held the ledger against her chest. Brann rowed, northeast, on a bearing he held in his head, while the haze came down sweet and grey across the water behind them and the shark followed them for most of an hour and then, as abruptly as it had come, was not there. The counting under the keel faded and gave way to the long patient westward pull of the Shelf Set. Somewhere in the haze, Maren knew, it was taking her blue-banded pegs along the coast toward Wabet, the way it had taken everything Ansel had ever lost off the point.

They found the shelf in the early afternoon by the colour of the water, and the point an hour after that, and the jetty as the haze came ashore over Saltjaw and everyone tied wet cloths over their mouths and shut their windows.

---

That night she sat at the table by the green window with the ledger open and Tamsy's slate beside it, and the smell of Vent Haze in her hair.

The ledger told her where Ansel had been. It told her exactly, sounding by sounding, rib by rib, to the shelf edge. And then it stopped, because past the shelf edge there was no bottom to sound and no mark you could trust, and the water itself picked you up and moved you somewhere else in groups that changed every time. A map of where someone had been was a map of the past. It could take her to the place where the counting began. It could not take her one stroke beyond it.

She needed to know what he had seen. What he had written, when he was out there in it, alone. She needed the letter.

She closed the ledger. She went to the shelf and took down the clay pot they called the plank fund, and tipped it out on the table, and counted the VEL marks in it into columns, the way Ansel would have done.

It was enough to get to Wabet. Just.
