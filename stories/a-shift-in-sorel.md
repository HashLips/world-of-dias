# A Shift in Sorel

## Premise

A Wetwake morning at Saltjaw Mine. Ansel Kestry, the mine's shelf surveyor, reads a tunnel face for the crew who must cut it, goes home to Maren at the end of the shift, and turns down extra pay. Nothing extraordinary happens. That is the point of the chapter: this is the life he will want back.

## Story

The shift bell went at the grey end of the night, three strokes and a wait, and Saltjaw came out of its tarps.

Ansel Kestry was already on the slope above the headframe with his cap in one hand, combing salt out of the feathers at the back of his neck with two fingers. The wind came off the water wet and cold, the way it does in Wetwake, and it laid his crest flat whether he liked it or not. Below him the settlement steamed: kettle smoke from the cookfire under the hull ribs, a thin grey thread from the kiln, and higher, inland, the slow breath of Sprout Vent over its shoulder of broken ground. Nobody in Saltjaw looked at the vent first thing in the morning. They looked at it last thing at night, the way you check a sleeping animal.

He pulled the cap on. It had been cut to clear his crest and patched twice where the crest had won anyway. Then he went down to the tag board.

The tag board stood at the mouth of the main adit under a little roof of hull plank: rows of hooks, and on each hook a brass tag stamped with a name or a mark. You took your tag off the *out* side and hung it on the *in* side when you went underground, and you did the reverse when you came up, and if your tag was still on the *in* side when the last bell rang, people came looking. Ansel's tag had a small drawn bird on it because the stamp-maker had found his name too long. He had never corrected the man. He hung it in.

"Third Drift," said Bastien Crale, who ran the mine and therefore stood beside the tag board most mornings as though it might wander off. He was a heavy human man with a clerk's ledger under one arm and a rope burn across one palm that he had never let heal properly. "Face moved in the night. The night crew says it's talking."

"The night crew says everything is talking."

"The night crew is usually right about Third." Bastien looked at him sideways. "Go and listen to it. I need to know if we cut today or prop today. Propping costs me a day of ore."

"Cutting a face that's moving costs you a crew."

"That is why I pay you to go first," Bastien said, without unkindness.

Third Drift ran east under the slope, through the heat-warped strata that made the whole southeastern flank of Driftfall a gamble. The stone here had been cooked once, long ago, and had cooled in the wrong order. It came in plates. Some plates were sound as a kiln floor. Some looked sound and were held up by nothing but habit. The ore lived in the seams between them: verdigris iron, green-crusted and heavy, and the yellow sulfur crust that burned the nose and sold to the dye yards in Claimscar. It was not treasure. It was a living. Saltjaw paid its debts in VEL marks earned a sack at a time.

The drift narrowed and the air warmed. Lamps hung from iron pins. Two crews were waiting at the face, sitting on their heels, picks across their knees, and they made room for him without comment because they had done it a hundred times.

Ansel was lighter than any of them. That was the plain fact of his body and the reason he had the job. His bones were hollow the way a gull's are hollow, and under the slate-and-rust feathers of his shoulders and forearms there was a layer of dense down that held air. He weighed about as much as a child of twelve. He could lie flat on a ledge of rotten plate that would have dropped a Kharad miner into the dark, and he could feel through his chest when the air in a drift changed pressure. He could not fly. People asked. He could not even glide off a roof without landing badly. The feathers were for keeping warm and keeping dry, and for being stared at in Nauw on the one trip he had made there.

He took out the sounding hammer, a short-handled thing with a brass head, and tapped the face in a line from roof to floor. He listened with his head tilted so that his left eye, the better one, was toward the stone. Sound plate rang. Hollow plate thudded. Plate with a crack behind it made a noise he had never managed to describe to anyone, a sort of tired answer, as if the stone were agreeing with him reluctantly.

There was a tired answer a hand's width under the roof, and another one low on the left.

He snapped a blue chalk line across the face above the first and drew a circle around the second. Blue chalk was survey chalk at Saltjaw. White was for ore, red was for danger, blue was for *the surveyor says*. The crews read it the way sailors read sky.

"Prop under the line," Ansel said. "Two posts and a cap. Then cut the left seam but stop at my circle. Don't chase the iron past it. There's a plate behind there that wants to come down and it will take the iron with it, and you, and me if I'm stupid enough to be standing here."

"It's good iron past the circle," said the older crew lead, not arguing, only grieving.

"It's good iron in a day, when it's been propped."

They propped. He stayed until the cap was set and he had tapped it twice, because that was the rule and because he liked them. The younger crew lead, a Kharad girl with copper-patina skin and an apprentice's habit of asking everything, wanted to know how he heard the crack. He told her the truth, which was that he did not know, and then the more useful truth, which was that he had listened to two thousand faces and been wrong about eleven, and she should write down every face she ever tapped and what it did afterward, because her ears would learn faster from a ledger than from a hero.

Then he went up. He moved his tag from *in* to *out*. The bird on it was dull with someone's thumb.

---

Bastien caught him at the kettle at midday with a bowl of crab broth in each hand, which was Bastien's way of making an offer feel like hospitality. Driftfall rope-crabs had been pulling at the pier lines again; there was a lot of broth.

"I need a second survey day this week," Bastien said. "Paid at shore rate and a half."

"On what?"

"On the shelf. Down by the outfall. I want soundings off the point."

Ansel drank the broth. It was too hot and he drank it anyway, tipping his head back the way he had to, since a beak does not sip. "Who's taking me out?"

"Dorrit, if I ask nicely."

Dorrit Vash was eating at the next bench, a Kharad woman as broad as a door with basalt-grey skin and a skipper's squint. She ran the mine's second ore lighter, the one bought after the storm took the first, between Saltjaw and the Driftfall piers and did the mine's occasional water work when the money was right. She did not look up. "Ask nicely," she said, "and then ask again with numbers."

"It's a day," Ansel said. "It's not a season."

"Today it's a day," said Bastien.

Ansel put the bowl down. "Then I'll give you the day next week. Not this one. Maren's shift runs double while the second bell-keeper's off with her arm, and I'm on the house."

Bastien opened his mouth to say something about money and then, to his credit, shut it. Everyone in Saltjaw knew about the house.

---

The house was a hull-rib room on the slope above the settlement, two ribs of an old keel set in kiln-brick footings and roofed with tarred plank and three layers of salvaged sail. It had one window of green bottle-glass and one door that stuck in wet weather. Beside it stood the frame of a second room, posts only, with a tarp over the floor to keep the boards from warping. For two years every spare VEL mark had gone into a clay pot on the shelf they called the plank fund, and every rest day had gone into that frame.

He worked on it until the light went. He set one cross-beam and pegged it. He was not a carpenter; he was a surveyor who had learned carpentry from people who were patient with him. The beam was level when he finished. He checked it twice with the water-level and once more with his eye, because he trusted his eye more.

Maren came up the path after the evening bell with her sleeves pushed past her elbows and the bell-rope's red mark across both palms. She was human, dark-haired, quick, with a face that did not bother to hide being tired. She stopped at the frame and looked at the beam.

"That's straight," she said.

"It's straight."

"You did that today?"

"I did Third Drift today. I did that after."

She sat on the step and he sat beside her, and she leaned into the feathers of his shoulder the way she always did, as if they were a coat he had been kind enough to wear for her. Below them Saltjaw lit its lamps one by one. Sprout Vent glowed faintly on its shoulder, not enough to mean anything. The sea was dark and loud.

"Bastien wants you on the water," she said. She had heard; everyone heard everything at the kettle.

"Next week. One day."

"Shore rate and a half."

"I told him next week."

She was quiet for a while. "The plank fund likes shore rate and a half," she said at last.

"The plank fund can wait a week. You can't run a double on no sleep and come home to an empty house."

She laughed at that, low, and pressed her forehead to his shoulder. "You'd rather sit here than make money."

"I'd rather sit here than do almost anything," Ansel said, and meant it, and did not know yet that it was the truest thing he would say that year.
