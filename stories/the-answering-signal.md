# The Answering Signal

## Premise

Maren waits at Low Bell Cove for the water to stop. On the ninth day it does. At dusk a thin blue gathers low on the sea, far out, and the old wreck bell in the shallows makes no sound. She knocks two stones together and whistles, cracked and rising. Out of the blue come two knocks and a clean rising whistle. No boat comes in. No one steps ashore. The blue thins and goes. She proves nothing to anyone. She keeps the original letter, and writes what she measured in one column and leaves the other blank.

## Story

She went down to Low Bell Cove the morning after the margins were read, and she went every day after that.

It was not far. A quarter of a morning's walk west along the shingle from Sallow Strand, softly, the way Wick walked, with Glasswake Rock growing black and tall ahead. The cove was a small bite out of the dune line, a curve of grey shingle and pale sand between two low arms of rock, so ordinary that a person walking the coast would pass it without a second look. At its mouth, a few strokes out in the shallows, a dark shape lay half-buried in the sand: an old bronze bell, green with age, tipped on its side, with weed growing from the lip. When the tide went very low and the small waves ran in over it, the clapper rolled and the bell spoke, once, dully, under the water. *Bong.* Then nothing until the next wave.

Things came ashore here. Wick showed her. A tangle of Sorel netting with Driftfall floats still on it. A Saltjaw-pattern cap off some other canister, years old, round-turned. A plank with tar on it from a hull built the Driftfall way, overlapped. Everything the Sorel coast had ever lost to the west seemed to end up here eventually, in this cove, in this sand.

She sat on the rock at the cove's eastern arm with Ansel's spare cap in her lap, and watched the water, and waited.

---

Tamsy came with her the first days and counted. The Lumira water was not the Stillmouth; it did not count the way the shelf did. But the Shelf Set ran along this coast like a slow hand, and the outward water from the south, when it came in, came in on it. Tamsy dropped blue-banded floats off the rock and timed them on a glass she had borrowed from the cookhouse, and wrote her columns, and frowned at them, and wrote more.

"It's slowing," she said on the fourth day. "The set. It's not stopping like the shelf stops. But it's going slack at the edges."

On the sixth day she had to go. Brann had written; the second bell-keeper was ill and Saltjaw needed every hand. Tamsy stood on the shingle with her bag of slates over her shoulder and did not know how to say goodbye, and in the end did not, exactly. She put her hand flat on Maren's shoulder, once, the way you put a hand on a timber to feel whether it's sound, and said, "Write what you measure," and went.

Odile came at noon each day with bread and dried fish and a jar of tea, and sat side-on on the rock beside Maren for an hour, and did not ask what she was waiting for. Wick came at evening on his way back from the glasswort and walked her home along the shingle in the dusk. The letter-keepers kept the house. Nobody else came. The Keep had said no boats; the Keep had said it to every cove. And nobody but the nine of them knew the cove mattered.

---

On the ninth day the water stopped.

She knew it before she saw it, the way Brann had known it in the skiff, from the way the shingle sounded. All her life by the sea there had been a sound under every other sound: the small waves coming in and going out, the hiss and rattle and draw of them over stones. That morning, sitting on the rock at the cove's arm with the sun not yet over the dunes, she noticed that she could hear the cookhouse smoke at Sallow Strand. She could hear a gull a long way off. She could hear her own breath.

The sea lay flat. Not calm. Flat. The little waves had stopped coming in. The weed on the wreck bell's lip hung straight down in clear still water, and the bell lay silent in the sand, and stayed silent all morning, and all afternoon, because there was no wave to roll its clapper.

Odile came at noon and looked at the water and sat down without saying anything, and stayed. She did not go back to the Letter House that day.

---

The blue came at dusk.

It came the way it had come at the Stillmouth, from nowhere in particular. Far out, so far it sat on the horizon, the evening light thickened low on the water into a faint soft haze, the colour of breath in cold air, or the faded blue of the Letter House door. It did not move. It did not come closer. It lay along the edge of the world like a line of chalk smeared with a thumb.

Maren stood up on the rock.

She looked down at herself in the last of the light, and she had a shadow; long, thin, stretching back across the shingle toward the dunes. Out there, under that blue, she knew, nothing would have a shadow at all.

She did not call his name. She had learned that much from the Keep and from Lumira and from the boom. She did not go down into the water. She did not wave, or light anything, or run along the shingle. She bent and picked up two stones from the beach, round grey stones the size of her palm, the kind every child on every shore in Dias has knocked together.

*Tok.*

*Tok.*

And she whistled. Cracked, as it always was. Rising, and breaking in the middle, and finding the rise again on the other side of the break. The bell-keeper's whistle that had never once been clean in nine years, no matter how many times Ansel tried to teach her.

It went out across the flat water toward the blue and was gone.

---

For a long time there was nothing.

Odile stood up beside her on the rock. Behind them, on the shingle, Wick had come along the shore from the west with his glasswort basket and stopped dead at the cove's edge, and stood there with the basket in his hands. Nobody moved. The blue lay on the horizon. The sea lay flat. The wreck bell lay silent in the sand.

Then, out of the blue, across the whole width of flat water between, faint and clear the way sounds are clear over still water at dusk:

*Tok.*

*Tok.*

Not a bell. Not metal. Maren had spent nine years at a bell-stand; she knew metal from wood from stone in her sleep. Two knocks, hard and short and dry. Stone, or knuckle on wood.

And then a whistle. Rising. Not cracked. Clean, all the way up, without a break, the way only one person she had ever known could whistle it, the way he had whistled it every evening for nine years coming up the path from the jetty, so that she would hear him before she saw him.

It hung over the water for a moment.

Then it was gone.

---

No boat came. Maren stood on the rock until full dark, and no boat came out of the blue, and no one walked up out of the sea, and no shape appeared on the horizon that was anything but haze. Little by little the blue thinned, the way it had thinned at the Stillmouth: from the middle outward, like breath on glass. And then it was not there.

Sometime after full dark, very softly, the first small wave of the returning set came in over the shingle and rolled the clapper of the wreck bell in the shallows. *Bong.* And then the next. The sea was moving again.

Maren sat down on the rock. She found that she was still holding the two stones, and she put them in her coat pocket, beside the cap.

"You heard it," she said.

"I heard something," said Odile. She was quiet a moment. "Two knocks. A whistle. Rising. Clean."

"I heard it too," said Wick, from the shingle. His voice shook. "I'll say so to anyone. I'll say it was two knocks and a whistle from the sea at dusk on the day the water stopped. I won't say anything else, because I don't know anything else."

"Nor do I," said Maren.

---

She did not tell the coast. She did not tell the Houses. She told the nine, at the long table, the next night, exactly as it had happened, and every one of them kept it.

A letter came from Veloria two weeks later, by the long relay, in a careful hand on good House paper. Severin Ault wrote that the Witness bloc had at last carried a vote at the classification table to request the loan of the original letter, under every courtesy, to be read, copied, and returned within the year. He wrote that he had been asked to carry the request and had agreed, because he would rather carry it than have someone else carry it worse. He wrote that she should feel free to refuse. He wrote, at the bottom, in a different ink, as if he had added it later: *I hope you are well. I hope there has been some news. I hope, if there has, that you keep it.*

Maren read it twice. Then she wrote back.

*Envoy. Thank you. No. The letter is to me, and I'm keeping it, and Odile is keeping it with me. Read the fair record whenever you like; every mark is there. As for news: I have measured something, and I have not guessed anything, and that's all I'll say to anyone. Two and a rise. —M.K.*

---

That night she climbed the ladder in the Letter House and took down the box, and opened it, and looked at the original one more time: the waxed survey leaf, the fold in three and then in half, the red crescent over the fold, unbroken. *Maren Kestry. Bell-keeper. Saltjaw Mine. Driftfall. Sorel. Please.*

She did not unfold it. She did not need to. She knew the last lines by heart.

*Maren. I am not finished. Do not let anyone tell you I am finished because it is tidy. If I can come up the path I will. You'll hear me before you see me. Two and a rise.*

She put the box back on the high shelf. Then she sat down at the long table under the window, and opened her notebook to a clean page, and drew the line down the middle.

In the first column she wrote: *MEASURED. At Low Bell Cove, on the ninth day of waiting, the sea stopped. At dusk a blue lay on the horizon. I knocked two stones and whistled. From the blue: two knocks, stone or wood, not metal. A whistle, rising, clean. Heard also by O. Brask and W. Hollen. No boat. No one came ashore. The blue went. The sea moved again after dark.*

She looked at the second column for a long time.

Then she closed the notebook, and left it blank.

## Notes

Chapter 30 of **The Island Saga**, the end of the saga. A faint [Stillmouth Blue Mist](../phenomena/stillmouth-blue-mist.md)-like blue sits on the horizon. Maren gives [The Two and Rise](../symbols/the-two-and-rise.md) and receives [The Low Bell Answer](../phenomena/the-low-bell-answer.md) (status **unknown**): two non-metal knocks and a clean rising whistle, heard by three witnesses.

**Nothing is closed.** There is no reunion, no boat, no proof. It does not say whether Ansel is alive, where [Aumere](../regions/aumere.md) went, whether it can return, or whether the signal was him, an echo, or something else. Maren keeps the original of [The Kestry Letter](../artifacts/the-kestry-letter.md) and refuses the Houses' loan request. The "guessed" column stays blank on purpose.
