# Found in Wabet

## Premise

Twelve days after the strike, a glasswort gatherer named Wick Hollen finds a tinned survey canister on the Lumira shore east of Glasswake Rock. He recognizes Sorel survey marks and Sprout Vent's sign. He carries it to Odile Brask at the Letter House of Sallow Strand, where the sea's letters are kept until their people come. Word, not the letter, goes inland.

## Story

The tide had left the glasswort wet and the shore empty, which was how Wick Hollen liked both.

He walked the seaward edge of Lumira most mornings in Wetwake, from the dunes above Sallow Strand west toward the dark hump of Glasswake Rock and back, with a flat basket on his hip and a short knife for the glasswort. Dune-approach glasswort grew in the damp hollows where the sand met the shingle, jointed and green and salt-sweet, and the cooks in Sallow Strand and the carters who took it north on the Keep track paid for it in bread and small marks. It was quiet work. It suited the coast. On this shore, so close to Glasswake Rock and the Lantern in the Stone, you walked softly whether or not anyone was watching, because Silence Law was not a rule you obeyed so much as a manner you breathed. You did not shout on the shingle. You did not make a show of what the sea gave you.

He saw the glint a little after sunrise, at the tide line, among a wrack of weed and wood.

He thought at first it was a bottle. Saltjaw children's bottles came ashore here a few times a year: festival notes rolled up in old glass, sent off the point on the Shelf Set with wishes in them. Wick had found a dozen in his life and taken every one to the Letter House. But this was not glass. It was a tube of tinned copper as long as his forearm, dented, salt-crusted, with a screw cap seated tight and a scrap of strap still fastened to a ring on its side. It was heavier than a bottle and lay lower in the weed.

He crouched beside it and did not touch it for a while.

When he was a young man, before he had come west and learned to walk softly, Wick had worked three seasons on Driftfall Salvage Shore. He had hauled iron off old keels and painted claim marks on uprights and seen what Sorel crews used on the water. He knew what this was. A survey canister. A Saltjaw pattern, by the cap: Driftfall yards turned their caps square-edged and Saltjaw turned them round. Surveyors carried them on the water to keep their leaves dry.

On the side of the tube, scratched into the tin with a point, there was a small mark: a circle with a ring of short strokes around it. He had seen that on Driftfall oral charts all three seasons. It was the many-mouthed sign of Sprout Vent.

He picked it up.

---

He did not open it on the shore. That was a matter of manners as much as anything. Glasswake Rock was a quarter of a morning's walk to the west, and the Keepers did not like the sea's things being made into spectacles within sight of it. He wrapped the canister in his glasswort cloth and put it in the bottom of his basket under the morning's green and walked back east along the shingle, softly, the way he always did, and up the dune path into Sallow Strand.

Sallow Strand was twenty houses and a drying-rack, low timber buildings with grass on their roofs set into the lee of the last dune before the sea, where the Lumira sand gave way to the coast. It smelled of salt and smoke and drying fish. Most of the year it was as quiet as the rest of Lumira. Its one odd thing was the Letter House.

The Letter House stood at the top of the strand, a long timber room older than any other building in the hamlet, with deep eaves and one wide window facing the sea. Inside it was all drawers. Hundreds of them, small and wooden and labelled in a narrow hand, running from floor to roof-beam along every wall. In the drawers were the sea's letters: every bottle-note and tied packet and scratched board that the coast had ever brought ashore and the people of Sallow Strand had ever carried up the dune. Saltjaw children's festival wishes, most of them. Fishermen's last words, a few. Lovers' notes from somewhere along Sorel's south shore. Things in hands nobody could read.

Odile Brask kept it. She was Lineborn, which in a room full of paper was almost a joke she was tired of: tall and narrow and planar, her outline crisp as a pen-cut, the inside of her face faintly crosshatched like charcoal shading. When she turned side-on she nearly vanished. When she turned to face you she was more there than anyone. She was copying a tide ledger at the long table under the window when Wick came in, and she looked up and saw his face and put the pen down.

"What did it bring?" she said.

He unwrapped the canister on the table between them.

---

They opened it together, carefully, with a cloth under it to catch the salt. The cap was very tight. Someone had greased the leather ring with tallow, thickly, all the way round, and screwed it down harder than any surveyor needed to for a day's work. Odile noticed that before anything else, and Wick saw her notice.

"Someone wanted this to stay dry for a long time," she said.

Inside were twelve sheets of heavy waxed paper, eleven of them blank. And one folded leaf, closed with a smear of red wax over the fold, and in the wax a small sharp crescent mark, as if someone had pressed a curved hook into it.

Odile did not break the seal. She lifted the leaf on the flat of a paper-knife and held it to the light from the window, and through the waxed paper they could see, faintly, the shadow of writing on one side and of a drawing on the other, and down both margins, columns of small strokes and dots.

"Survey marks," Wick said. "Those strokes. Driftfall surveyors write them like that for timed floats. Groups and rests."

"Can you read the outside?"

There was writing on the outermost fold, in a small square hand, and he could. *Maren Kestry. Bell-keeper. Saltjaw Mine. Driftfall. Sorel. Please.*

Odile set the leaf down on the cloth very gently.

"We'll open it," she said. "We have to, to know what it is and whether the person it's for is living. That's the House rule: open once, with a witness, record what's in it, and close it again. You're the witness." She took up her pen. "Then we send word to Maren Kestry that the sea has brought her a letter. We don't send the letter."

"Why not?"

"Because carriers lose things," said Odile, "and carriers copy things, and carriers sell things, and the sea already chose where to put this. The person it's for comes to it. That's how it's always been done here. It's slower and it's safer." She slit the wax along one edge with the paper-knife, cleanly, so that the crescent mark was not broken, and unfolded the leaf.

They read it side by side at the window. It did not take long. Odile's face, from the front, did not change at all. Wick had to sit down.

---

The word went inland that week, as words did in Wabet: slowly, softly, and much farther than anyone meant.

Odile wrote two lines on a slip of paper, *the sea has brought a letter for Maren Kestry, bell-keeper of Saltjaw Mine; it is kept at the Letter House of Sallow Strand for her*, and gave it to the carter who took Wick's glasswort north. The carter would take it up the Keep track to the Saltwhisper road-head, with the Keepers' assent, and hand it to someone going east toward the Westfold crossing, who would hand it to someone going over the toll into Hearthvale, who would, sooner or later, hand it to someone going to Driftfall. That was how Sallow Strand had always sent word to the Long Shore of Sorel. It took weeks.

The carter also talked. Carters do. He told the Keep runner at the road-head about a surveyor's letter from a drowned man with a picture of a shore where there wasn't one. The Keep runner told a fruit-cart steward on the Saltwhisper margin, softly, because it was a sad story. The fruit-cart steward told everyone.

In the Letter House, Odile wrote out a careful record of the letter, both sides and both margins, mark for mark, in the House book. Then she folded the leaf back along its own creases, laid it in a flat wooden box lined with dry cloth, and put the box not in a drawer but on the high shelf above the window, where she kept the things the sea brought that she did not yet understand.

She stood looking at it for a long time after Wick had gone.
