# The Cost of Silence

## Premise

Five weeks in, Ansel asks the gathering for a boat and a route home. They refuse, and explain: his head is a chart, and the sea only lets boats out on the rare long rest. He tries to row out alone through the boom at night and fails. Tavi brings him back. Nobody in the argument is wrong enough to be a villain.

## Story

He asked on the thirty-fourth morning, after the count, in front of everyone.

He had thought about how to do it for days. He had thought about asking Nessa alone, in the warm house, and decided it would be unfair to her. He had thought about asking Tavi, who might say yes, and decided that would be worse. In the end he waited until the pebbles were set and people were turning toward their work, and he said, loudly enough to be heard along the wall, "I need to go home."

People stopped. He saw them look at each other, and at Nessa.

"I have a wife," Ansel said. "Maren. She keeps the bell at the mine. She will have been told I'm dead by now, because that's what the mine master will think, and because it's tidy. She won't believe it, but she'll be alone with not believing it. I need a boat. I need someone to take me as far as the Long Shore, anywhere on it. I can walk the rest."

The silence went on. Then an old man near the steps, a human with a face like a dried fig, said, "No."

"Let him finish," said Nessa, without looking round. To Ansel: "Finish."

"I'll swear whatever you want me to swear. I won't tell anyone where you are. I won't tell Bastien there's an island off his shelf. I'll say I drifted to Wabet and walked back. I'll say anything." He spread his hands. "I've been here five weeks. I propped your gallery. I count with you every morning. I know what the Clearing cost you. I'm not the Clearing."

"No," said Nessa. "You're not. Come and sit down, and we'll do this properly."

---

They did it properly, which meant the whole morning, on the high terrace, with perhaps thirty people sitting on the low walls and the rest drifting in and out as their work allowed. It was not a court. Nobody stood above anyone. People spoke when they had something to say and stopped when they had said it. Ansel had seen Freeband crews settle a claim at the Claimscar cookfire like this, and it had taken all night there too.

The answer, when they had walked all round it, was still no. But he understood it by the end, and that was worse.

"It isn't your word we doubt," Nessa said. "It's your head. You're a surveyor. You told us so yourself, the first night. You measure. You can't help it any more than Tavi can help being heavy. If we put you in a boat and row you to the Long Shore, you'll count the strokes. You'll feel the pulls under the keel and know how long they are. You'll look at the sun and know where it stands. You won't mean to. And when you step out onto the shingle on the Long Shore, you'll be carrying us on the inside of your skull like a chart in a drawer."

"Then blindfold me."

"You'd still count," said Tavi, from the wall. He said it not unkindly. "You count in your sleep. I've heard you."

"And if I did? If I carried it in my head and never told anyone, all my life?"

"Then you'd carry it to your mine master, and your mine master's backers, and your wife," said the old man by the steps, "and one day you'd be old and frightened and want to tell someone, the way old people do. Or someone would be clever and work it out from you without your saying. Or you'd be drunk once. Or you'd be kind once, to someone who was lost, and tell them where to find shelter." He shook his head. "We don't think you're wicked. We think you're a person. That's what frightens us."

"There's also the water," said Nessa. "That isn't a choice we make. That's just the sea."

She explained it, sitting on the wall with her hands folded, as plainly as he would have explained a face to a crew. The quiet water inside the boom was not still because of anything the Aumeri did. It was still because the Counting Current ran round it, in its groups, pulling inward and resting, pulling inward and resting. Anything on the water inside the ring was pushed back toward the island on every pull. Their own boats could go out to the boom and back, timing the rests, but they could not go beyond it. Past the boom, the pulls were too strong; a narrow boat rowed out into them would be turned and brought back, or broken on the timbers. Only one thing changed it.

"The long rest," Ansel said. "The count stops. And the water runs out."

Nessa looked at him sharply. "You've seen it."

"Once," he said. "The morning after the strike. Before I came into the blue."

She said nothing for a moment. Then: "Yes. That is the only time anything leaves. It comes perhaps twice a year, perhaps three times, and never at the same distance apart. The pebbles help us guess. They don't tell us." She held his eyes. "The last one was five weeks ago. The next will not be for months."

---

He tried that night anyway.

He was not proud of it afterward. He was not even sure, afterward, what he had thought would happen. He had been a careful man all his life, and for one night he was not. He lay awake in the warm house listening to Nessa breathe in the next room, and to the knock of the boom down in the dark, *tok*, and the long rests between, and at some point the thought of Maren standing at the bell-stand ringing three and three and three for someone who was not coming up became too much to lie still under.

He went down the steps to the harbour in the dark. He took the smallest of the narrow boats. He pushed it off the stone and rowed toward the boom, counting, the way he had watched Nessa count, and on what he thought was a rest he pulled hard for the gap where the two timbers swung on their loop of rope.

He had it wrong by a breath. The pull came early. The timbers swung, not open but closed, and the bow of the narrow boat struck the boom with a crack that went up through his arms. The boat slewed. The next pull came and shoved it back along the timbers, scraping, and he was not strong enough or fast enough to fend it off, and he understood with a cold clarity exactly what had happened to The Patient Lead in the fog, because it was happening to him at a smaller scale.

A hand closed on the gunwale.

Tavi was in the water. He had swum out from the harbour, heavy as he was, along the inside of the boom, hand over hand on the timbers, and he held the narrow boat off the wood with one arm and his iron face a hand's width from Ansel's.

"Rest," Tavi said. "Wait for the rest. Now. *Back*."

They brought the boat back together. Tavi hung on its stern and kicked, and Ansel rowed, badly, and on each rest they gained and on each pull they held. It took a long time. When they reached the stone landing, half the village was there with lamps, and nobody said anything, and that was worse than if they had shouted.

Nessa was there. She looked at the narrow boat, at the split in its bow where it had struck. She looked at Tavi, soaked and shaking. She looked at Ansel.

"You could have drowned him," she said quietly. "He swims like a stone."

"I know."

"You could have drowned yourself, and then your wife would have been right not to believe them, and wrong as well." She took a long breath. "Some people here will want you watched now. Some will want you kept away from the harbour. I will tell them no, because I don't think you'll do it again, and because I would have done the same at your age if it had been my husband. But you'll have to earn that back, Ansel. Not with a promise. With mornings."

He bowed his head. Behind him, someone was already turning the split boat over on the stone to look at the damage. Someone else had gone for a blanket for Tavi.

"I'm sorry," Ansel said, to Tavi, to all of them.

"Don't be sorry," said Tavi, through chattering teeth. "Be right next time. That's harder."

## Notes

Chapter 11 of **The Island Saga**. The refusal has two parts, both canon: an ethical one (a surveyor's head is a chart; see [Aumeri Unfound Ethic](../cultures/aumeri-unfound-ethic.md)) and a physical one (the [Counting Current](../phenomena/the-counting-current.md) returns anything inside the boom on every pull; only the [Long Slack](../phenomena/the-long-slack.md) lets anything out, two or three times a year at uneven spacing). The second part is the sea, not a rule anyone chose, and it is why Ansel cannot simply be rowed home.

Ansel's night attempt shows the strike in miniature and gives Tavi a debt to collect in kindness.
