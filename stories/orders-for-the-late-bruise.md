# Orders for the Late Bruise

## Premise

Lark fuels the plane against her own mouth. The flight back is still a hire. She does not yet refuse it. The cost of that delay starts in the cockpit.

## Story

Night pads smelled of fuel and cold fruit.

Lark ran her hands over The Late Bruise the way she always did, not for luck, for loose cowling and the lie of a tired latch. The hull answered with ordinary metal. It had no opinion about vengeance. Opal loaded nothing commercial. No Softfruit. No slate for Yarrow. A case with slats, strapped where the cloth crate had ridden, and a man the lamps could not fully locate. Sometimes his shoe showed. Sometimes the strap cinched against a pressure and looked cinched against air. He was in a foul, glittering mood, the mood of a person who has decided pain should be contagious.

"You tell anyone at the bar," he said, "and I will know, because the bar has one talent and it is repeating."

"I'm not going to the bar," Lark said. True, that hour. She hated how true.

Opal caught her sleeve at the stair. "You could sit," she said. "You could be late. Weather. A mag. You have used weather before when the grass was wet. Use weather when a man wants a kitchen."

Lark looked at the sky over Veloria, high and occupied by its own mysteries, Drifters if they were bothering to be a cluster, none of them landing to adjudicate a freight contract. The inward rings glowed their too-much glow. The outer ring was hoof and lamp and someone singing badly off a stoop. Medieval and wired in the same dark. She belonged to both and was protected by neither.

"If I don't fly him," she said, "he hires someone who lands harder. Yarrow's strip isn't a secret anymore to the people who already use it. Another pilot won't rake the grass. Another pilot might think the farmer is a door."

"And you think you are a leash," Opal said.

"I think I am already the person who put the fruit in the hull." The thumb-scar itched, which was not a sign, only skin. "I want to be in the room when he tries. That is not the same as agreeing. Don't make it cleaner than it is."

They lifted with the city falling into rings below, a diagram of itself. Dain did not look down. He could not be seen looking. His voice sat in the cockpit a little left of his seat, which made Lark's shoulders crawl. She flew by instruments and by the bone-knowledge of the Wabet heading. The resonant compass sulked, as it sometimes did, and she ignored it in favor of the dumb magnetic and the rivers she trusted. No band opened. The night was only night. F432 continued under the wings in roads and one pale thread that might have been Span's lamps, waystations still awake because fruit did not care about a rich man's face.

"When we are down," he said, "you stay with the plane. I know the house. I watched her cook once, when she still thought I was a buyer who might leave. There is a pot. There is always a pot. Farmers are predictable. It's why they can be paid."

"You were predictable today," Lark said. "You ate the sample."

The voice went ugly. "Fly."

She flew. Below, somewhere, oxen would be down in their smell and Nia would be asleep with chalk in her dreams. The road did not notice one more set of engines. That was the obscenity and the mercy. A private hire could cross the same country the wagons crossed and change nothing on the berm until it decided to change a person's sight. Lark kept the case in the corner of her eye. The last kept fruit. Not the last on the tree, though she did not know that yet. She knew only the weight, and the heading, and the fact that every mile she did not turn was a choice she would have to pay for in a room with witnesses, later, if she was still the kind of pilot who could walk into one.
