# A Place to Remain

## Premise

At the season's turning of the count, the whole island counts together through one long night, and Ansel, counting with them, hears the island's quiet as a held thing. Afterwards the gathering makes him two offers. He may stay forever, as one of them. Or, at the next long rest, they will carry him out on the run, hooded, and set him on a far shore to walk home. He chooses the second, and stays until then.

## Story

The season turned in the count before it turned in the sky.

Ansel had been on Aumere for nine weeks when the pebble wall told him so. He had learned to read it by then, roughly, the way a new crew lead reads a tag board: not every stone, but the shape of the rows. For weeks the groups had run sixes and fives. Then, over a handful of mornings, the fives went out of them and the sevens came back, and the rows on the wall changed their rhythm, and the old people at the count began to look at each other and smile.

"The turning," said the small girl, beside him at the wall. "Tonight everybody counts."

"Everybody?"

"*Everybody*," she said, with enormous satisfaction, as if she had arranged it.

---

They gathered on the seaward terrace at dusk: all of them. He had not known until that night how many there were. Not fifty; nearly two hundred, if he counted the children, which he did, because he could not stop. The old who had to be helped up the steps. Mothers with babies on their hips. The boom crews still damp from the water. Tavi, enormous at the end of the wall. Nessa, in her undyed coat, sitting on a stool someone had carried up for her.

The blue was very bright that night. It lay all round the island a few hundred strokes out, and in the dusk it gave its own light, soft and even and shadowless, so that two hundred people stood along the terrace edge and none of them cast a shadow on the stone.

Nobody made a speech. Someone near the middle of the wall began to count under her breath, and the people on either side of her took it up, and the count went along the wall in both directions like a fire along a fuse until everyone was counting. *Seven. Seven. Six.* Soft. Not in unison exactly: each voice a little ahead or behind, so that the numbers overlapped and blurred into one long murmur, rising on each pull of the current below and falling on each rest.

Ansel counted with them. He had counted alone through a whole night on the open sea to stay alive, and counted every morning for nine weeks at the wall to be polite, and this was neither. He counted because everyone did, and because the girl beside him would have been offended if he had not.

It went on for a long time. Full dark came. The blue held its light. The count did not stop.

And somewhere in the middle of the night, without his noticing when it began, he started to hear it differently.

He would try, later, to describe it to himself honestly, the way he would describe a face, and fail. It was not a voice. It was not a vision. Nothing appeared. Nobody glowed. It was more like the moment underground when you have been listening to a drift for so long that the separate sounds of it stop being separate: the drip and the creak and the far-off picks and your own breath all becoming one sound, the sound of that place, and you understand that you have been hearing it the whole time.

He heard the island.

Not the people, though they were part of it. The stone of the terraces under his feet. The water in the cisterns behind him. The boom in the dark below, lifting and knocking on the count. The pulls and the rests of the current all the way round, holding the quiet water in its ring like a pair of cupped hands holding water. And the quiet itself. He had thought of it, all these weeks, as an absence: no surf, no gulls, no wind. It was not an absence. It was held. Two hundred people counting were part of what held it, and so was the stone, and so was the sea, and so, for as long as he stood at the wall and counted, was he.

He understood then, in his body rather than his head, what Nessa had meant on the high terrace. *A place on a chart stops being a place.* This was a place. If a boat came from the Long Shore and a surveyor measured it, the stone would still be stone and the water would still be water and the count would still go round, and none of it would be this. This would be gone, the way the sound of a drift is gone when you start shouting in it.

He had tears running into the feathers of his face and did not know when they had started.

The count went on until the long rest of the turning, near dawn, when it stopped all at once along the whole wall. Two hundred pebbles went down onto the stone in the same breath, a sound like the first heavy drops of rain. Then people began to laugh, and stretch, and pick up sleeping children, and go to their houses.

The girl beside him had fallen asleep against his arm hours ago. He carried her down the steps to her uncle.

---

Nessa sent for him the next evening. Tavi was there, and three others from the gathering.

"You heard it," Nessa said. It was not a question.

"Yes."

"Most people who are born here hear it by the time they can count to twenty. You took nine weeks. That's fast, for the Long Shore." She folded her hands. "The gathering has talked. We have two things to say to you, and you can choose. Tavi argued for the second one for a long time. You can thank him or curse him later."

She held up one finger. "You can stay. Not as someone we pulled out of the sea. As one of us. A house. A place at the wall. Work in the galleries, because nobody else here can do what you do. Children who would grow up hearing the count." She did not soften it. "You would never leave, and nobody would ever come for you, and in a few years you would hear the island every morning instead of only on turning nights. Some of us think you'd be happy. I think you might."

Ansel said nothing.

She held up a second finger. "Or. At the next long rest, when the water runs out, a narrow boat goes out on the run with you in it. Hooded. Two of us will row. We'll sing the whole way, loud, so that you can't count the strokes or the pulls. You'll lie in the bottom of the boat and see nothing. The run goes west. In the old songs, it comes ashore days away on a coast of sand where the dunes come right down to the sea. We'll put you on that sand, and turn, and come back on the next pull before the run closes. Where you are then, you'll have to find out. It'll be far from your mine. Far from here. You'll walk home." She paused. "You'll swear never to look for us. And we'll believe you, because you'll have earned it by then. With mornings."

"When is the next long rest?"

"The wall says three months. Perhaps four. It never comes at the same distance twice."

He closed his eyes and thought of Maren on the step, leaning into the feathers of his shoulder as if they were a coat he wore for her. He thought of the island the night before, held in the count like water in cupped hands. Both were true. Neither cancelled the other.

"I'll stay," he said. "Until the long rest. I'll work the galleries and count at the wall and earn it. And then I'm going home to my wife." He opened his eyes. "I'm not choosing between this and her, Nessa. I'm choosing the order. You've given me a peace I didn't know there was. I'm grateful for it. I'm not going to pay for it by leaving her alone at a bell for the rest of her life."

Nessa looked at him for a long time. Then she nodded, once.

"Then that's what we'll do," she said. "Tavi will teach you the run songs. You'll hate them."

"I already hate them," said Tavi, grinning.

---

That night Ansel went to the edge of the high terrace alone. The blue lay bright and shadowless all around. Somewhere beyond it, days away along a coast he had never seen, his letter was in someone's hands or no one's.

He knocked twice on the low wall with his knuckles, *tok, tok*, and whistled, clean and rising, out into the blue. Nothing answered. He had not expected anything to. He did it so that there would be one more true thing said in that direction, even if it went nowhere.

Then he went to bed, in a house on an island that was not on any chart, and slept until the count.

## Notes

Chapter 13 of **The Island Saga**, end of Act II. The **shared resonance** is the full-island [Tide-Count](../cultures/aumeri-tide-count.md) at the season's turning. It changes Ansel's perception (he hears the island's quiet as held, not absent) without granting him any power, and without explaining how the quiet is held. The rule for what the island can do with that held quiet in extremity is in [Aumere withdrawal resonance rule](../rules/aumere-withdrawal-resonance-rule.md) and is not shown until chapter 26.

Ansel stays **temporarily**. He refuses the offer of permanent belonging because it would mean abandoning Maren; he accepts a release at the next [Long Slack](../phenomena/the-long-slack.md), hooded, onto a far western shore. The old songs' "coast of sand where the dunes come down to the sea" is Lumira's coast in Wabet, where his letter has already landed. The islanders do not know that name.
