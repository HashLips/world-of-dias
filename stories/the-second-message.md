# The Second Message

## Premise

The boom watchers saw a glint go out on the last long rest. Ansel tells them it was a letter, and that it carried a drawing of their shore. Nessa warns him that information travels farther than anyone intends. He writes a second letter to tell Maren he is alive and to stop anyone from coming. Then he understands what a second letter would prove, and burns it.

## Story

It was a child who told him, in the end, because children do not know what is supposed to be secret.

The small girl who had moved his pebble on his first morning at the count-wall had decided he was hers. She followed him to the cisterns. She sat on the steps while he and Tavi mended a cradle-rope. She asked him questions about the mine and corrected his counting and told him, unprompted and at length, about everything that had happened on the island since she was born, which was not very much and took a long time. On the forty-first day, sitting on the harbour wall with her legs swinging, she said, "On your long rest, the watch saw something go out on the run. Shiny. Like a fish. My uncle said it was a bit of your boat."

Ansel went very still.

"Was it?" she asked.

"No," he said. "No, it wasn't."

---

He went to Nessa, because it would have been a lie of a kind not to.

She was in the warm house, laying stones in the pit. She listened to him without stopping her work: the canister, the leaf, the letter to Maren, the sketch on the back. When he described the sketch, the blue smeared with his thumb and the cliff line and the terraces drawn as level strokes, her hands stopped on a stone and stayed there.

"You drew us," she said.

"I didn't know you were here. I saw a shore for twenty breaths at dusk and I drew what I saw. I wrote that I'd only seen it once. I wrote that it might not be real." He sat down on the bench, hard. "I wrote GUESS beside the position. I wrote that the marks in the margins were the current and not a map. I was careful."

"I believe you." She set the stone down. "It doesn't matter how careful you were. Do you know what a letter does, Ansel?"

He did not answer.

"It goes where it's carried, and then it goes where whoever has it carries it. It's a seed. You plant it for one person. It comes up in a field you've never seen." She sat down across from him with the warm stones between them. "Somebody finds a canister on a beach. They open it. They read a letter from a drowned man to his wife. It's a sad letter and a strange one, with a picture of an island where there isn't one. What do they do?"

"Take it to her."

"Perhaps. First they show it to someone. Then that person shows it to someone. Someone copies the picture because it's beautiful. Someone copies it because it's strange. Someone who can read your survey marks copies it because they think it's worth money. By the time your Maren holds it, if she ever does, there are ten of it, and the ten have gone ten ways, and one of the ways is to whoever on the Long Shore files strange things in a book and sends boats." Nessa looked at him steadily. "That's not cruelty. It's just how saying something works. Information goes farther than you meant it to. Always."

"Then let me send another." The words came out of him before he had thought them. "At the next long rest. Not a picture. Not a position. Just a letter to Maren saying I'm alive and well and she's to tell everyone not to come looking. That I'll come when I can. That there's nothing out here worth a boat."

Nessa was quiet for a long while.

"You'd do that," she said. "You'd tell them not to come."

"For you. And for her. She'd know I was alive. She'd stop—" His voice cracked, the way Maren's whistle cracked at the top. "She'd stop ringing for someone who isn't coming up."

---

They took it to the gathering, because everything went to the gathering. The answer came back in two parts, as answers on Aumere did.

The first part was the sea. Tavi showed him, that afternoon, on the south shingle below the cliff. It was a long strip of pale stones at the foot of the rock, and it was covered, end to end, with things. Bits of wood. A broken oar that was not an Aumeri oar. Fragments of rope with knots in them that Ansel recognized from Driftfall Piers. Shells. A child's carved boat. The torn-off end of a blue-banded float, his own paint on it, from one of the floats he and Tamsy had dropped at the shelf edge.

"Everything we throw in comes back," said Tavi. "Everything that comes into the quiet water from outside comes here too, sooner or later. The pulls bring it. You could put a letter in the sea every day from now to the long rest and every one of them would wash up on this shingle and the children would find it and bring it to Nessa." He picked up the bit of float and turned it over in his heavy fingers. "Your canister got out because it went in on the one morning the water was going out. That was luck. Or the opposite of luck. I can't decide which."

The second part was the ethic, and Nessa gave it, in the warm house that night, with Tavi there and two others.

"Nothing made here leaves," she said. "That's old. Older than the boom. We don't send things. Not food, not wood, not words. Because anything made here points back here. It has our stone in it, or our rope, or our way of knotting, and somebody clever on the Long Shore can follow it home like a dog following a scent."

"It wouldn't be made here," Ansel said. "I'd write it. On my own leaves. I have eleven left in the canister I—" He stopped. The canister was gone. He had nothing. He would have to write on their bark-paper, with their ink.

Nessa watched him understand it.

"And there is a worse thing," she said gently, "which Tavi thought of, not me. Tell him, Tavi."

Tavi did not want to. It was in his face. He said it anyway, looking at the stones in the pit. "A second letter tells them the first one was true. One letter from a drowned man with a picture of an island is a sad story. People cry and put it in a drawer. Two letters, the second saying *I'm alive, don't come*, is proof. It says: there's somewhere he is. It says: there's a shore and it has people on it who are keeping him. It says: the picture was right." He looked up. "If I were on the Long Shore and I got a letter saying *there's nothing out here worth a boat*, I'd get a boat."

---

He wrote it anyway. That night, alone, by the light of the stone pit, on a sheet of the pale bark-paper the Aumeri used for their boom rotas, with a stick of their soft black ink.

*Maren. I am alive. I am well. I am being looked after by good people. Tell them all not to come. There is nothing here worth a boat. I will come up the path when I can. You will hear me before you see me. Two and a rise.*

He read it. He read it again. It was the most honest thing he could write and every word of it was a chart. *I am alive*: then where? *Good people*: then a people. *Not to come*: then somewhere to come to. *Nothing here*: then a here.

He held the sheet over the stone pit for a long time.

Somewhere far away on the Long Shore, if Nessa was right, his first letter was already coming up in a field he had never seen. He could not call it back. He could only decide not to plant a second one beside it.

The bark-paper caught at one corner and burned quickly and almost without smoke, the way their paper did, and he watched it until it was gone and then sat with his face in his hands until morning, when he went to the count-wall and took a pebble and counted with the rest.

## Notes

Chapter 12 of **The Island Saga**. Ansel learns his first message may have escaped: the boom watch saw the [Saltjaw Survey Canister](../artifacts/saltjaw-survey-canister.md) go out on the [Long Slack](../phenomena/the-long-slack.md). Nessa's warning, *information goes farther than you meant it to*, is borne out in Act III.

**Why Ansel cannot send a second message** (canon): (1) the sea: anything put into the quiet water except on a long rest is returned by the pulls to the island's south shingle; (2) the ethic: nothing made on Aumere leaves, because it points back (see [Aumeri Unfound Ethic](../cultures/aumeri-unfound-ethic.md)); (3) the logic Tavi names: a second letter would prove the first. Ansel writes it and burns it. He is not giving Maren up. He is refusing to make her hope into a map.
