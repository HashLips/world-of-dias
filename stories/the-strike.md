# The Strike

## Premise

The fifth survey day goes back to the shelf edge to measure the counting current properly. Fog takes the vent's plume. The current takes the boat farther than anyone knows. Something heavy under the water breaks The Patient Lead at her seam. Dorrit goes down. Tamsy and Brann cling to a dark timber. Ansel floats, and the sea carries him away from all of them.

## Story

Bastien kept the ledger.

He had read Tamsy's columns at the kettle the night before with his lips moving, and in the morning he asked to keep the book a day longer so he could copy the shelf soundings into the drift plan. Ansel let him. It was the mine's book. For the fifth survey day he packed loose field leaves instead, a dozen sheets of heavy waxed survey paper, into the survey canister with two wax pencils, a stub of blue chalk, and a twist of tallow for the lead, and buckled the canister across his chest on its strap the way he always did on the water, so it rode high and dry against his breastbone.

"Home every night," Maren said at the door.

"Two and a rise," said Ansel, and went down to the jetty.

---

The morning was clear. Sprout Vent was smoking well, a long pale plume that leaned inland on the light sea wind, and Ansel took his sightings on it from the point and from the shelf edge and was happy with them. Dorrit was not happy with anything. She had agreed to the fifth day because Bastien had raised the skipper's share and because, Ansel suspected, she wanted to see the counting water again herself and would not have admitted it under torture.

At the shelf edge Brann shipped his oars and Tamsy knelt in the bow. Ansel dropped floats. The current did what it had done four days before: pulled, let go, pulled. Tamsy counted aloud this time so that he could write it as she spoke. Six, six, five. Long rest. Six, six, six. Long rest.

"It's settling," she said. "It's going to six."

"Or it's passing through six on its way to somewhere else," said Ansel, writing.

They worked for most of the morning. He moved the boat along the edge in short steps, setting floats and timing them, trying to find where the pulsing started and where it gave way to the ordinary westward pull of the Shelf Set. It seemed to start farther out than he could measure, past the edge, in deep water to the southwest. The groups grew stronger the farther out they went. By late morning the boat itself was moving with them, a soft surge under the keel every few breaths, then a stillness, then a surge.

That was when the fog came.

It came from the south, low and white, the way sea fog does in Wetwake, and it came fast. One moment Ansel could see the vent's plume standing over the land like a pale tree. The next the plume was a smudge. The next it was gone, and the point was gone, and the world was a grey room with the boat in the middle of it and the water underneath going *pull, and rest, and pull*.

"In," said Dorrit. "Now. Brann, Tamsy, give way. Ansel, give me a bearing."

He gave her the last bearing he had trusted, northeast by the vent, and the oars bit, and they rowed into the fog.

He knew within a few hundred strokes that it was wrong. Not the bearing; the bearing was what it was. The water. The pull of the Counting Current was stronger now and it was not pulling northeast. It came in groups from somewhere behind them and to the left, each group a long patient shove, and between the groups the boat sat still on water that had gone strangely flat and quiet. No chop. No slap at the hull. A hush that made the oars sound too loud. He dropped the lead and found no bottom at the end of the line. They should have been back over the shelf by now. They were not.

"We're being set south," he said. "And west. I don't know how far."

"How do you not know?"

"Because I can't see the vent and I can't touch bottom," said Ansel, "and anything else I tell you is a guess."

Dorrit did not swear. She put the tiller over and told the rowers to pull against the surges and rest between them, and they did, and for a while it seemed to be working.

Then the boat hit something.

It was not like striking rock. Ansel had heard boats strike rock. That was a crack and a scream of wood. This was a deep, dull, enormous knock, wood against something both wood and stone, from below and slightly forward, as if the sea floor had reached up and put its shoulder to the keel. The Patient Lead lifted, slewed, and came down. And at the seam where her two halves had never entirely agreed, she opened.

Water came up through the floor in a sheet.

There was no time for anything orderly. Brann was on his feet with an oar trying to fend off whatever it was, and the oar struck something under the water and jarred out of his hands. Tamsy was bailing with the bailer, which was useless. Dorrit was shouting at them to get the mast down so it wouldn't trap anyone, and Ansel was at the well trying to get the lead line in before it fouled, which was stupid, and he knew it was stupid, and he did it anyway because it was his job.

The next surge of the current came under them and The Patient Lead rolled over.

---

The cold took his breath first. Then the sea closed over him, and then, almost at once, let him go.

He came up like a cork. That was simply what his body did. The down under his feathers held air, his bones were hollow, and the water could not keep him under for long however hard it tried. He broke the surface coughing and found himself riding high, shoulders out, the canister thumping against his chest on its strap, the fog all around.

"Dorrit!"

He saw her. She was a few strokes away, her basalt-grey head just above the water, her arms beating at it. Kharad bodies are dense. Dorrit had told him once, laughing, that she sank in a bath. She was not laughing now. Brann was swimming toward her, thrashing, one-handed, and he got to her and got an arm around her and they both went under together and came up together, and Ansel heard her say something to Brann, short and hard, and saw her push.

She pushed Brann away from her. Toward something.

There was something long and dark in the water beyond them. A timber, Ansel thought, part of the wreck, though it was too big for anything on The Patient Lead. It lay low and heavy in the swell, and there were lines on it, ropes, and Tamsy was already there, hauling herself up onto it with her fingers in the lashings. Brann reached it. He turned back for Dorrit with his hand out.

Dorrit was gone. There was a swirl where she had been, and then there was not.

"Dorrit!" Brann's voice cracked across the water. "*Dorrit!*"

Ansel kicked toward them. He was a bad swimmer; floating and swimming were not the same thing, as Maren had told him. His arms were short for the work and his feathers dragged. And as he kicked, the Counting Current came under him in its long patient group, one, two, three, all the way to six, and moved him. Not far. A few strokes. Then it rested, and he kicked again and gained a little, and then it came again and took more than he had gained.

The timber with Tamsy and Brann on it was moving too, but not the way he was. It lay deep in the water and the surges seemed to slide under it. He rode high, and the surges took him.

"Ansel!" That was Tamsy. "*Ansel, this way!*"

He called back. He swam until his arms burned. The fog thinned for a moment and he saw them both clearly, two small figures on the dark length of timber, Brann with his face in his arms, Tamsy kneeling up and reaching. Then the fog came down and the next surge came, and when it rested he could not see them at all. He called. He heard her call back, fainter, and then fainter, and then only the hush of that flat strange water and his own breathing.

He floated. The sea counted under him. The light went slowly toward evening, and nobody came.

## Notes

Chapter 5 of **The Island Saga**. The cause of the strike is deliberately not revealed in this chapter. It is not accidental in the saga's design: the reader is told it was neither rock nor wreck, that it lay along the boat's drifted line, and that it carried rope lashings. The answer is given in chapter 9 (see [Aumere Turning Boom](../artifacts/aumere-turning-boom.md)).

Dorrit Vash drowns. She dies pushing Brann toward the timber. Tamsy Kell and Brann Oake survive on it; their rescue is told in chapter 16. Ansel's buoyancy keeps him alive and is exactly what separates him from them: the timber rides deep and the Counting Current slides under it, while Ansel rides high and is carried.

The bound [Saltjaw Shelf Survey Ledger](../artifacts/saltjaw-shelf-survey-ledger.md) is ashore with Bastien. Only loose field leaves are in the [Saltjaw Survey Canister](../artifacts/saltjaw-survey-canister.md).
