# Eight Chairs and a Cloth

## Premise

Morning at Mercer Shed. Eight chairs, a washed cloth, wagons still queued outside. A blimp hangs over the basin with a peel painted on it. Ludo Parn prepares to sell a thing the cloth has not yet allowed to be fruit.

## Story

Ludo Parn tapped his stick on the chalked floor and hated how small the sound was.

Mercer Shed had been cleared in a circle that pretended to be a theater and kept smelling like a warehouse. Eight chairs. Not a hundred. Dain wanted hunger more than he wanted a crowd, and then he had invited half the bar anyway, so people stood behind the chairs in a thicket of tickets and good coats and sorter's scarves. Crates of honest Softfruit had been pushed to the walls. A wagon still knocked at the outer door because market day had not agreed to become a myth. Nia Toll's voice, outside, told someone to hold their oxen and not to gawk with a wheel on the threshold. The road had come up to the shed and stopped, offended, alive.

Above the roof, visible through the high vent, The Soft Mark hung in the basin air. Cress Adel had put the blimp where the gilding would find the painted peel. The bite in the paint looked cheerful and stupid from the ground. The gondola was a speck with a purpose. It blocked a patch of light and dropped a slow shadow across the cloth, and for a moment the table seemed to be under a cloud that had taken a bribe.

Yarrow Bine sat on the last chair as if it might be a trap made of manners. He had come because the ticket named a fruit and the fruit had left his grass. His hat was in his hands. He did not belong in lamp-bright rooms where a loader arm rested like a servant. He belonged with stakes. He stayed.

Dain stood beside the table and did not touch the cloth. He had slept or he had not. The clean shoes were a decision. Lark was at the side door in her wool, uninvited to speak, present the way a hinge is present. Ludo cleared his throat and found the patter he used for melons, then backed out of it. Melons did not get eight chairs.

"You will see it before you price it," he told the room, stick still. "You will not touch it until the man who brought it says. If you came for a crossing, walk back to the bar. Bram is cheaper, and he will insult you with more accuracy."

A small laugh. Fear in it, already, which Ludo recognized from bad auction years when the lot was rumored sick. He tapped again. Under the cloth something held light wrong. The weave was not thick enough to kill a glint. A green-gold suggestion moved when Cress's shadow moved, as if the object were remembering the sky and disagreeing.

Ilen Voss had his slate on his knee and his face in the arrangement he used for other people's historic budgets. Hess Calder had his hands folded so tightly the knuckles had their own little audience. Kett Rind stood where sorters stand, near the honest crates, loyal to bruises.

Dain looked up once at the blimp, and the look was not gratitude. A sign in the sky made his secret look like advertising. He had wanted a reveal, not a peel with a bite in it floating over his head like a punchline. Cress, who could not hear thoughts and would not have refunded them, kept the bag steady. Gas, ballast, wind. A vehicle. The pale far clusters of Sky Drifters, if they were out, were higher and unemployed by her ropes.

"Now," Dain said.

Ludo's stick stopped. The cloth was still on. The room leaned. Outside, an ox bell clanked, ordinary, perfect, a reminder that fruit was still a job. Ludo put his fingers on the hem of the cloth and felt, ridiculously, like a man about to uncover a window someone had grown.
