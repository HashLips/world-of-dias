# The Man Who Would Not Come Back

## Premise

The show's glow does not close. Dain's invisibility will not wear off cleanly. By the time he can be furious in private, he is already planning a flight back to the farmer.

## Story

The shed emptied and the man did not return to it.

Lark waited because crew wait, and because leaving an unseen employer in a building with a knife and a kept fruit felt like the kind of story that ended on someone else's floor. Opal coiled rope and pretended the coils needed her entire mind. The loader arm parked. Lamps hummed their dumb hum. From the office end came the sounds of a person mislaid inside his own afternoon: a glass set down too hard, a drawer pulled, a curse with no face to twist around it.

Then, for a few breaths, he flickered.

Lark saw a hand. It was Dain's hand, the clean nails, the ring he wore when he wanted marks to feel ancestral. It groped the air as if the air had a ledger. A slice of mouth appeared and vanished before it could shape the word it wanted. A shoulder. Gone. The coat on the chair did not know whether to be occupied. Flour from the morning still whitened the boards and would not sketch him now at all.

"It should have been an hour," he said. The voice came from the left of where the hand had been. "The farmer said the piece she tried on a bird wore off before the bird finished panicking. A bird. I am not a bird. I paid for a yield, not for a costume that sticks."

He threw the glass. It broke where a wall was. Everyone could see the glass. That was the insult. Objects remained loyal to sight. He did not.

"Get her," he said.

"She's in Wabet," Lark said. "On her own ground. You ate the sample in front of half of Nauw. That's not her hand on your face. That's yours."

"She mixed it." The voice was raw now, pride burned off in patches, the patches not visible, only audible. "She knows how it stops. She will say it, or she will wear it. I still have the one I kept. I am not going to stand in my own shed as a draft and let them invoice a door. You will fly me back tonight. Quiet pad. No tickets. No bar."

Opal stopped coiling. "The road will see the plane," she said, surprising herself. "Yarrow will see it. Night doesn't make you a different hire."

"Night makes me harder to point at," Dain said. "Which is the first advantage I have had since noon. Lark. You are paid through the season. Do not become a philosopher. Philosophers are visible. They like it."

Lark looked at the kept fruit when he set it — somehow, angrily, the hands working even when the eyes of the room could not find them — into a small case. Glass through the slats. Green-gold. It had not browned. It waited the way the bowl had waited, patient as a tool. She thought of the wedge he had eaten and the way the pleasure had opened his face a moment before the face was abolished. The effect was not a stain you wiped. It was a decision the fruit kept making.

"If I fly you," she said, "I am not flying a cure I haven't seen. I am flying a man who wants to put his bad hour into someone else's kitchen."

"Yes," he said. "That is the job. She did this. She can have a piece of it back. Stew, if she's eating. I don't care about the poetry. I care about the proportion."

He flickered again, worse, a jaw and not the eyes. The jaw was furious. It was also afraid. Lark saw the fear and did not forgive him for it. Fear made the plan more likely, not less. Opal looked at her with a rope in her hands like a question.

Outside, Veloria went on selling ordinary fruit. The show was already a story in the rings. The story thought it had an ending: a man ate glass and became a wonder. In the shed, the wonder could not find his own mouth reliably enough to drink water without pouring a line down a shirt that sometimes was not there. Lark picked up her coat. The night was not a yes. It was the direction the hire still pointed, and she hated the pointing, and she went to fuel the plane.
