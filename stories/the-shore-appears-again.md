# The Shore Appears Again

## Premise

On the flat water of the Long Slack the fleet goes into the blue. The mist thins, and the shore Ansel drew stands up out of the sea: pale cliffs, cut terraces, walls, and a dark ring of roped timbers on the water. Nessa Orvay comes out through the boom in a narrow boat. She refuses any landing, measuring or ward, and offers talk instead, and with it the one thing the fleet came for. On the harbour stones behind her, hood pushed back, stands Ansel, ready to leave on the very tide the fleet has ridden in on.

## Story

They went into the blue at mid-morning, five hulls in a loose line on water so flat that the oars of the Driftfall cutters left round dimples behind them and nothing else.

Tamsy stood in the bow of Bastien's lighter with her slate and did not write on it, because there was nothing to write. The counting had stopped. For the first time in her life she was past the shelf edge and the water under her was not doing anything at all. It felt wrong in her feet, like a stair that isn't there.

"It's not all flat," she said suddenly. Severin turned. "Out there. Ahead. It's still going. Slow. Round." She pointed at the blue. "The shelf water's stopped. That hasn't. It's turning. Like a wheel coasting."

Amidships the elder Operator bent over the brass drum, one hand flat on its dark glass, her eyes half shut. The younger one wrote down what she murmured. Severin went to stand beside them.

"Well?"

"Ours," said the Operator. "F432. Every reading is in F432's own register. Nothing leaning in from anywhere else. No bruise, no other-band signature. If that's bleed weather, it's the quietest bleed I've ever heard of." She opened her eyes. "It's quiet. That's all I can tell you. It's very, very quiet."

On the lead cutter Hadrin Stell had heard her across the water; voices carried too well that morning. He said nothing. His Ward stood along the rail with their bow-cases open.

---

The blue was not a fog. Maren had thought it would be like Wetwake fog, grey and wet and clinging. It was not wet. It lay on the water like light that had settled, soft and even, and when the lighter slid into it there was no chill and no damp, and the sun did not go out but went everywhere at once. Maren looked down at the deck and saw that she cast no shadow. Nobody did. Brann looked at his own feet and then very carefully did not look at them again.

Then the blue thinned. It did not lift; it thinned, the way breath on glass thins, from the middle outward. And the shore stood up out of the sea in front of them.

Pale cliffs, not high, perhaps the height of the Saltjaw headframe twice over, stepping back in level terraces cut into the stone, green with low crops. Walls along the terrace edges. Above them, on a spine of dark rock, the turf roofs of houses so grassed over that they looked like hillside, and a thread of peat smoke. A cleft in the cliff with a harbour in it and narrow boats drawn up on stone. And between the fleet and all of it, lying on the quiet water in a long dark curve that ran away on both sides into the thinning blue, a ring of great roped timbers, joint to joint, riding low, lifting and knocking very slowly on the coasting turn of the water. *Tok. Tok.* Like a heartbeat in a sleeping room.

Maren had the fair record out of its oilcloth before she knew she had reached for it. She held it up against the shore. The cliff line. The level strokes of the terraces. The dark squares that were houses, or walls. *Seen once, at dusk. No shore here on any chart.*

"He drew it right," she said. Her voice cracked. "He drew it exactly right."

Behind her Bastien Crale said something under his breath that was not a prayer, because Bastien did not pray, but was close to one. At the lighter's stern the backers' agent had gone very still, facets catching the shadowless light, looking at the terraces and the cut stone of the walls the way a buyer looks at a yard.

On the lead cutter, someone in grey said, "That's the obstruction," and pointed at the timbers, and Hadrin Stell said, "Hold."

---

A narrow boat came out through the ring.

It came through a place where two timbers swung apart on their ropes, smoothly, as if someone had been waiting for it: a gap that opened like a door and closed behind. Two rowers. One was enormous, iron-skinned, with a face like a rockfall, and he rowed as though the oars were made of straw. The other was a young woman Maren did not know. In the stern, very upright, sat an old woman with grey eyes and white hair cut short, in a long coat of undyed wool.

The boat stopped thirty strokes from the lead cutter, inside easy speaking distance on that silent water, and the old woman looked at the five hulls one at a time. She looked at the bows along the Ward's rail. She looked at the brass drum. She looked, for a long moment, at the Concord marshals with their books, as if she knew exactly what books were for. Then she spoke, and her voice was not loud, and everyone heard it.

"My name is Nessa Orvay," she said. "I speak for this place today because the others asked me to. You have come in on the long rest, which is the only way you could come, and you have come with bows, which is the way your people have always come. We would like you to go back out on it, before it closes."

Severin stepped to the lighter's rail. "Elder. I'm Severin Ault, envoy of the Resonant Houses. We've come under writ to learn whether there is a shore here or an obstruction, to recover any survivors of a Saltjaw boat lost on that line"—he nodded at the timbers—"and to place a hazard ward so that no other boat is lost. We don't come to take anything."

"You come to measure," said Nessa. "And to put a mark on us. That is taking. It's the first taking. Everything else follows it." She did not say it angrily. She said it the way someone states the weight of a stone. "No landing. No measuring. No ward on our stone. Those are the answers, and they will not change while you are here."

"Then—" Severin began.

"But I'll give you words," said Nessa. "Which is more than we've given the Long Shore in longer than your Houses have had stamps. You want to know whether there is a shore. There is. You can see it. You can go home and say so, and say also that it doesn't want you, and if your people are wise they'll draw nothing. You want to know why your boats break on that line. It's our boom. It keeps the quiet. A boat came over it at a joint in fog on a hard pull, last Wetwake, and broke on one of our stones, and a woman drowned, and that grief is ours as much as yours." Her eyes went to Tavi at the oars, and back. "And you want your survivor."

The whole fleet went still.

"He's alive," said Nessa Orvay. "He came out of the sea half dead and we warmed him and fed him, and he has worked our galleries and counted at our wall, and he has earned going home. He was to go home today, on this long rest, by a way of our own, and walk to his wife from a far shore." She turned her head a little, toward the harbour in the cleft behind her. "Turn your boats north, put your bows back in their cases, and we will bring him out to you ourselves. That is the offer. It is the only one."

---

Maren was looking at the harbour.

She had been looking at it since the narrow boat came through the boom, because something there had caught her eye the way a wrong tag catches your eye on a board, before you know what's wrong. On the harbour stones, beside a second narrow boat that sat ready in the water with its oars shipped, stood a small group of people. And among them, a little apart, one who was the wrong shape for that island.

Narrow-shouldered. A crest. Slate and rust, even at that distance, even in that light. Holding a hood of dark cloth in one hand, as if he had just pushed it back off his head to see.

She did not say his name. She could not. Her throat had closed on it.

Brann saw where she was looking and made a sound like a man who has been hit. Tamsy turned and saw too, and gripped the rail with both hands.

On the harbour stones the figure with the crest stood very still, looking out at the five hulls on the flat water. He could not have seen faces at that distance. He could not have known which boat was which. But he took one step forward to the very edge of the stones, the way someone at Saltjaw steps to the end of the jetty when a boat comes round the point that might be carrying the person they're waiting for, and stood there with the hood hanging from his hand.

"That's the tide," Maren whispered. "That's the boat. He was going today. He was going *home* today."

Severin had turned from the rail. His colour was moving freely now, a long bright wash down the planes of his face, and he did not try to stop it. He looked at Maren, and then across the water at the lead cutter, and raised his voice.

"Captain Stell. The envoy accepts the elder's terms. We'll turn north. Cases on the bows. The survivor comes to us."

On the lead cutter Hadrin Stell did not move. He was looking at the terraces. At the cut stone. At the blue thinning around a shore that was on no chart, that the Houses had called a hazard and the road had called a door, and that a woman in an undyed coat had just told him was not his. Beside him a Ward bowman held a bow half-drawn, the bolt nocked, the string trembling very slightly in his fingers.

"The writ," said Hadrin Stell, "says place a ward."

Maren put the fair record down on the rail. She put her knuckles to the wood. *Tok.* And again. *Tok.* And she drew in her breath to whistle, cracked and rising, out across the quiet water to the man on the harbour stones.
