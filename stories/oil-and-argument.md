# Oil and Argument

## Premise

Orrin Veld argues that honest frequency measurement belongs in graphs, not House stamps—and vows to build an instrument that shows bands without selling travel.

## Story

The Row smelled of warm metal, orange peel from someone else's lunch, and the particular pride of people who fix what Houses only name.

Orrin Veld's alcove was not large enough for a destiny. It was large enough for a workbench, a rented floor with chalk ghosts of furniture that had moved on, and a graduated cylinder that held tea because Orrin trusted lines more than cups. The tea had gone lukewarm. The lines had not lied about that either.

He held a blank Resonant House form the way other people hold a dead fish.

"I'm not filing a mood," he told Tam Brinkoil. Tam was seventeen, knuckles permanently oil-dark, already tired of being an audience and still somehow loyal. "I'm filing a measurement."

Tam wiped his hands on a rag that had never been clean in living memory. "They'll stamp it nonactionable before the ink dries."

"Then the stamp will be wrong." Orrin's eyebrows climbed when he got earnest—animated, almost comic, the face of a man who believed wrong ink could be embarrassed into honesty. He drew a waveform in the air with two fingers. Softfruit vendors up the lane had learned to duck when his hands started talking. Operators, when they passed, pretended the gesture was not contagious.

"F432 is home," Orrin said. Floor hum traveled up through the metal plates on his boots. Veloria's multi-realm core was a district away and still under his soles, a second heartbeat he had never been cleared to name in public. "Markets. Soup. Heel-caps on floor hum. Everyone agrees we live here. The rest of the stack gets poetry or panic. I want the middle—evidence you can graph without selling a ferry ticket."

What he meant, in plain Row talk: people already lived inside one band and gossiped about others. Houses and Operators named the loud fears and filed the quiet ones as mood. Orrin wanted signatures you could reproduce on paper—still F432 underfoot, still no ticket to elsewhere.

Outside the alcove mouth, graph-dust finches ticked along the eaves—small birds that bathed in eraser grit and sang in short measures. Dox Khar walked past, dense and exact, glanced at the blank form, and kept walking as if refusal could be caught like a cold. Orrin almost called after them. He did not. Dox's respect was a tool you borrowed carefully.

Tam jerked his chin at the empty rectangle chalked on the floorboards—the future footprint of an instrument that did not exist yet. "You're going to build that thing."

"I'm going to build a window that doesn't pretend to be a door," Orrin said. Window meant: graph what bands look like from home. Door meant: a ferry, a subway, a crossing product. He wanted the first and refused the second. He set the cylinder down with the care other people reserved for relics. "Colleague," he told the absence on the floor, half joke, half vow, "we are going to teach the basin how to count without lying."

Somewhere in the third ring a House junior was already warming a stamp. Orrin smiled anyway—asymmetrical, sleepless, already halfway into the argument that would become his life.
