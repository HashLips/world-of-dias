# Copies on the Road

## Premise

A salt-cart steward pays to sit with the letter for an hour and leaves Sallow Strand with a copy. On the long road east, through the Keep track, the Verdant orchards, the Eastbound Fruit Road, and Span Toll, the copy is copied again and again, and every copy loses something true and gains something false. By the time it reaches Veloria it is a handbill, a charm, a door, and a price.

## Story

The first copy was made with permission, and that was the only one that was.

The salt-carts came down to Sallow Strand twice a season from the orchard country to the north, empty on the way down and heavy with Lumira salt and glasswort on the way back. Their steward that Wetwake was a careful, cheerful man who had driven the Keep track for twenty years and never once been labour-taxed for noise. He heard about the letter in the hamlet's one cookhouse on his first night, and on his second morning he climbed the dune path to the Letter House and asked Odile Brask, very politely, if he might see it.

The Letter House custom was old and simple. A sea-letter belonged to the person it was written to. Until they came, it belonged to the strand, which meant any person of Sallow Strand could read it in the House under the keeper's eye, and any visitor could ask. Odile could have said no. She looked at the steward for a long time from the front, which was the way Lineborn looked at you when they meant it, and then she said he could sit with it for one hour at the table by the window, and he could make notes for his own memory, and he could not take it off the table.

He sat with it for exactly one hour. He copied the drawing as well as he could with a stick of charcoal on the back of a salt-tally sheet, and some of the words. He did not copy the columns of strokes in the margins, because he took them for decoration, or for some private surveyor's tally that meant nothing to anyone else. When the hour was up he thanked her and folded his sheet and went down the dune to his carts.

On his sheet, under the drawing, he had written: *letter of a drowned Sorel surveyor to his wife. He saw this shore once. Sallow Strand, Wetwake.* He had not written *GUESS*, or *MEASURE*, or *no shore here on any chart*. He had meant to. He had run out of hour.

---

The carts went north on the Keep track, softly, with Silent Dune Keep assent, past the Saltwhisper road-head where the steward handed Odile's two-line word for Maren Kestry to a Keep runner going east. The salt went on toward the orchard country of Verdant Reach. So did the sheet.

At the first orchard waystation, where the Lumira track met the fruit roads, the steward showed it at supper. It was a good story, and he told it well. An orchard steward's son, who drew, asked to copy the picture, and the steward let him, because it seemed a kind thing. The son was a better artist than the steward. He made the cliff taller and the terraces cleaner. He made the blue, which on the steward's sheet was only a smudge of charcoal described in words, into a wash of real blue from his own paint-box. And in the middle of the cliff, where the original had a dark square that might have been a wall or a shadow, he drew a doorway, because that was what it looked like to him, and because a doorway in a cliff is a better picture than a shadow.

He did not mean any harm. He was making it beautiful.

From the orchard waystation the copies went onto the Eastbound Fruit Road, and the road did what it always did with anything small and portable and interesting. It multiplied it. Drivers copied it for their families. Stewards copied it to sell at the next waystation. Each copy was made from the one before, not from the one before that, and each person who made one left out what they did not understand and put in what they wanted to be there.

By the third waystation the drowned surveyor had a name, though not his own. By the fourth, the blue shore had a gold light over it. By the fifth, someone had written beside the doorway in the cliff: *the way through*. By the sixth, a driver from the outer ring of Veloria swore it was the same island as in the stories about the Lumens boy who went to Stillhollow and did not come back, and that the doorway was where he had gone, and that this proved there was a crossing.

Nia Toll heard that one at a waystation fire two days short of Span Toll, and put her cup down.

She was a wagon steward on the Eastbound road and had been one longer than most of the drivers had been alive. She kept bruise-time in her pocket and waystation manners in her mouth, and she did not believe in doorways. She took the copy out of the driver's hands, looked at it for a long time by the fire, and then held it up so the whole circle could see.

"Where did this come from?"

Nobody knew. A cart before theirs. A waystation before that.

"Then it came from nowhere," said Nia, "and it's going nowhere either. Look at it. There's no name on it. No hand. No place it was found. It's a picture of a cliff with a door drawn on it by somebody who wanted a door." She handed it back. "If there's a real letter, somebody has it, and it doesn't say *the way through*. Real letters from drowned men don't. They say *I'm sorry* and *tell my wife*. You want to trade pictures, trade pictures. Don't trade them as a road."

Some of the drivers laughed. Some looked ashamed. Most of them kept their copies.

Overhead, a little after noon the next day, a plane went over the road eastward, the dark freight plane that some of the drivers called The Late Bruise, low enough that the ponies threw their heads. Nobody looked up for long. A plane was a hire. The road was the road.

---

At Span Toll the copies met the chalk.

Span Toll was a working bridge on Brindle March where Road Marshal discipline met the fruit convoys, and it did not like anything it could not count. In the first week of Brightpass a driver from the fifth waystation tried to pay part of his toll with a painted copy of the blue shore, on the grounds that people were paying good marks for them in the Basin. The clerk refused it. The driver argued. The argument went up the hill to Bren Holloway's office, where the marshal's people looked at the picture, looked at the words *the way through* written beside a doorway in a cliff, and did what Span Toll did with any claim that sounded like a crossing: they wrote it down.

They wrote it down in the marshal's book with the date and the driver's name and the waystation he said he had it from, and beside it, in the column the clerks used for such things, they wrote *copied claim, no source, crossing language*. And because the marshal's office sent a weekly summary of crossing language up the road to the Concord desks in Nauw, as the partial-knowledge manners required, the picture of the blue shore went to Nauw that week as a line in a ledger.

The copies went too, by the cartload, on the ordinary road.

---

In Veloria, in the second ring, on Pot Signal Lane, Lira Pot carried one in the second week of Brightpass.

It was a commission like any other. A woman in a third-floor room on the Lane, whose brother had gone down with a fishing boat off the Outer Rim years ago, had heard about the drowned surveyor's letter and wanted a copy, not to sell, but to keep. She could not afford a copy from a Basin stall, where they had started to go for real marks. She sent a white bloom face-inward on her sill, which meant a private errand, and Lira came up the stairs and listened and went out and came back two days later with a sheet bought cheap from a driver at the depot.

The woman held it at her window for a long time. Lira, who did not usually stay for the endings of errands, stayed.

"It's not real, is it," the woman said at last. "Not this one."

Lira looked at it. The cliff. The door. The gold. *The way through.* At the bottom, somebody had added two small dots and a rising stroke, and under them, in a different ink: *say it twice and he comes back to you.* A charm. A lovers' charm made out of a dead man's letter.

"I don't know what's real," Lira said honestly. "This is what's on the road."

The woman paid her and kept it anyway. That night, in the Peel and Bell, under the older chalk about glass fruit, someone wrote *BLUE ISLAND — DOOR IN THE CLIFF — ASK THE DRIVERS*, and someone else wrote under it, in a smaller hand, *no ferry*, and drew the No-Ferry Mark, and the two lines stayed on the board side by side for a week, arguing.

---

The original letter lay on a high shelf in a timber house on the Lumira coast, in a flat box lined with dry cloth, with its seal unbroken where Odile had cut beside it. It did not have a door in its cliff. It did not have gold. Its margins were full of columns of strokes that no copy on the whole of the Eastbound Fruit Road had kept. In the corner of the drawing, beside the blue, it still said, in a small square hand: *seen once, at dusk. No shore here on any chart.*

Nobody on the road had read that line. It had been left out of the first copy, by a careful man, for want of an hour.
