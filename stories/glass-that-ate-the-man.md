# Glass That Ate the Man

## Premise

The cloth comes off Vitrin. Dain describes nothing he does not have to. He eats a piece, and the man leaves sight while the room is still deciding whether to cheer.

## Story

The cloth came away with a sound like a bed being turned down, and the fruit was worse than the rumor because it was beautiful.

Vitrin sat in a shallow wooden bowl, heavy, clear, about the size of a proud share-apple and nothing like one. Lark could read the grain of the bowl through the rind and then could not, because the inside disagreed: a green-gold depth, layered, as if someone had sliced light thinly and pressed it back into a round. Faint cords, leaf-colored, ran from stem to heart. The seeds were a small cluster of lenses. When Ludo's stick knocked the table leg by accident the fruit answered with a cup-ring, the same note Kett had heard under cloth. Cut-ready. Cold-looking. A thin sweat of sweetness stood on it without dripping, pear skin and green banana and struck metal.

Nobody in the shed had a rank for it. Nauw knew fruit the way it knew gates. This had not come through a gate anyone could name. Dain let the silence work, then spent it.

"Grown in Wabet," he said. "A mix. A farmer who is not here and is not your business. One tree. About ten fruits you could count without lying. No one in Nauw has sold this. No one in F432 has a stall for it. If you came to hear the recipe you can leave thinner than you arrived. I don't have it. She didn't sell it. I bought the yield, not the hands."

Yarrow's hat creaked in his grip. He did not say Sella's name. The room did not have it yet. That mercy was accidental and would not last.

Dain took a knife from the desk, not a ceremony knife, a shed knife wiped too well. He cut a wedge. The face of the cut stayed bright. It did not brown. Juice did not run so much as loosen, cold, almost dry, a sweetness that made the nearest mouths water and the nearest spines unhappy. He lifted the wedge. For a second he was only a man about to eat, which is an old courage and a common one.

He ate.

The rind rang faintly against his teeth, then gave. Lark saw his throat move. She saw the pleasure hit him, real, almost indecent, the face opening. Then the face became difficult to keep. Not a puff of smoke. Not a trick of the vent-shadow from the blimp. The edges of him stopped reporting. Light from the third-ring lamps went through the place his shoulder had been and found the chair behind. His coat was there and then less there, a suggestion of cloth, a fold, gone. The wedge was gone. The hand that had held it was a gap that still knew how to be above the table.

He laughed.

The laugh had no mouth to watch. It came from the height where his mouth had been, and the room made the sound of people who have been given a gift and a threat in the same hand. Someone cheered because cheering was what shows were for. Someone else scraped a chair back so hard it fell. Ludo's stick hit the floor and rolled under a boot. Hess had both hands in the air as if to catch a hat he was still wearing.

Dain — the place Dain was — stepped away from the table. Air moved. A paper on the desk lifted and settled. He was invisible the way a pane is invisible when you are looking at the yard instead of the glass: by the smudge, by the shift, by the fact that a man-shaped nothing had weight.

"Still fruit," the voice said, delighted and already a little too proud. "Still me. Look at your faces. You wanted a hull to mean something. It meant this."

Lark did not cheer. She watched the bowl, where the rest of the Vitrin waited, glass and patient, seeds like eyes that did not bother to open. The bite had not emptied it. The man had not come back. The show, she understood in her stomach, had been designed as a glow. Glow was supposed to end before the lamps did. His laugh went on, and there was nowhere on him to put a hand and ask him to be done.
