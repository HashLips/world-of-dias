# The Houses Classify It

## Premise

The Span Toll summary and a stack of road copies reach a Resonant House classification room in Veloria by way of the Nauw Concord desks. The first reaction is not conquest. It is a file. Around the table, security, truth, legitimacy, and profit pull the file four ways. Severin Ault, a Choirglass envoy, is sent west to ask for the original.

## Story

The file was thin when it reached the room and thick by the end of the morning, and none of the thickness was the letter.

Severin Ault had sat at classification tables for eleven years, first as a reader, then as a clerk of grades, now as one of the envoys the Houses sent out when a file needed a face. He was Choirglass: tall and faceted and translucent, his thoughts showing to anyone who knew how to look as a faint shift of colour along the planes of his face, his voice carrying an overtone under it like a second speaker in another room. He had learned long ago to keep his colour still at tables. It was the first thing envoys learned.

The room was on the third ring of Veloria, high in a House building whose name was not on its door. Through its long windows he could see the Basin and the rings below in Brightpass light, and over them, as always, very high, the pale clusters of the Sky Drifters, gathering and parting. Nobody at the table looked at them. Nobody in Veloria ever did, for long.

On the table lay the summary from the Concord desk in Nauw. It was one line, copied from the weekly crossing-language report of the Road Marshal's office at Span Toll: *Painted image of shore with doorway; text "the way through"; offered in toll payment; copied claim, no source, crossing language.* Under it, the Concord clerk had added a note of her own: *Fourteen further instances reported at Basin stalls. Image now selling.*

Beside the summary lay the copies. The Houses had bought nine of them in four days through ordinary channels, from depot stalls and ring markets, and two more had been brought in by people who thought the Houses ought to know. They were spread across the table like a hand of cards. Cliffs. Terraces. Blue paint. Gold. Doorways. *The way through.* One had two small dots and a rising stroke at the bottom and *say it twice and he comes back to you.*

"Not one of them," said the House archivist at the end of the table, "is the same as any other."

She was an old human woman with ink in the creases of her fingers and the dry patience of someone who had spent her life reading things that were not what they claimed. She had laid the copies out in an order of her own, and Severin, watching her do it, had seen that it was an order of descent: the plainest at one end, the most elaborate at the other, each one a little further from wherever they had started.

"There's a source," she said, tapping the plainest one, a charcoal sketch on what looked like the back of a salt-tally sheet. "Somewhere. This one says Sallow Strand. That's a hamlet on the Lumira coast with a Letter House. They keep sea-letters. If there's an original, it's there."

"And if there's an original," said the man to Severin's left, "it says there's a shore off the Sorel shelf nobody has charted, with blue weather on it that throws no shadow."

---

The man to Severin's left was Hadrin Stell, a captain of the Bleed Weather Ward. He was not a member of any House; he sat at the table by petition, as Ward officers sometimes did when their warning nets caught something the Houses had not yet filed. He was a broad human with a scar across his chin and a soldier's way of sitting, and Severin had not yet decided whether he was frightened or eager. He suspected both.

"Blue that throws no shadow," the Ward man said again. "Quiet water. A current that pulses. You know what that sounds like as well as I do. That's bleed weather. That's something leaning into F432 from somewhere else, on our coast, close to a mine. And the road's turning it into a door with *the way through* written on it." He leaned forward. "Every handbill that calls it a crossing is a dare. We've seen what happens when dare culture meets real bleed. I want it closed. I want the copies seized and the position sealed and a watch put on that water."

"We don't know there's anything on that water," said the archivist.

"We know a boat went down there."

"We know a boat went down somewhere off the Saltjaw shelf, in fog, on a survey for a Claimscar-backed mine." That was the third voice at the table, smooth and courteous: a well-dressed Axiomorph whose facet-planes turned slowly as he spoke, an agent for the mine's backers who had come up the Long Gate as soon as the copies began to sell. "My principals are grieved. They would also like the shelf classified. A hazard grade on the water past the edge would let them file the survey as complete and proceed with the drift under the shelf, with appropriate care. And if there is in fact a shore out there, with good stone and fresh water, as the images suggest—" he spread his facets in a gesture that might have been a shrug "—then it is unclaimed, and someone will claim it, and it would be better for everyone if that were done in an orderly way."

The archivist looked at him over the top of her copies. "Unclaimed."

"It's on no chart."

"Neither was the west, once," said the archivist, "and Wabet would like a word with you about it."

---

Severin let them argue. That was what the table was for.

He had been taught, as all House readers were taught, that every file pulled four ways, and the work was to see all four before anyone stamped anything. *Security*: the Ward man, who wanted it closed before it hurt someone. *Truth*: the archivist, who wanted the original before anyone decided what it said. *Legitimacy*: the Houses themselves, which needed to be seen to have heard a crossing rumor and filed it properly, so that the High Resonant's stamp still meant something in the markets. And *exploitation*, which nobody at House tables ever called by that name, but which sat there all the same in a good coat, turning its facets.

There was a fifth pull, which the readers' manual did not list. A clerk at the far end of the table, a young Lineborn who had not spoken all morning, said it at last, quietly, almost to himself.

"There's a woman," he said. "In the letter. It's to his wife."

The table looked at him.

"I'm only saying," the clerk said, flushing in crosshatch, "the last time we filed someone's raw testimony as hazard before we'd read it, it was the echo-reader at the DEB Gate, and that ended in a schism. Raw letters frighten us because they're true in ways we can't grade. This one's a letter to a wife. If we seize it and seal it, we're sealing a man's last words to her. People will remember that longer than they'll remember a blue shore."

Nobody answered him. Severin saw the archivist's mouth move a little at the corner, in what might have been approval.

---

They stamped it before noon.

It was not a conquest. Severin would insist on that, later, to people who did not believe him. The Houses did not send boats that morning or decide to take anything. They did what they always did first, which was to give the thing a name and a grade so that the markets would stop panicking about it.

The grade was *unfound-shore, hazard pending*. Under the House protocol for such things, which was old and had been used perhaps four times in living memory, that meant: the copies were copied claims with no source and would be stamped as such wherever House clerks found them; no House or Concord document would print any position for the shore; the Ward's watch request was noted and deferred; the backers' petition for a hazard grade on the shelf was noted and deferred; and an envoy would go to Sallow Strand to request the original under the partial-knowledge manners, so that it could be read, graded, and, if it proved to be what it seemed, returned to the person it was written to.

"Request," said the Ward man, flatly.

"Request," said the archivist. "It's in Wabet. The Keepers will not thank us for anything stronger, and neither will the Freebands if we start sending boats into Sorel water on the strength of a painting."

"And if they refuse?"

"Then we will have asked," said Severin, speaking for the first time, "and they will have refused, and we will know more than we do now." He kept his colour very still. "I'll go."

He went down to the Basin that evening to find a seat on a fruit wagon going west. On the way he passed a stall on the market loop where a man was selling the painted copies for real marks, three for a VEL, and a crowd was listening to someone read one aloud. *The way through.* A child at the front of the crowd was clicking a little carved bird on a stick, a toy from some shop off the loop, click and click, not listening at all.

Severin bought one of the copies. He did not know why. He folded it into his coat beside the House stamp, and the two papers rode west together.
