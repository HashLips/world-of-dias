# Hats in the Air

## Premise

Unseen, Dain walks the crowd and lifts hats. The cheer and the fear are the same noise. Lark watches flour fail to explain him.

## Story

Hess felt his hat leave.

There was no wind worth the name, only the shed's high vent and the slow shadow of a blimp that had nothing to do with fingers. The brim tilted, lifted, and was gone upward into air that had a man's heat in it. He grabbed and caught nothing, then caught his own hair, which was worse. A laugh moved past his ear, close, without breath he could aim at.

"Careful," Dain said, from somewhere a polite guest should not stand. "It's a good hat. I am only borrowing the idea of you."

The hat dropped back crooked. People cheered. The cheer had splinters in it. Ludo got his stick and tried to make a lane with words. "Hands where we can — where you can — sir, the lot is not the crowd, the lot is on the table—"

A sorter's cap rose off Kett's scarf and spun. She did not cheer. She tracked the spin the way she tracked bad fruit, furious and precise. Yarrow clapped once, a rural politeness, and then stopped when his own brim twitched and he clamped it down with both forearms, a man defending a small roof. The unseen walked. You could follow him by the disturbance: a shoulder flinch that was not yours, a paper sliding, the warm side of the aisle.

Lark had flour on her cuff from a baker's boy who had brought morning bread to the shed and stayed to be ruined. She cast a pinch into the aisle because she wanted the trick to be dust and mirrors and a third-ring cloth that bent light, a thing a pilot could refuse. The flour found Dain and did not settle. It sketched a chest for a heartbeat, a suggestion of ribs of white, and then fell through him to the chalk as if he had been a rumor about a man. A few grains clung at the height of a wrist and gave up. Not clean magic. Not a costume. A body that light and dust were both failing, differently, like two witnesses who could not share a sentence.

"Again," someone shouted, meaning the hats, meaning the cheer, meaning please let it be only a trick we can clap closed.

Dain obliged. Three hats, a child's cap that had snuck in on a cousin's hip — Lark stepped then, and took the cap from the air by guessing, and put it back on the child's head herself, hard enough to be a scolding of the room. "Not that one," she said. The unseen paused. She felt the pause as a pressure, not a virtue.

"All right," he said, softer, and the softness was not kindness. It was a man saving his best pieces for the people with slates. "You've seen it walk. You've seen it play. Some of you look like you might be sick, which is honest. Don't be dull about it. The fruit did that. The fruit is the lot. I am the sample, and the sample is enjoying himself."

He was not entirely. Lark heard the enjoyment slip on the last word, a tiny catch, as if a tooth had met cold glass again from the inside. He covered it with another lifted hat, Hess's a second time, and the room laughed because laughter was a place to stand. Under the laugh, chairs had moved back. Nobody wanted the front. The bowl on the table was still full of remaining fruit, gorgeous and indecent. Eight would be sold. He had eaten one. He had said he would keep one. The arithmetic sat in Lark's head like cargo that had shifted.

Flour on the floor looked like a failed snow. The child's cap was on the child. Dain's voice circled back to the table, and where he stopped, the cloth they had removed lay in a heap that did not rise or cover him. There was nothing to cover. Hess put a hand on his own crooked hat and found he was shaking, and that the shake and the grin on his mouth were both true, which was the worst courtesy the morning had offered.
