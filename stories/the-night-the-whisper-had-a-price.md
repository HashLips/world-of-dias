# The Night the Whisper Had a Price

## Premise

By night the tickets are no longer gossip. They are a price. Ansel Hip's strange self-propelled craft brings sealed cards through the outer ring, and Hess Calder learns he wants a thing he has not seen badly enough to lose sleep.

## Story

Hess Calder did not like wanting in public, so he did it in The Peel and Bell, which was a failure of strategy.

He had a ticket. He had also heard, from a man who had heard it from a sharp-voiced carter, that the thing in the hull rang like glass and had been carried like a secret. His knee jumped under the table. He was a basin trader. He knew how to want bananas, dye, a stall with better light. This want was shaped like a door he had not measured. He kept almost ordering Hullwater and did not, because if he said *I am afraid it is wonderful* the sentence would arrive at the next table before he could marry it.

The street answered first.

A mutter of motor, wrong for a wagon, and then The Hipspoon came along the outer-ring stones: three biased wheels, a body like a split spoon in boiler plate and tin, a lamp too bright, Ansel Hip on the high seat with goggles he did not need. The craft wagged. It looked like a joke a kitchen had told a factory. Medieval coats stepped aside. A child ran after it and was not brave enough to touch. Hess went to the door because everyone went to the door.

Ansel idled. The motor smelled of hot metal. In the rear box, sealed cards sat in a clip like animals that might bolt.

"Bidding slips," he said, cheerful, to the room at large. "For people whose tickets were never going to be enough. I am not a ferry. I am not a Drifter. I am a spoon with a tiller. If you laugh, laugh while I am still here to enjoy it."

"Does it go to the shed by itself?" someone called.

"It goes where I steer," Ansel said. "That is the whole religion."

He looked at Hess's cream paper, made a delighted noise, and flicked a sealed card at his chest. "Calder. They thought you might try to be early. Don't open it in the bar if you cry easily. Bram charges extra for weather."

Bram, from the counter, said, "I charge extra for you scuffing the step."

Ansel saluted and the Hipspoon curtsied away, ridiculous and effective, already another errand in its teeth. Third-ring craft, outer-ring night. Hess held the card and felt the want acquire a number he had not read yet. That was how prices began: not in the auction, in the hands.

Inside, Ilen Voss had taken a corner as if corners were a profession. Tally slate. Clean shoes suffering the floor. He nodded at Hess with the sympathy of a fellow representative of other people's nerve.

"The counting room wants a demonstration," Ilen said. "They used the word *historic* and then the word *budget* and then they left me the distance between them."

"I'm not a room," Hess said. "I'm a man who cuts fruit for a living and is about to be stupid."

"Then we will be stupid in VEL marks," Ilen said, almost kind. "Do not let Ludo see your face first. He prices faces."

Hess opened the card at last against his palm, not flat on the table. A line of instruction. A suggested posture of wealth. No picture of the fruit. The absence was worse than a picture. His mind supplied glass, and a bite, and a crowd making the sound crowds make when they have paid to be frightened and the fright is on time.

He slept in pieces. Whenever he closed his eyes he saw the Hipspoon's wagging lamp and, behind it, a shed door. The whisper had spent the evening becoming a market. By dawn the bar would be a place people had left. The wood would still be wet. The price would be waiting under a cloth, and he would go toward it with his good teeth and his restless knee and no better plan than *look*.
