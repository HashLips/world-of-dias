# Sun-Sweet and Sky

## Premise

On a sun-sweet basin afternoon, Calen Brink learns that Dias rewards rest with beauty—and that the sky above Veloria remembers in a key no conduit can classify.

## Story

Gilding hour came early that day, as if the basin had decided to be generous.

Light laid gold along the Velorian seam where commerce thinned into field edge and field edge into the kind of afternoon people write songs about later, when they are trying to explain why they stayed. Tamsin walked beside Calen with no market ledger in hand—only time.

"Sun-sweet means the light won't bruise you," she said. "Look at the water. Look at the oranges. Stop thinking like a trial."

Calen tried. It was harder than any load-balance beam and more worth failing at.

They passed a patch of green where a boy threw a ball to his dog while a half-circle of strangers gradually became a circle **on purpose**—the afternoon the ball kept going, live and unscheduled. Andrew laughed. Russell ran. Someone brought water in cups. Calen felt the echo of it in the grass: not ancient bridge-grief, but **present joy**, layered so freshly it almost had no history at all.

"That," Tamsin said softly, "is also Dias."

Harold appeared on the basin road, sweat clean on his temple, pace steady as tide.

"You started running the city yet?" he asked Calen.

"I map by ear."

"Then you're smarter than me." Harold fell in for half a mile without invitation, three runners becoming two and then one again when Harold peeled back toward the long loop. Before he went, he tapped Calen's notebook cover. "Outer ring today. Core still humming. You don't have to choose one map. You have to **feed** yourself."

It was the first time Calen thought they might have a friend who did not want anything from them except continuation.

At the moon-market walk—Tamsin's name for the basin path where stalls stay open past sensible hours—she bought honey on a stick and let it touch Calen's lips before taking it back, laughing at their surprise.

"Don't read that moment unless you want to," she said.

"I want to."

The honesty cost less than echo-touching. Tamsin went quiet in the good way, shoulder near shoulder, while vendors sang price and children stole glances at Sky Drifters high above.

Calen had looked up a hundred times since the DEB Gate. Today they **listened** up.

The Drifters moved in pale clusters as always—too high to name, too steady to dismiss. But beneath their silence Calen felt a harmonic line **almost** like the core thrum, almost like the gate-echo, tuned to a third register: not bridge, not machine, **curiosity without landing**.

One figure at the cluster edge dipped a fraction lower than any record Calen had heard quoted in basin gossip.

Tamsin saw it too. Her hand found Calen's wrist—not fear, **witness**.

"That's new," she said.

"Or newly seen."

The Drifter did not descend. It did not need to. The dip was enough to make the sky feel **closer**, as if Dias had leaned in to listen back.

They watched until gilding faded and the first core lights woke on the far ring horizon—grandeur at distance, not threat. Calen wrote:

*Joy is not the opposite of mystery. Love is not the opposite of load. The sky has a third voice. I am not alone in hearing it.*

Tamsin read the line and did not flinch.

"Tomorrow," she said, "we eat breakfast like people who earned it."

They did.
