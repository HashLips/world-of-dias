# A Letter Against the Current

## Premise

Adrift through a night on quiet water, Ansel sees blue in the fog and a shore that should not exist. He counts the current until dawn to stay awake. In the first grey light he writes to Maren on a survey leaf, draws what he saw on the back, seals it in the survey canister, and pushes it into a rare outward run of water that he believes will join the Shelf Set going west.

## Story

The sea went on counting, and so did he.

He had no other way to stay awake. The cold was a slow kind, not the bite of the first plunge but a patience in the water that worked inward through his feathers and found the down and sat in it. He knew that if he slept he would stop moving, and if he stopped moving he would get colder, and at some point the cold would win. So he did what Tamsy had done in the bow of The Patient Lead with her chin on the gunwale. He counted.

*Pull.* That was one. A long, soft shove from behind and to the left, lifting him a little, moving him a little. *Rest.* The water going flat and hushed again, no chop, no slap, as if the sea were holding its breath. *Pull.* Two.

Six, six, six. Long rest. Six, six, five. Long rest. Six, five, five.

He could not write in the dark, so he kept the groups in his head by tying them to things: the six to the six posts of the second room's frame, the fives to the five notes of Brann's stupid song. Whenever he had a group he said it aloud, quietly, so he would hear himself and know he was still there.

Late in the afternoon, before full dark, the fog had thinned.

He would think about that moment for the rest of his life, and he was careful about it even then, as careful as he had ever been at a tunnel face. The fog did not lift. It thinned, the way gauze thins when you hold it to a lamp, and in the thinning there was colour. Low on the water to the south and west there lay a blue, not the blue of sky or deep water, but a soft cold blue like breath on a winter morning, hanging just above the surface in a long bank. It did not move with the wind. It did not drift. It sat.

And under the blue, for the length of perhaps twenty breaths, he saw a shore.

Pale cliffs, low and long. Lines across them, level lines, too regular to be strata. Steps, he thought. Terraces cut into stone. Above them something darker and squarer that might have been walls, or might have been the shadows of rock. A pale strip at the waterline that could have been shingle or foam.

There was no shore there. He had drawn the fan of the survey himself, and beyond the shelf edge to the southwest there was nothing until Wabet, and Wabet was days away along the coast. He knew this the way he knew the weight of his own hammer.

He closed his eyes and counted to forty and opened them again, the way the old surveyor had taught him to check a sighting he did not believe. The shore was still there. Then the fog came back down and it was not.

*Measure, by eye, once,* he thought. *Don't let it become more than that.*

Night came. He counted through it.

---

The grey came up out of the water before it came down out of the sky. Ansel had been counting for so long that when he could finally see his own hands he looked at them as if they belonged to someone else.

He unbuckled the canister from his chest. It was a plain thing, a tube of tinned copper as long as his forearm, with a screw cap seated on a greased leather ring. Saltjaw surveyors carried them on the water because paper was useless wet and survey leaves were expensive. He unscrewed the cap with fingers that did not want to work, and inside, dry, were the field leaves and the two wax pencils and the stub of blue chalk and the twist of tallow.

He chose one leaf. He laid it on the flat of the canister, which was the only flat thing in the world, and he held the canister up out of the water on his chest, riding high the way his body rode, and he wrote.

He wrote small because there was only one leaf he trusted to be dry enough. He wrote square, the way he wrote in the ledger. When he came to the parts he was not sure of, he wrote GUESS in capitals, and when he came to what he had seen with his own eyes he wrote MEASURE, because that was the rule, and because if this was the last thing he ever wrote he did not want it to lie.

He wrote to Maren.

When he had filled the face of the leaf he turned it over, and on the back, with the red wax pencil and the black and the stub of blue chalk, he drew what he knew. The point and the headframe at the top right, as he had drawn them a hundred times. Sprout Vent's many-mouthed mark above it, one core and a ring of little ticks. The fan of survey ribs. The southwest rib running out past the shelf edge, and on it a cross where The Patient Lead had been struck, and beside the cross: *struck—not rock.* The way the current had carried him, in arrows. And to the southwest, low on the leaf, he smeared the blue chalk with the side of his thumb into a long soft bank, and under it, in black, he drew the shore: the pale cliff line, the terraces as level strokes, the darker squares above. Beside it he wrote: *seen once, at dusk. No shore here on any chart.*

Down the margins of both sides, in columns, he set out the counting: every group he had held in his head through the night, in strokes and dots, six and six and five and the long rests marked with a dash. Those were not a map. He knew they were not a map. They were what the water had done. He wrote that too.

At the bottom of the letter side, under his name, he made the mark he had never made on paper before: two small dots side by side, and a single stroke rising from low to high. Two and a rise.

He folded the leaf in three, then in half. He softened the end of the red wax pencil between his palms until it smeared, and pressed it over the fold, and, because he had no seal and no stamp and his brass tag was hanging on the *out* side of the board at Saltjaw, he pressed the hooked tip of his own beak into the soft wax. It left a small, sharp crescent. Nobody else in Sorel could have made it.

He put the letter in the canister with the remaining leaves. He greased the leather ring with the tallow twist, slowly, all the way round. He screwed down the cap as tight as his hands could manage, and then tighter.

---

The long rest came while he was doing it.

He had been counting without thinking, the way you breathe, and it took him a moment to notice that the next *pull* had not come. The water lay flat and silent around him. He waited. He counted his own breaths instead: twenty, forty, a hundred. Still nothing. The sea had stopped counting.

Then, a few strokes off to his right, he saw the water change. A seam opened on the surface, a line of small ripples and foam, and along it the water began to run. Not toward him, and not toward the blue to the southwest. Away. Outward, north and west, toward the open water beyond the quiet, in a narrow, steady stream like a creek running out of a pond.

He knew what lay north and west. The shelf. And along the shelf, patient and certain as the tide, the Shelf Set, which took every float he had ever thrown off Saltjaw point and carried it west along the coast of Sorel, and on past it, toward Wabet. *Everything we ever lost off the point went west.*

He swam for the seam with the canister in both hands.

It was a few strokes. It took everything he had left. The quiet water held him the way thick air holds smoke; he pushed and it gave and closed again. He reached the edge of the stream with his arms shaking and felt it tug at his fingers, fast, much faster than he could swim, and he understood that if he went into it himself he would not be able to stay in it, and he would not be able to come back out. It wanted something smaller and lower than him.

He kissed the cap of the canister, which is a clumsy thing to do with a beak, and he let it go.

The canister rolled once, found its float line, and lay low in the water with its cap just showing. The stream took it. It went north and west along the seam, quickly, and he watched it until it was a glint and then until it was nothing.

Then the water around him moved again. Not a *pull*: something gentler, a slow drift south and west, toward the blue. He had nothing left to swim against it with. He lay back on the quiet sea, riding high, and as the light grew he began, out of habit, to count again.

## Notes

Chapter 6 of **The Island Saga**. The full canonical text of the letter, the layout of the sketch, the wax-and-beak seal, and the meaning of the margin columns are recorded in [The Kestry Letter](../artifacts/the-kestry-letter.md). The canister is [Saltjaw Survey Canister](../artifacts/saltjaw-survey-canister.md).

The long rest and the outward stream are the first recorded [Long Slack](../phenomena/the-long-slack.md). Ansel does not know what it is; he only knows that it runs toward the [Shelf Set](../phenomena/the-shelf-set.md). The blue is [Stillmouth Blue Mist](../phenomena/stillmouth-blue-mist.md); in this chapter it is a single sighting by one tired witness, and the letter says so.

Unclosed: whether the shore he saw is the shore he will reach. The next chapter answers that it is.
