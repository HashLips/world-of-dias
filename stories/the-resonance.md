# The Resonance

## Premise

On the seaward terrace almost the whole island gathers on the stone and counts, together, against the boats. The fleet watches without understanding. Severin orders the hulls back. The blue thickens and brightens until nobody casts a shadow and nothing can be seen, and the Operators' drum goes still. When the blue lifts, Aumere is gone: island, people, dead and living, and Ansel with them. The boom is left floating around empty water, and a few empty narrow boats drift loose. Then the long-held quiet lets go all at once, the Stillmouth turns to broken water, and the fleet fights its way home.

## Story

At first the count was one voice, and then it was a wall of them.

Maren heard it come along the terrace edge: one voice near the middle, then the voices on either side taking it up, then the whole line, the numbers overlapping and blurring into a single long murmur that rose on each pull and fell on each rest. Only now there was no pull and no rest. The shelf water was flat. The ring of current inside the boom was coasting, slow, a wheel running down. And the count was not following the water.

The water was following the count.

"It's turning faster," said Tamsy. She was at the bow with her slate in both hands and her face had gone the colour of the slate. "Inside the ring. It was coasting. Now it's picking up. Seven. Seven. Seven. It's going to their count, not its own."

"That's not possible," said someone.

"Write it down anyway," said Tamsy, and wrote it down.

---

Maren could see them now, all along the seaward terrace above the cliff: a line of people from one end of the wall to the other, close as stones in a wall themselves. Old people held up by younger ones. Children on shoulders. Boom crews still wet. At the middle of the line, on a stool, a small upright figure in a pale shirt, white hair cut short. Nessa, without her coat. Her coat was over the dead girl.

And three places to Nessa's left, narrow-shouldered, crested, slate and rust and still soaked dark from the water, a figure who did not belong to that island and stood on its stone and counted with it.

Maren knew he was counting. She knew it from the set of his head, the way she knew from the bell-stand which of the miners coming up the shaft were counting their steps. He had counted all night in the water to stay alive; he had written so. Whatever he had done on this island for half a year, he was counting now with two hundred people because he was standing on their stone and there was nothing else to do. Nobody on that terrace was choosing anything for anyone. The stone was the stone.

"Ansel," Maren said, and it came out as nothing. She knocked her knuckles on the lighter's rail, twice, and drew breath, and whistled, cracked and rising, across the water. The rise broke in the middle the way it always did.

Up on the terrace, the line of counting people did not move. If one crested head turned at the sound, she could not see it. The count went on.

---

On the lead cutter Hadrin Stell was shouting for his hooks to come off the timbers, for the cutter to back, for the bows to stay up; three orders that could not all be obeyed. The Ward obeyed none of them very well. Two hooks were still fast in the boom and the timbers were starting to pull on them, slowly, as the ring inside picked up speed.

"Captain." Severin's voice, with its second overtone. It carried over everything. "Captain Stell. Under writ, the envoy has authority over parley and you have authority over the ward. The ward is placed. There is no parley. I am telling every hull to go back out on the slack while there is slack to go out on. Cut your hooks."

For a long moment Hadrin did not move.

Then he drew his own knife and went to the rail and cut the first hook-line himself, and then the second, and the Ward cutter swung free of the boom and backed, oars clashing.

"Back," Severin said to the lighter, to the Driftfall cutters, to the whole flat sea. "All hulls. North. Now."

---

They did not get far before the blue came back.

It did not roll in like fog. It brightened. The thin breath of it that had lain on the water all morning grew denser and brighter at once, from everywhere and nowhere, until the air was full of soft even light with no source. Maren looked down and her hands had no shadows under them. The rail had no shadow. The mast, the sail, the oarsmen: nothing threw a shadow on anything, and the light went on getting brighter without getting any warmer, until the shore, the cliffs, the terraces, the line of people on the wall all went pale in it, like a drawing left too long in the sun.

The count was still going. It was the only thing left that had edges. *Seven. Seven. Seven.* Two hundred voices, one murmur, coming through the light from everywhere at once.

Amidships on the lighter, the elder Operator had both hands flat on the dark glass of the brass drum. Her younger colleague was writing so fast his pencil tore the page.

"Register?" said Severin.

"Ours," said the Operator. "F432. Still ours. All of it. No other band. No bruise. No signature from anywhere. It's—" She stopped. Her fingers spread on the glass. "It's going still. The needle's going still. Not dropping. Not swinging. Going still. In our own register. As if everything in front of us had stopped making any sound at all."

"Where is it going?"

"I don't know." She took her hands off the drum, slowly, as though it had become hot. "It isn't going anywhere I can hear. Write that down. *Not anywhere I can hear.*"

The count stopped.

It stopped all at once along the whole wall, the way the morning count stopped at the long rest, in a single breath. Across the bright water Maren heard, or thought she heard, two hundred small stones go down on stone at once, a sound like the first heavy drops of rain.

Then there was nothing to hear at all.

---

The blue lifted the way it had come, from everywhere at once, until it was only a thin haze on the water and then not even that. The sun was in the sky again. The masts threw shadows. The ordinary noon light of the open sea came back over five hulls, and showed them what was there.

The boom. The great ring of roped timbers lay on the water, joint to joint, unbroken, the grey-pennanted ward-bolt still standing in one of them, rising and falling very slightly.

Inside the ring, a few narrow boats, empty, turning slowly. Some bean poles from an outer garden, roots and all, floating. A drift of turf. A basket.

And beyond them, where the cliffs had been and the terraces and the walls and the houses on the spine of rock and the harbour in the cleft, nothing. Open water. No shore. No rock. No foam on a reef. The lead went over from the lighter on Bastien's order before anyone could think about it, and ran out its whole line and found no bottom.

Maren stood at the rail with the fair record in her hand and looked at the empty water where the drawing had been true an hour before, and did not make a sound.

Behind her Brann was saying, "No. No. No," very quietly, over and over, with the same rhythm as his five-note hum.

---

The quiet broke a few breaths later.

It went the way a held breath goes. The water inside the ring, which had been turning to the count, suddenly had nothing to turn for, and it heaved. A long smooth swell came out from the empty middle of the ring and lifted the boom and lifted the five hulls with it, and set them down, and then the next one came, steeper, and the next, and the sea that had been flat as a table for half a day was all at once broken and confused, short steep waves coming from every direction, slapping against each other, white-topped. The Long Slack was over. The counting current was coming back, and it came back angry.

A Driftfall cutter swamped in the first quarter-hour, filled over the gunwale by a wave that came sideways out of nowhere. Bastien got a line to it. Another took on so much water that its crew had to bail with their hats. The overturned cutter from the morning was smashed against the boom and went to pieces. On the lighter, Tamsy stood in the bow soaked to the waist, reading the water out loud as if it were a slate: "Long one coming. Now. Bow into it. Rest. Rest. Now pull, *now*." Bastien steered by her voice. So, after a while, did the others, because she was the only person on five boats who could tell what the sea was going to do next.

It took them until after dark to fight back to the shelf edge, and half the night after that to reach the jetty at Saltjaw, two of the five hulls under tow, every crew bailing. Nobody else died. Tamsy counted all the way. She did not stop until her voice gave out somewhere past the first rib of the shelf, and then Brann took the count over in his deep uneven voice, with all his fingers, real and missing, beating it on the rail.

Maren sat in the stern of the lighter all the way home with the fair record in its oilcloth against her ribs, facing backward, south and west, into the dark, where there was nothing.

## Notes

Chapter 26 of **The Island Saga**. The [Aumere withdrawal resonance rule](../rules/aumere-withdrawal-resonance-rule.md) is shown working. Trigger: a near-unanimous gathering on the island's stone, counting during the long rest, against imminent violence. It drives the ring current to the count, brightens [Stillmouth Blue Mist](../phenomena/stillmouth-blue-mist.md) until nothing casts a shadow, and ends in one breath. The Operators' drum "went still in F432's own register": no other-band signature, no bruise, nothing heard leaving. Where Aumere went is **not disclosed** and is not a crossing.

Costs shown here: the boom, outer gardens, empty boats and the ward-bolt are all left behind; the held quiet breaks and [The Stillmouth](../regions/the-stillmouth.md) turns to rough water. Costs that fall on the islanders themselves, such as spent elders and how long before it could be done again, are not visible from the fleet. They are recorded only as island song, in the rule entry. Ansel was on the stone, so he went with it. Nobody chose for him. The fleet loses no one else, because Tamsy reads the returning [Counting Current](../phenomena/the-counting-current.md) aloud.
