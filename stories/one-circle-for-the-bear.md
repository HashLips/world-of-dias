# One Circle for the Bear

## Premise

In First Fruit week, a quiet wagon driver off the Eastbound Fruit Road sits down at the noisy back bench of Softfruit Table Hall and is beaten at Valhop by a stall cook who has never lost on her own board. He takes the Switcheroo pebble early, saves it for the perfect moment, and holds it a round too long, so the pebble goes dead in his hand and the game seems lost. What follows is three rounds the bench still talks about, a bear twenty that moves him exactly one circle, and a first bowl that becomes a mark on the back of a board.

## Story

The crates had been coming in for three days, and the whole of Veloria smelled of it.

First Fruit week does that to the outer market. Wabet wagons stand nose to tail outside the gates, oxen steaming, drivers half asleep on the boards, and the stalls ring with ranks spoken out loud before anybody dares say a price. The back bench of Softfruit Table Hall had given up being a bench. There were boards on the bench, boards on upturned crates, and one optimist with a board balanced on a stack of clay bowls by the door.

The driver came in at the tail of the meal rush, smelling of fifty-five days of road. He was from the Wabet end of the Eastbound Fruit Road, and he moved the way people from there move: soft-footed, careful with doors, as if the hall might be asleep. He paid his ladle fee with a fruit from his own share, ate standing, and then stood behind the back bench for a long time, watching.

The stall cook noticed him on her fourth game. She noticed everything at that bench. Her board was the oldest on it, its back so crowded with fruit-stain thumbprints, stamped names, and little marks of steps and bridges that the cloth had gone soft as a dishrag. She had never lost on it. The bench liked to say so, loudly, whenever anyone sat down across from her.

"You've been counting circles under your breath," she said, without looking up. "Sit down or start paying rent."

He sat down. He took a die out of his pocket. It was quiet timber, sanded smooth, with dots pressed in by a warm nail, and when he set it on the bench it made almost no sound at all.

"Ah," said a porter on the end. "A quiet Hare. This'll be good."

"It's a Hare," said the driver, so softly the porter had to lean in. "It's only quiet at home."

They waxed their rocks. Hers was a river pebble rubbed so many times with sap that it had gone dark and glossy as a bean. His was a chip of something grey off the road. The cook took Team Sun because she always took Team Sun. The driver didn't mind. In Wabet, he said, the crescent row was the lucky one, because you could play it after dark. Nobody at the bench could tell whether he was joking.

He threw first, from above the sun, the way the rules say. The timber die touched the big box, hopped once, and settled squarely in the hatched block.

The whole bench said "*Hare-kind*" softly, the way they do. Go gentle on him, he just lost his number.

"The Hare gives way," said the cook, grinning. "Pocket the pebble."

He lifted the Switcheroo pebble off its little square and closed his hand on it. He looked pleased. People always look pleased the first time, because the pebble feels like a secret.

She dropped a plain four onto the scenery, beside a waterfall. Four beat his minus three. Her rock moved one circle toward the sun.

The next few rounds went the way they usually went for her. A plain five. A frog double, ten, and the porter said the frog line, "once for you, once for the meadow," and the driver smiled at that. He won one round with a plain six against her minus two, and the bench clapped Nauw-loud, and he flinched, then laughed. When he won his second, he showed the score on his fingers out of habit, and three people at the next board started doing it too. He won a third with a moth, three times three, and a stall-girl lifted an imaginary moth off the sheet with an imaginary brush.

Through all of it, he kept the pebble.

"Use it," said the porter, when her frog beat his three.

"Not on a frog," said the driver. "You don't spend the hare on a frog."

"Use it," said the stall-girl, when she had moved five circles and he had moved three.

He shook his head. He was saving it for something better: a moth, a cat, a round worth swapping.

The cat came. She dropped a six squarely into the cat's block: five times six, thirty. He dropped a two that stayed in the big box, minus two. It was the round worth swapping, and every face on the bench turned to his closed hand.

He looked at the board for a long moment. Then he left the pebble where it was.

"I'll want it more later," he said.

Her rock moved its sixth circle. One more win and it would reach the last circle, the one beside the sun. The bench made a noise like a kettle starting.

"What?" said the driver.

"It's dead," said the Lineborn on the far end, a copy clerk who sat side-on so she took up no room, kindly enough. "The pebble can't be used in the round that ends the game. She's one win from the end. Any round she wins ends it, and any swap that would hand it to her ends it too. It's a pebble now. It's just a pebble."

The driver opened his hand and looked at it. It was a very ordinary pebble.

"Hare-kind," said the cook, and meant it.

He laughed then, a real one, the first loud sound he had made in the hall. He set the pebble on the bench beside his bowl, where he could see it, and said, "Then I'll just have to win four."

Nobody wins four in a row against her from one off the end. The bench knew it, the cook knew it, and the children who had gathered behind the bench to count knew it best of all.

He won the first one because of a cat. Her die was still rolling when a Lap-Cat stepped off the bench, walked across the sheet as if it owned it, and sat down in the middle of the big box. The die bumped its tail. A bumped die is thrown again. And a Lap-Cat on a board stops the game until it leaves on its own, so the whole back bench sat with their hands in their laps while the cat washed one paw.

"The cat has chosen your lap," the porter told the board. "Don't move."

While they waited, someone asked the driver about the road, and he told them, quietly, about fifty-five days at ox pace, about waystations where the keepers shout ethics before prices, about evenings on the choir meadows where you win a game and get a clap smaller than a frog. A Market Grin-Pig that had wandered in from the food rows nosed at the floor for dropped pits and found none, because nobody at that bench drops a pit. Eventually the cat yawned and left. The cook threw again and her die stayed in the box: minus four. He dropped a plain two. Four circles.

He won the second with the wolf: a three in the wolf's block, twelve, against her plain six.

"Four for the wolf," said the stall-girl. "It's howling, not hunting."

Five circles.

Then he stood up to throw, held the timber die over the sun for a long breath, and let it go.

It touched the big box, skipped, rolled the length of a thumb, and stopped inside the bear's block, the smallest block on the sheet, the one most people never land in at all. The top face showed six.

Nobody said anything. Then everybody did.

"In."

"It's on the line."

"That's in."

"That is *on the line*."

By the rule, only the two players' eyes count. If they can't agree whether a die is more than eight parts in ten inside a block, the thrower throws again. The cook bent close. Her nose was a finger's width from the die. She looked for a long time, and the bench went so quiet that the hanging pans could be heard ticking as they cooled.

"Give it the edge," she said, at last, to the Lineborn.

The copy clerk turned side-on, so that from the front she nearly disappeared, and sighted along the die's outline against the printed line. "In," she said. "By a hair's width of a hair. But it's your eyes that count."

The cook straightened up. "It's in," she said. "Six bears. A hundred and twenty."

Then she said the bear line herself, which the loser almost never does: "Small bear, big huff. Twenty, and leave it a fruit."

What happened next is the part people at that bench still tell. Nobody cheered. The driver didn't move. Then, from somewhere near the door, someone made the Wabet clap: palm to palm, barely a sound. Then somebody else. Then the whole back bench of the loudest hall in Nauw was clapping the way they clap on the choir meadows, softly, so softly you could hear the steam lift off the bowls. For a moment the hall was quiet, and the quiet was full of people.

Her throw against the hundred and twenty was a plain one. His rock moved its sixth circle. Six and six, both one win from the end.

"You know what a bear is worth," said the cook, setting up the final round, "on the last stretch?"

"One circle," said the driver.

"One circle," she agreed. "Same as a frog. Same as a plain two on a waterfall."

"I know," he said. "It was still a bear."

The final round was nothing. That is the way of final rounds. She dropped a plain three onto the river scenery. He dropped his die square into the big box, and it slid out the far side onto a printed waterfall and lay there showing two. Three beats two. Her rock went home to the sun.

He was already standing. He took the kettle, poured the first bowl, and set it in front of her with both hands, Wabet-fashion, without a word. Then he went to his crate by the door and came back with one fruit, and put it on the bench "for the bear." The children behind the bench understood exactly what that meant, and the fruit was gone before the bowl was cool.

The cook folded the board once across the empty gap below the big box and tied it, the way the winner does. Then, because she was the owner, she untied it again and turned it over.

"Mark it," she said.

"It's your board."

"It's my board," she said, "and I've never lost on it, and the bench is going to talk about the night I nearly did until I'm older than the hall. Mark it."

He had a leaf from his wagon in his pocket, a Wabet leaf, still soft. He pressed it flat against the cloth, traced its outline with her pen, and beside it, very small, drew a bear walking.

When he came back the next week, on the empty return, the bench saw him at the door before he had the ladle fee out of his pocket. Three boards were already open.

"Fancy a Hare?" said the porter.
