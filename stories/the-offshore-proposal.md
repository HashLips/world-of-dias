# The Offshore Proposal

## Premise

The verdigris seam dips seaward. Bastien Crale wants a tunnel under the coastal shelf and needs to know whether the shelf will hold. Ansel agrees to chart depth, current, and rock from the water over a short season of survey days. He and Maren decide it together, for the second room and for nothing grander.

## Story

Bastien unrolled the plan on the kettle bench and weighted the corners with four bowls, which meant he wanted everyone to see it.

It was a good plan, as plans went. Ansel had drawn half of it himself over two years without knowing that was what he was doing. The verdigris seam that fed Third Drift ran east and slightly down, and every face they cut along it was richer and wetter than the last. It did not stop at the shore. It ran on under the shingle and the outfall and out beneath the coastal shelf, the long shallow table of rock that lay off Saltjaw's point before the sea floor dropped away. If the plate above it was sound, a drift could follow the seam under the water for a long way. If the plate was not sound, the sea would come in and take the drift and everyone in it.

"The Claimscar backers want an answer before Brightpass," Bastien said. "They'll fund the drift or they'll fund someone else's hole. I'd like it to be ours."

"You want the shelf surveyed from the top," Ansel said.

"I want depth, so I know how much rock is over my roof. I want the currents, because if we're pumping out of an under-shelf drift I need to know where the outfall goes and whether it comes back. And I want to know whether that plate is whole or whether the vent's put holes in it that I can't see from here." Bastien tapped the inked outline of the shelf. "I can't send a crew down to tap the face of something that's under the sea. I can send you over the top of it in a boat."

Dorrit Vash, at the end of the bench, made a noise in her throat like a cart going over a stone. "Over the top in my boat."

"At water rate," Bastien said. "Survey days at double shore rate for the surveyor, a skipper's share for you, deck wages for two hands. Eight days, spread across the rest of Wetwake as the weather allows. And a bonus at the end if the answer lets me cut."

"And if the answer doesn't let you cut?"

"Then the answer stops me drowning eighteen people," Bastien said, "and the bonus is smaller, and I'll still pay it."

That, more than the money, was why Ansel listened.

---

He did not say yes at the bench. He took the plan home.

Maren read it at the table by the green window with her chin in her hands while he stood behind her and explained things she already understood. She had kept the bell for nine years. She knew the mine by sound and by count better than most of the crews. She knew what *under-shelf drift* meant in the same way she knew what *double shift* meant: it meant somebody's tag would be on the *in* side for longer.

"Eight days," she said.

"Eight days on the water. Spread out. I'd be home every night."

"In a boat over the shelf."

"In Dorrit's boat. Dorrit's been running ore lighters to Driftfall Piers for fifteen years and never lost a sack."

"Dorrit's never had to bring the lighter back with a surveyor in it who thinks rotten staging will hold him." She turned the plan sideways and looked at the shelf. "What does double shore rate and a bonus come to?"

He told her.

She was quiet. It came to the second room. It came to the second room with a proper floor, and a shutter for the window, and something left over in the plank fund for once. It came to the thing they had been building toward with pegs and rest days for two years, done in a season.

"Do you want to do it?" she asked.

"I want the room," he said. "I don't especially want the boat."

"That isn't an answer."

"It's an honest one. I'd rather tap faces. I know what stone does. Water's worse at telling you." He sat down across from her. "But I know the shelf a little. I know the set. Every float I've ever lost off the point went west. It's not a mystery current, it's just the coast pulling. If I'm careful and Dorrit's careful, it's eight days of measuring and a lot of being cold."

"And if it isn't careful."

"Then I float," he said. "You know I float."

She looked at him for a long time. "Floating isn't the same as coming home."

He did not have an answer to that. He reached across the table and turned the plan the right way up again, and after a while she put her hand over his, and that was how they decided: not with a promise, but with the plan between them and her hand on his, both of them knowing what the other was afraid of.

"Eight days," she said. "Home every night. And you knock on the door when you come up the path, so I know it's you and not Bastien coming to tell me something."

"Two and a rise."

"Two and a rise," Maren said.

---

He began on the shore, because you always begin with what you can stand on.

For the next nine days, between his shifts underground, Ansel worked the edge of the land. He took sightings from the point with his little brass sighting-tube and set three marks he could see from the water: the headframe of the mine, a tall rib of an old hull on the shingle that the settlement used as a drying post, and, inland and much higher, the steam of Sprout Vent over its many-mouthed shoulder. When the vent was smoking he could fix a position off the point to within a boat's length. When it was not, or when fog lay on the water, he could not, and he wrote that down too, because a survey that does not record what it cannot see is a lie in a good binding.

He opened a new ledger for the work, a heavy bound book with waxed covers and numbered pages, and wrote on the first page in his small square hand: *Saltjaw shelf survey. Depth, set, plate. For B. Crale. Begun Wetwake.* Underneath, in smaller letters, the rule he had learned from the old surveyor who had trained him, the one he had never once broken: *Write what you measured. Write what you guessed. Never let the second look like the first.*

From the end of the ore jetty he took soundings with the lead: a long marked line with a lead weight on the end, its base hollowed and packed with tallow so that it came up carrying a smear of whatever it had touched. Sand. Shell grit. Green-stained rock dust, which made his chest tighten with pleasure, because green on the tallow meant the verdigris plate was right there under the water, close enough to touch.

He threw drift floats off the jetty, painted wooden pegs with a blue band, and timed them with a sand-glass as they moved off. They did what they always did. They idled a little in the slack under the point and then the coast took them, steady and patient, westward along the shelf toward the long line of Sorel's southern shore and the far, unseen coast of Wabet beyond it. Saltjaw people called it the Shelf Set. Fishermen cursed it. Children threw messages into it on festival days and never heard of them again. It was as ordinary as the tide.

On the ninth evening he brought the ledger home and showed Maren the tallow smears pressed between waxed sheets, the columns of soundings, the drawn fan of survey lines running out from the point to the shelf edge like the ribs of a hand.

"It looks safe," she said, looking at the fan.

"It looks like a plan," Ansel said. "Tomorrow we find out what it looks like from on top."
