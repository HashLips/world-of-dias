# The Corner That Forgot Its Edges

## Premise

Pulled along the span, the three understand the echo is not taking them into Sorel. The cold corner forgets its edges. They try to get away. They are still in F432, and the corner still does not open.

## Story

The blind side of the Long Gate was still a bridge. Rill kept telling herself that, because the alternative was the story adults used to end conversations: you stare at nothing until nothing stares back and takes you to the abyss. She was not staring. She was being walked, which was less mystical and worse. Boards under her boots. Bolt heads. A gull or something like a gull complaining in ordinary air. Hearthvale's smoke still visible if she wrenched her neck, a town-weather they were not being allowed to choose. Sorel remained a country beside them. It did not become a threshold that ate.

The Lean Echo had stopped pretending to lead from ahead. It was around them the way a net is around fish before it is drawn, colors close, their colors, so that looking at it felt like looking at a memory of their own hands. The notch — the corner — had forgotten it was supposed to be small. Edges failed a hand's breadth from Rill's cheek. Highlights on the rail vanished when the corner passed over them and returned, sulky, when it moved on. No horizon lived inside it. No road. No voice explaining a sequence of steps, a word, a toll, a captain. If the echo knew a way, it kept the knowledge the way a locked mouth keeps a name. The corner was a sign that the knowledge might be real. Signs are not crossings. Rill clung to the difference until her fingers hurt on Harl's sleeve.

"We're not going to Sorel," she said, loud enough for the wind. "Joss. Look at the smoke. It's over there. We're not walking toward it. Turn."

Joss tried. She would give him that in every retelling, even the ones that did not flatter her. He wrenched his shoulder north, toward Nauw, toward the niche, toward the watcher who had already gone for help they could not see. The satchel strap sawed his neck. The gray stone inside might as well have been hooked into the echo's seam. His boots did turn. His hands did not. They stayed half-curled in the shape of holding, though he had dropped the strap, though he was shouting at his own fingers.

"I don't want it," he said. "I don't want the wonderful thing. I want the shop. I want down. Down."

The echo flinched at the word the way the stone, in Pim's care, had obeyed it. For a heartbeat gray rushed through the stolen colors, a memory of linen. Then the notch widened in refusal, and the flinch became a grip. Not teeth. Weather. A shell of amber and blue and chalk beginning to film over Joss's wrists like ice that had learned a palette.

Harl got his chalk out, absurd, magnificent, and said aloud, to the stick and the span and anyone listening, "We are trying to go back. We stole it. We are trying to put it down." The chalk marked the rail in one ugly true line. The line did not open anything. It witnessed. Rill loved him for the waste of it.

She threw her weight toward the north walkway. Her boot slid on ordinary damp. The corner slid with her, not chasing like a beast with legs, enclosing like a room deciding to be smaller. She saw, in the last clear second, a pier number painted by some Nauw clerk who had never intended to save a child: black on stone, F-nothing, just a count of boards. F432's bureaucracy, blessedly dull. Not Frequency Zero. Not a null dock. A number that meant this plank, this realm, this unfinished mistake.

"It doesn't have a door," she shouted, for Joss, for herself, for the echo if it could be disappointed. "You never showed a door. You only walked us until we couldn't tell the road from you."

The Lean Echo did not argue. Argument would have been a kind of explanation. It drew the colors tight, and the corner forgot a little more edge, and the three stopped being people who were about to turn around and became people the cold was fitting into a pocket that was still, terribly, on a bridge in the world they already knew.
