# The Fruit Still on the Tree

## Premise

At dawn one smaller Vitrin is still on the tree, outside the count of ten. Dain orders it flown out as a secret. Lark does not take it. The empty hull is the refusal.

## Story

Dawn came up Softfall-thin, bark-sweet, indecently calm.

They had not made it back to the plane before the light. Dain was a difficulty propped against a stump, cuff ruined, voice still arriving late, hands unreliable. Sometimes a knee appeared, filthy, and he stared at it — Lark assumed he stared — with a hope that died when the knee did. Sella had gone inside and barred the door with the practical bar, not a charm. Hob's lamp had been blown out. The thrown stew glittered in the grass like a snail-path and then was only wet.

The tree kept its own secret badly. High on the young wood, one fruit the auction had not counted clung, smaller, greener in the glass, a late set. It rang when a breeze pushed it against a twig. About ten had been the yield a businessman could say aloud. The tree had not signed his sentence.

"That one," Dain said. The words landed to the left. "You will climb. You will put it in the case. There is a buyer who does not attend shows. Private pad, not the shed, not the bar. You fly it out before this lane fills with her nephew's mouth. I am owed a remainder. The remainder is right there."

"You are owed a physician or a priest or a nap," Lark said. "You are not owed her last fruit."

"I paid for the yield."

"You paid for what she cut and you stole the keeping of it. Look at you. You want a second show in a locked room. I won't be the hull."

He tried to stand into a threat. The threat had no face and poor feet. He got three steps and met a root with a body the root could feel better than she could see. The sound he made was not powerful. It was the sound of a man whose invisibility had stopped being a trick and become a handicap with opinions. Moth, curse the child, was at the hedge again. Lark pointed back toward the stakes until the blanket retreated. Yarrow would be awake by now. Engines at night did not stay a secret in a country with so few of them.

Sella came out with the bar still in her hand, ear-rim absent, the rest of her sharply drawn by morning. She looked at the fruit and at Lark. She did not beg. Begging would have made the pilot a judge. She only waited, which was heavier, apron hem still wrong, eyes entirely present.

Lark walked to the tree. She could have had the fruit in a minute. She knew how to climb. Her hands knew crates and branches both. The Vitrin tapped her knuckle, cold, gorgeous, a smaller sibling of the bowl in the shed. Up close the seeds were lenses full of sky. She thought of Hess's box and Ilen's slate and a room cheering while flour failed. She took her hand back without the fruit.

"It stays," she said. "I'm not flying it as a quiet. If you want it so badly, ask her in a voice that comes from your own mouth, in daylight, where Yarrow can hear the price. I won't hide the tree in a hull again."

Dain called her a hire, a coward, a woman who had already done the crime and wanted the innocence of the last mile. The insults arrived late and winded. Some of them were fair. She let the fair ones hit. She got him to the plane because leaving him in Sella's orchard was another kind of weapon, and Sella had refused weapons. He was cargo she hated. The case was empty. She showed Sella the empty, from a distance, lid up, no glass in it. Sella nodded once. That was the whole contract.

The Late Bruise lifted in the soft light with a hull that knocked because it was underloaded. Wagons on a far track paused. Oxen did not look up. A machine left. A fruit stayed. Lark did not feel clean. She felt aimed, finally, at a room that would make the aim expensive. Below, the lent strip's grass took another pair of scars. The last Vitrin turned once on its twig and kept the morning in its rind, unseen by any auction, refusing to become a secret just because a plane had asked.
