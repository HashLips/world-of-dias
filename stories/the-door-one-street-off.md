# The Door One Street Off

## Premise

Rill Ostmark leaves Veloria’s market loop by one street and finds the old door adults call a story. Inside Farstock, the wonder is specific, and the stone at the back of the counter is already changing color.

## Story

The market loop was doing what it always did at midmorning: shouting prices as if volume could improve a pear. Rill Ostmark had a message slip for a basin cooper and no intention of being late, until Joss Merrow caught her sleeve at the gutter where the bakery smell thinned.

"One street," he said. "Harl says it isn't there. I say the string in the stall-boy's pocket is the length of it."

Harl Fenlow held up a waxed cord, offended that it existed. "A man sold me this for a half-mark and told me it goes slack at a door. If it goes slack at a wall I am getting my half-mark."

They took the street anyway. The loop's noise turned a corner and became footsteps. Stone here was older, wet in the seam, flower pots on two sills and none of the guild banners that made the loop feel official. No sign. An old door in a frame that had been replaced around it, iron straps, a step worn down on the left as if hesitation had a favorite foot.

A bell the size of a thumb gave one small ring as Rill's boot found the step. She looked up. Draft, she decided, because the alternative was that the street had noticed her.

Joss knocked the way he did everything, too soon. The door stuck, then gave, and the room beyond was narrow and full of things that had not been arranged to impress a guild inspector. Light came from a high slit and from a paper whale of a lantern hung where you had to lift your chin, festival work folded small, not a living sky. A bunch of peel-sweet herb dried by the latch and made the air honest. A bowl of ring-fair tokens sat where money would have sat in a worse shop, years stamped on them that nobody was spending. Candy in gold paper. A palm lens on a nail. Pencils in a jar, orange, already bending a little though no one in the room was arguing yet.

Pim Farstock looked up from the counter without performing surprise. Dust in the lines of his hands. A mended cuff. "If you are here for the guild," he said, "it does not visit, and I do not miss it."

"We're here because he bought a string," Rill said, and nodded at Harl, who had gone red because the cord in his fist had indeed gone slack.

"Then the string did its work," Pim said. "It knows one street. Do not ask it for a second life."

Joss was already moving, not touching, which for him was restraint. A spoon on a hook hummed faintly when he leaned toward the back kettle. A marble on the sill was clear. A nail with a square head lay on a cloth square as if iron could rest. None of it thundered. All of it was more interesting than the loop's shout, and Rill hated how quickly her errand loosened in her pocket.

At the back of the counter, on undyed linen, sat a stone the size of a palm, river-gray.

Joss put his hand out before Pim spoke, not quite touching. The gray warmed. Amber went through it like gilding hour poured into a cup. The color of the basin when the day decided to be kind. Joss made a sound he would have denied later.

"That," he said. "How much for that."

Pim's hand came down, not a slap, a lid. The amber retreated a shade and did not vanish. A red-brown ribbon hung on the hook above the linen, tied where a price tag was not.

"You may look," Pim said. "Looking is not a purchase. And that one is not priced, because I am not selling it."

Outside, the loop raised its voice again, selling fruit to people who had never left the street. Inside, the stone held a last thread of amber, as if it had opinions about being interrupted. Rill heard the little bell settle on its nail. She did not believe in drafts anymore, and she did not yet believe she would come back after dark. Both of those facts were about to cost her.
