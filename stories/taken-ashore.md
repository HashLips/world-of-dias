# Taken Ashore

## Premise

Two Aumeri, Nessa Orvay and Tavi Corran, take Ansel off the boom, bring him through it on the count, and lift him up the cliff in a counterweighted cradle. They warm him, feed him, and question him slowly in an old coastal speech. They are clever in specific, earned ways. They are not all-knowing. They owe him something about his boat, and Nessa says so.

## Story

The boat was long and narrow and rode almost without sound.

Ansel watched it come from where he lay over the timber, too cold to do anything else. It was built of thin planks laid edge to edge, not overlapped as Driftfall boats were, and the seams between them were stitched with something dark, cord or root, in rows of tiny even stitches like a sail-maker's. There were two people in it. A young one at the oars, broad and heavy-shouldered, whose bare forearms had the grain and dull sheen of worked iron: Kharad, then, or something very like Kharad. And an old woman in the stern with a steering paddle across her knees and a coat of undyed wool, white-haired, her face weathered to the brown of a ship's rail.

They did not hurry. They did not call out. When they were thirty strokes off, the young one stopped rowing and they both simply sat and looked at him, the old woman with her head a little on one side, for what felt to Ansel like a very long time.

He understood after a while that they were watching the water as well as him. The old woman's lips were moving. Her fingers tapped once on the paddle-shaft, then again, then again. She was counting.

*Pull,* went the sea, and the line of timbers lifted and knocked. *Rest.*

On the rest, the old woman said a word he did not know, and the young one pulled three strokes, hard, and the narrow boat slid in alongside the timber Ansel was clinging to, exactly as the line settled, and stopped dead with its bow a hand's width from the wood.

"Can you let go?" the old woman said.

It took him a moment to understand her. The words were words he knew, but they were shaped oddly, old-fashioned, with the vowels long and the ends of them dropped, the way the oldest people at the Salt Ledger talked when they told stories about Driftfall before the piers.

"I don't know," he said. His voice came out as a croak.

"Then don't. Tavi."

The young one, Tavi, leaned out and slid a broad sling of netting under Ansel's chest and arms, as easily as if he had done it a hundred times, and lifted. Ansel came up off the timber and out of the water and over the gunwale and was set down in the bottom of the boat on a heap of wool, and the whole of it took perhaps three breaths. He weighed almost nothing. Tavi made a small sound of surprise at that and looked at the old woman.

"Light," Tavi said.

"Hollow," said the old woman. "Look at the feathers. Look how high he was riding. That's why the water brought him and not the others." She looked at Ansel directly for the first time, and her eyes were pale grey and very steady. "You'll be cold for a day. You'll be stupid for two. Don't try to be clever before then. I'm Nessa. This is Tavi. You're on the water of Aumere, and we're taking you in."

She tapped the paddle, counting. On the next rest Tavi turned the boat, and they went back toward the cliff.

They did not go round the line of timbers. They went through it. There was a gap, Ansel saw, where two timbers were joined not by lashings but by a long loop of rope that let them swing apart, and Nessa took them into it on a rest, and Tavi pulled hard, and the next pull of the current swung the timbers closed again behind them with a soft knock. He had the impression, lying in the bottom of the boat, of having gone through a door that would have broken anyone who did not know when to knock.

---

At the foot of the cliff there was a cleft, and in the cleft a stone landing, and above the landing a cradle.

That was the word he found for it later. It was a wooden platform hung from four ropes that ran up the cliff to a frame at the top of the first terrace, and from the frame other ropes ran down the far side of the cliff to a basket of stones. When Tavi lifted Ansel onto the platform and stepped onto it with him, and Nessa called up, someone at the top let a little water out of a trough into the basket of stones, and the stones grew heavier, and the cradle rose. Slowly. Smoothly. Without anyone hauling on anything. Ansel lay on his back and watched the cliff go by, the lichen and the cut edges of the terraces, and in his stupid cold state the thing that made him want to weep was not the rescue. It was the engineering. Someone had thought about this very carefully for a very long time.

There were faces at the top. Six or seven people, of several kinds: humans, two more of Tavi's iron-grained build, one tall shape whose face caught the blue light in planes and colours the way Choirglass faces did. They looked at him the way Nessa had: long and quiet, without fear and without much welcome either. Nobody touched him except to help. Nobody asked him anything.

They carried him into a low stone house set back from the terrace edge, roofed with turf, with walls so thick that the doorway was a short tunnel. Inside it was warm. Stones the size of loaves sat in a pit in the middle of the floor, glowing faintly from a fire beneath, and the warmth came off them steadily and filled the room. They laid him on a bench near the pit and wrapped him in wool, and Nessa put a bowl in his hands and held his hands around it until he could hold it himself.

It was broth. It tasted of shellfish and some green bitter leaf he did not know and, faintly, of smoke. He drank it the only way he could, tipping his head back, and nobody stared at that either.

---

They questioned him that evening, when he had stopped shaking.

Nessa did it, with Tavi sitting on the floor by the stone pit and two or three others in the doorway. She asked one thing at a time and waited for the whole answer before she asked the next, and when he did not understand a word she said it a different way instead of louder.

"Where is your boat from?"

"Saltjaw. The mine at Saltjaw. On the coast below Sprout Vent."

She looked at Tavi. Tavi said, "The smoke," and Nessa nodded slowly.

"The mountain with many mouths," she said. "We see its smoke on clear days, from the high terrace. We did not know the mine had a name. We have watched the boats come and go from that point for a long time. We call it the Long Shore."

"Sorel," Ansel said. "It's Sorel. Driftfall, in Sorel."

She repeated the names carefully, as if putting them on a shelf. "Driftfall. Sorel." She did not seem to know them. She asked him what a mine wanted with the sea, and he told her about the seam and the shelf and Bastien's drift, and she listened with her hands folded, and when he had finished she said, "So you were measuring."

"Yes."

"Measuring the water. And the stone under it."

"Yes."

She was quiet for a while. "What is a VEL mark?" she asked, because he had said it, explaining the bonus, and Ansel stared at her and then laughed, a small cracked laugh that hurt his chest. They did not know. They had cradles and stitched boats and doors in the sea that opened on the count, and they had never heard of money from Veloria.

"It's what people pay with," he said. "On the Long Shore. Everywhere."

"Ah," said Nessa, and put that on a shelf too.

Then he asked the question he had been holding since the boom. "There were three others with me. Dorrit, Tamsy, Brann. Dorrit went down. Tamsy and Brann were on a timber. A timber like the ones out there." He made himself say it. "Did you see them?"

Nessa looked at him for a long moment. In the doorway, someone moved.

"We saw one boat, in the fog," she said. "We saw it break. We saw two people on wood and one under the water and one riding high. The water brought you. The wood went the other way, toward your shelf." She held his eyes. "We will speak about your boat. Tomorrow, when you are warm enough to hear it properly. It is owed to you. I won't pretend it isn't."

Then she took the empty bowl out of his hands and told him to sleep, and to his own surprise, on a bench by a pit of warm stones on an island that was not on any chart, with people in the doorway who had not decided what to do with him, he did.

## Notes

Chapter 8 of **The Island Saga**. Introduces [Nessa Orvay](../inhabitants/nessa-orvay.md), [Tavi Corran](../inhabitants/tavi-corran.md), and the [Aumeri](../inhabitants/aumeri.md) as a mixed community of known F432 kinds (human, Kharad-like, Choirglass-like) rather than a new race. Their intelligence is shown as observation, timing, counterweight engineering, stitched-plank boatbuilding, and careful questioning. Their ignorance is shown too: they do not know the words Sorel, Driftfall, or VEL mark, though they have watched Sprout Vent's smoke for generations and call the mainland the Long Shore.

Nessa counts the current to pass the boom. That counting becomes the [Aumeri Tide-Count](../cultures/aumeri-tide-count.md) in chapter 10. Nessa names a debt about the boat and defers it one night; it is paid in chapter 9.
