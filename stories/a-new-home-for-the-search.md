# A New Home for the Search

## Premise

Maren leaves Saltjaw with the plank fund and a notebook. She goes overland: north to Driftfall, across Sorel's interior to Hearthvale, through Westfold Toll, south along the border trails, and down to the Lumira coast. On the way she stops grieving the way a wife grieves and starts asking questions the way a bell-keeper counts. At Sallow Strand she reads the original letter for the first time.

## Story

She left on the first dry morning after the boat came back, with the plank fund sewn into the lining of her coat and Ansel's spare cap folded in her pack, though it would not fit her and she knew it.

The night before, she took the survey ledger back up the slope to Bastien Crale's hut and put it on his table. He looked at it and then at her.

"Keep it," he said.

"It's yours. It's the mine's. Your backers will want it."

"My backers can want." Bastien turned the book over in his rope-burned hands. "All right. I'll hold it. Nobody takes it off this shelf but me, and nobody reads it but me, and if you send for it, it comes to you. That's a promise, Maren, not a kindness. I owe him the drift."

At dawn Brann walked her to the top of the coast path. Tamsy came too, carrying nothing, which for Tamsy meant she had something to say and could not find the way into it. At the place where the path turned north and the jetty went out of sight, Tamsy finally said, "If anyone out there asks about the margins—the counting—tell them I keep a slate. Tell them to write to me. Not to the mine. To me."

"I will."

"And come back."

Maren did not promise that. She knocked two stones from the path wall together, once and twice, and whistled, cracked as always, the rise breaking halfway. Brann's face did something complicated. Then she turned north and did not look back, because she had learned from the bell that if you look back at the thing you're leaving you ring the wrong stroke.

---

Driftfall Piers took a day to reach and an afternoon to get through. She asked at the pier stalls for carts going inland and found a salvage wagon taking rope and pitch to the yards, and rode with it as far as the crossroads where the Hearthvale roads began. That night she slept in a cart-shed among sacks of hemp and wrote the first page of a notebook she had bought at the pier for two VEL marks she could not spare.

At the top of the page she wrote: *What I know. What I have been told. Never let the second look like the first.* It was Ansel's rule from the ledger, turned to her own use. Under it she began two columns.

In the first column she wrote: *Boat struck, SW rib, fifth day, fog. Dorrit drowned. Brann and Tamsy found on the timber, Tamsy saw blue. Ansel floated. Letter written, put in the canister, found in Wabet on the twelfth day.*

In the second column she wrote: *Painted copies on the roads. Door. "The way through." Gold. Fruit-runner at the Salt Ledger says crossing. Houses taking notice (pier-boy).*

She looked at the two columns for a long time. The first was short. The second was getting longer every day, and she had not yet seen one word Ansel had written.

---

The road across Sorel's interior to Hearthvale was eight days by cart and foot, through yard country and salvage camps and long stretches of nothing where the Freebands' signs on the posts were the only law. She had come this way nine years ago in the other direction, a kiln family's daughter with burns on her forearms and a cracked whistle, going east to the sea because Hearthvale's kilns had too many daughters. Then she had thought the road was long. Now it seemed long for a different reason.

She did not go to her family's kiln at Secondfire. She thought about it for a whole day on the cart and decided that she could not stand at her mother's door and say *Ansel's gone* and then say *but I don't believe it* and watch her mother's face decide what to think. She sent a note by a Hearthvale boy instead, and a VEL mark for the trouble, and went on.

What she did instead, all the way across Sorel, was ask.

At every cart-stop and every waystation she asked whether anyone had seen a painted copy of a sea-letter from the Sorel coast, and what it looked like, and where they had seen it, and who had brought it. Most people had not. Some had. She wrote down everything they told her in the second column, exactly as they told it, with the place and the day. A carter at a Hearthvale crossroads had seen one with a shore and no door. A tinker had seen one with a door and no shore. A woman at a well had heard that the letter was written by a Driftfall man who had found a crossing to another band and was waiting there for his wife to follow him, and Maren wrote that down too, with a steady hand, and then had to stop writing for a while.

By the time she reached Westfold Toll she had eleven accounts of the copies. No two agreed. But when she laid them out in the notebook by place and by day, she saw what the House archivist in Veloria had seen with the copies themselves, though she did not know that anyone else had seen it: the further from the coast, the more there was. Doors appeared once the copies reached the orchard waystations. Gold appeared on the Fruit Road. *The way through* appeared a waystation after the gold, and after that it was everywhere.

"Lies grow as they go," she said aloud, to no one, at the toll queue. "Like a bad plate. The crack's small at the bottom."

---

Westfold Toll wanted marks, and she paid them, and the plank fund got lighter. The clerk at the booth looked at her notebook while she counted out the VEL and said, not unkindly, "You're the bell-keeper. From the letter."

She stared at him.

"Someone came through a few weeks ago saying the wife would come looking," the clerk said. "There've been three women through claiming to be you since. One of them had a painted copy and wanted the Houses to pay her for it." He stamped her slip. "You're the first one who's paid the toll without arguing. And you've got salt in your hair. Go on through."

From the toll she went south and west, on foot now, along the border trails where Wabet's green edges came down toward Sorel's dry ones, past the last orchards of Verdant Reach and down into dune country. A salt-cart going the same way let her walk beside it for two days. The steward driving it talked about the Keepers and the Silence Law, and told her to walk softly once she came within sight of the sea, and said, when Maren asked about the letter, "I carried a charcoal copy of it out of Sallow Strand myself, in Wetwake. I'm sorry for it. It wasn't mine to carry." Maren wrote that down too. It was the first account on the whole road that went the other way: not *it says*, but *I was wrong*.

---

She came down the last dune into Sallow Strand at the end of Brightpass, in the evening, with the wind off the sea and Glasswake Rock black against the west. The hamlet was smaller than she had pictured: twenty low timber houses with grass on their roofs in the lee of the last dune, a cookhouse, drying racks, and at the top of the strand a long timber building with deep eaves, one wide window facing the sea, and a door painted the faded blue of old sea-glass.

A figure was standing side-on at the top of the dune path, so thin against the sky that Maren did not see her at first. Then the figure turned, and was a Lineborn woman with ink-dark crosshatching at her edges and a ring of keys at her belt, looking at Maren the way the toll clerk had looked at her, only more carefully.

"Maren Kestry," the woman said. It was not a question.

"How did you—"

"You walk like someone who's looked at a lot of copies and is about to see the real thing." Odile Brask came down two steps and held out her hand. "And you're the only one who hasn't asked me where the door is."

---

Odile did not give her the fair record. She took Maren into the Letter House, past the long table and the people sitting quietly in the evening light, past the glass and the copy under it, and up a short ladder to the high shelf, and took down a plain wooden box, and opened it.

Maren knew the leaf before she touched it. She knew the waxed survey paper the mine bought by the bundle from Claimscar. She knew the fold in three and then in half, the way Ansel folded everything, square and tight. She knew the smear of red wax over the fold, and the hooked crescent pressed into it, the shape of the tip of his beak. And she knew the hand on the outside fold, small and square and careful even with the pencil shaking: *Maren Kestry. Bell-keeper. Saltjaw Mine. Driftfall. Sorel. Please.*

Odile had slit the leaf beside the seal, not through it. The crescent was whole.

Maren sat down on the floor of the Letter House with her back against the shelves, and unfolded it, and read.

She read it once fast, the way you read a letter when you are afraid of what is at the end. Then she read it slowly. Then she turned it over and looked at the drawing for a long time: the point, the headframe, the many-mouthed sign for Sprout Vent, the fan of survey ribs, the cross on the southwest rib marked *struck—not rock*, the arrows, the blue bank smeared with his thumb, and at the bottom, small and black and level, the cliff with its steps. *Seen once, at dusk. No shore here on any chart.*

Then she turned it back and read the last lines a third time. *Maren. I am not finished. Do not let anyone tell you I am finished because it is tidy.* And under the name, the two dots and the rising stroke.

She did not cry. She had expected to. Instead she found that she was running a finger down the margin, along the columns of strokes and dots and dashes, the way she ran a finger down the tag board at the end of a shift, counting who was in and who was out.

"He counted all night," she said. "To stay awake."

"Yes," said Odile.

"These aren't where he is. He says so. *They are what the water did.*" Maren looked up. "Tamsy keeps a slate. She counts the current at the shelf. She sent me with a column of her own." She took the notebook out of her coat and opened it to the back page, where she had copied Tamsy's slate before she left. Sixes and fives. "Odile. I need a room. I need a table. And I need to stay."

Odile looked at her for a long moment. Then she nodded at the long table under the window, where the letter-keepers were packing up for the night.

"There's a room behind the cookhouse," she said. "The table's that one. Nobody copies the margins. Nobody carries them out. Those are the rules."

"Good," said Maren. "Those are my rules too."
