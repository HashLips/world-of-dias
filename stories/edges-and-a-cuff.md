# Edges and a Cuff

## Premise

Sella tastes, and only her edges fade. She stays angry and visible, throws out the pot, and catches Dain by a cuff the stew has marked. He is the one the fruit keeps.

## Story

She tasted because it was her pot and she would not be bullied by a surface.

One sip. The cold sweetness under the onion. Lark saw the change the way you see a hem drag through water: not the woman removed, the outline edited. The rim of Sella's left ear went out, a crescent of absence. The shadow-hem of the apron thinned until the cloth seemed to end early and did not. The edge of her left hand, the one on the ladle, faded to a drawing the lamp could not commit to. The rest of her stayed rudely, fully there. Eyes. Mouth. The knot of hair. The stain of ordinary peel on the fingers that had not faded. She was in the room. She was angry in a way invisibility could not subcontract.

"Hob," she said, voice entire, "if you drop that lamp I will make you hoe in the dark for a week."

Hob did not drop it. He was crying without leaving. Sixteen and useful.

Dain lunged, or the air where he was lunged. He wanted the bowl, the evidence, the reversal. His sleeve — he had worn a sleeve, a coat that had been flickering since the shed — brushed the pot. Stew marked the cuff. Cloth remained cloth. That was the mercy Sella used. She put down the ladle and grabbed. The faded outline of her left hand closed on wet fabric and held. He was heavier than a ghost and less coherent than a man. Pulling away tore nothing she could see except his balance. The voice yelped from a step to the left. The cuff stayed in her fist. A button bit her palm. Real.

"I see you enough," she said. "Don't flatter the fruit. It took your face and it took your manners and it left me the part I can hang on a nail."

"Make it stop," he said. Late, offset, furious. "You mixed it. Say the stop."

"I mixed a tree," she said. "I did not mix a leash. The bird came back. You are not a bird, and you put a kept piece into a bowl that was not yours. I don't know why it likes you better than it likes me. If I knew, I would not sell it to a shed."

She was shaking. The shake did not erase her. Lark came in at last and got the other side of the cuff, not to save his dignity, to keep him from dragging Sella into the hearth. Between them he was a struggle with one visible point: wool, stew-stained, a man's wrist implied inside it like a word you could not read. His other hand passed through the back of a chair and then, horribly, did not, and he gasped from the wrong place because the inconsistency hurt.

Sella looked at the pot. She would not feed Hob. She would not feed the stranger in the doorway. She would not feed the field with a punishment. She carried the iron to the door and threw the stew into the grass in a steaming fan, pale rim and onion and the last glitter of glass-sweet. Not into a mouth. Into the ground, where it could be a mess and not a lesson. The smell rose, sweet and wrong, and then was only night air.

"You are not erased," Lark said, to Sella, stupid with relief, and Sella gave her the look that sentence deserved.

"I was never going to be," she said. "He wanted a matching pair. He got a cuff. Get him off my boards. And if you fly anything else of mine out of this lane in a hull, I will know the sound of your machine, and I will be standing where the wheels want to be."

The ear stayed half-gone. The apron hem stayed thin. She stood in the doorway, visible, apron dripping a little, nephew behind her with the lamp, and watched them haul a worse silence toward the trees. Dain did not become a cautionary vapor. He suffered, present and unpresent, and the suffering had a sound, and the sound could not find his mouth.
