# Building the Spectrum Frame

## Premise

Orrin and Tam finish the Spectrum Frame—an instrument that graphs frequency-band signatures from F432 home, not a travel door—while Noro mocks the unfinished glass and leaves.

## Story

The Frame rose like a window that had decided to stand without a wall. Plain job: lattice, glass, etched ticks, heel plates on floor hum. When powered, it would draw band signatures the way a spectrum chart draws peaks—looking out from F432, refusing to become a ferry.

Mio Chalkrule delivered a folding rule with crooked ticks that still measured true—affectionate imprecision, craft honesty. Tam fed filament until his knuckles shone. Orrin apologized aloud to every cracked pane before scrap, oil-smell dark, the Row rite that kept tools from becoming enemies. Ren Oilwick's midnight lamp burned slow enough for gossip: which slits leaked, which stamps had hesitated that week. Cylinder tea went cold and was refilled because argument pauses required honest lines.

Noro Halfmeasure leaned in the alcove mouth, rival dial at his belt, pride like a second apron. "Unfinished instruments are unfinished character."

"Then stay and finish with us," Orrin said. "Colleague."

Noro did not stay. Pride is a clearance level too. He left a silence shaped like a dare, and Tam muttered something unprintable about ceremonial haircuts.

On the second night the glass refused to hold a clean F432 middle line until Orrin apologized to a cracked pane he had already scrap-bound. Tam rolled his eyes and still held the filament steady. "If the Frame needs manners," Tam said, "it is already more person than Noro."

Mio returned once to check the etched ticks and left without praise, which on the Row meant the work was acceptable. Ren's lamp smoked.

By the third night the lattice held: shoulder-high, waxed cord, etched measure-marks, heel plates tasting floor hum. Silk Scrap stole a filament end for a kite and swore it flew in ticks; Tam pretended not to see. When Orrin first powered the glass, F432 drew a steady middle line—as expected as breath. Faint ghost traces waited above and below that line: the quiet bands the Supplemental Leaf had named, not yet proven, already shy as Softfruit jokes before a stamp.

Orrin laughed once, short, almost scared. He bowed to the glass the way he bowed to lamps. "Hello," he told the ghosts. "We're not here to board you. We're here to admit you exist." Window, not door. Graph, not ticket.

Outside, graph-dust finches answered the Row morning bell. Inside, the empty chalk footprint on the floorboards finally had a body. The alcove smelled of oil, mint, and the particular hope of people who have not yet learned what their hope can invite.
