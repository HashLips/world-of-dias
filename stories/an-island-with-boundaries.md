# An Island with Boundaries

## Premise

On the high terrace Nessa tells Ansel what struck his boat: the island's own turning boom, carried across his line by the current in fog. She tells him why the Aumeri refuse contact with the Long Shore, partly from history and partly from conviction. Tavi, who built the boom's new stone weights, says the refusal has just cost a life and that silence does not make it free.

## Story

The high terrace was the top of the island, and from it Ansel could see almost nothing.

The blue lay all around, a soft wall a few hundred strokes out, bright and shadowless. Inside it, Aumere spread out below him like a hand laid flat: the terraces stepping down to the cliff on the seaward side, green with beans and some low silver-leaved crop he did not know; the turf roofs of perhaps fifty houses tucked into the lee of a long spine of rock; stone cisterns cut into the hillside with channels running between them; a little harbour in the cleft where the narrow boats were drawn up on stone. And beyond the cliff, curving away on both sides into the blue, the dark line of the boom.

It was a small place. He could have walked around it in a morning.

"On clear days," Nessa said, "the blue goes thin and you can see the smoke of your mountain from here. Not often. A few days a year." She sat down on a low wall at the terrace edge, slowly, because her knees were old. "Sit. I said I would tell you about your boat, and I don't like owing."

He sat. Tavi stood a little apart, arms folded, his iron-grained face giving away nothing.

"The line of timbers is ours," Nessa said. "We call it the Turning Boom. It goes all the way round the quiet water, outside the island, where the count runs strongest. Every timber is chained or roped back to the island's own stone, to the shelf of rock at the island's root that runs out under the quiet water. There's no sea bottom out there to tie to; your lead would never find one. Every few timbers there is a weight, a stone in a rope cradle, to keep it low. It has been there, in one form or another, since before my grandmother's grandmother."

"What is it for?"

"Turning boats. Most boats never come near. The blue and the quiet frighten them; the count confuses their soundings; they go home and say the sea is strange off the shelf. A few come closer. When they reach the boom, they touch it, and it turns them. It is built to give a little and push back, soft, like a hand on a chest. They bump along it and slide off and drift back out on the next pull, and they think they have struck a reef or a log raft, and they go home." Nessa looked at him steadily. "It is not built to break anything."

"It broke The Patient Lead."

"Yes."

Tavi spoke for the first time. His voice was low, like stone being dragged. "It was my weight," he said. "Last Hushlean a storm parted the boom on the south side and took three cradles. I rebuilt them. I made the stones heavier so the timbers would ride lower and not part again. I didn't think about a boat coming over the boom at a joint, in fog, on a strong pull, with the count carrying it sideways. Nobody did. It came down on the stone." He looked at Ansel. "Your skipper. The heavy one. She went down because of something I made."

Ansel found he could not say anything at all. He looked down at the dark line of the boom in the quiet water and saw, at last, all of it: the deep knock up through the keel, the seam opening, the long roped timber that Tamsy had climbed onto. It had not been wreckage from The Patient Lead. It had been a piece of this, torn loose.

"Tamsy and Brann were on a timber," he said, finally. "One of yours."

"One of the south-side timbers parted when your boat struck," said Tavi. "We counted them after. It was gone."

"Then they had something to hold." Ansel put his face in his hands. Between his fingers he said, "Dorrit pushed Brann to it. She was sinking, and she pushed him to it."

Nobody spoke for a while. On the terraces below, someone was singing, a few notes over and over, the way Brann did.

---

"Why?" Ansel said at last. He lifted his head. "Why the boom? Why the quiet and the blue and the turning? You have a harbour and boats and cradles that climb cliffs by themselves. You could row to the Long Shore. You could trade. You could tell Saltjaw where the shelf is dangerous and save us drowning on it. Why hide?"

Nessa nodded slowly, as if he had asked the right thing.

"Two answers," she said. "One is old and one is now. The old one first, and you should know it is our telling. We have it in songs and in the count, not in any book you could check.

"A long time ago, the Long Shore found us. A boat came in on a clear year when the blue was thin. They measured us. They drew us on their chart. They were pleased with us; they said we had good stone and good water and good terraces, and they went home and told people. And people came. Not all at once and not with swords at first. With claims. With papers that said this terrace belonged to someone on the Long Shore now, and this cistern, and this cliff, because it was on their chart and we were not on any chart at all. They took the cut stone off the terrace walls to build with. They took the young ones to work. When we would not go, they called us hazards and came back with more boats." She looked out at the blue. "We call that time the Clearing. There were three or four times as many of us before it. After it, the ones who were left made the boom, and learned the count, and learned to keep the blue, and stopped going to the Long Shore. That was the old answer."

"That was a long time ago," Ansel said. "Sorel isn't—" He stopped, because he thought of the Claimscar backers, and Bastien's plan weighted with four bowls, and the green on the tallow, and the word *claim* painted on every upright in Driftfall.

"Perhaps not," said Nessa, kindly. "Perhaps the Long Shore is gentler now. Here is the answer that is now, and it is the one that matters more. We don't hide because we think we are better than you. We have no great secrets. We grow beans and dig cisterns and argue about whose turn it is to mend the boom. We hide because we have decided, every one of us, again and again, that a place on a chart stops being a place and becomes a thing someone owns. To be charted is to be owned. We would rather be unfound and ourselves than found and somebody's." She turned her grey eyes on him. "It is a choice. We make it every year at the turning of the count. We could make another. We don't."

"And when the choice drowns someone?"

That was Tavi, not Ansel.

Nessa did not turn round. "Then we carry it," she said. "As we are carrying it now."

"We're not carrying it. We're sitting on it." Tavi unfolded his arms. "Her people don't know how she died. They'll think it was the sea, or the vent, or the stupid surveyor. They'll never know it was a stone I tied. That's not carrying it, Nessa. That's letting them carry it for us without telling them what it weighs."

"If we tell them, they come."

"Maybe they come. Maybe they don't. We decided a long time ago that they would. We've never checked."

"We checked," said Nessa, very quietly. "That was the Clearing."

Tavi looked at her and then at Ansel, and something in his face that had been held very still came loose for a moment, and he turned and walked away down the terrace steps without another word.

Nessa watched him go. "He's not wrong," she said. "That's the trouble with Tavi. He's very often not wrong." She put her hands on her knees and stood up, slowly. "You'll hear that argument a great deal while you're here. We don't all agree. We agree on the boom and the blue. Past that, we shout at each other, like anyone."

"How long," Ansel said, "is *while I'm here*?"

She did not answer that. She held out her hand to help him up, and after a moment he took it.

## Notes

Chapter 9 of **The Island Saga**. **What struck the boat is resolved:** The Patient Lead came over the [Aumere Turning Boom](../artifacts/aumere-turning-boom.md) at a joint, in fog, on a strong pull of the Counting Current, and came down on a stone weight Tavi Corran had made heavier after a storm. The torn-loose timber is the one Tamsy and Brann survive on. The boom is built to turn boats softly; this time it killed someone. That is the cost the Aumeri carry.

The history of [The Shelf Clearing](../myths/the-shelf-clearing.md) is the islanders' own telling and is recorded at **myth** grade; F432 records do not confirm or deny it. The present-tense ethic is [Aumeri Unfound Ethic](../cultures/aumeri-unfound-ethic.md): *to be charted is to be owned*, chosen yearly, not claimed as superiority. Compare Calareth's hard line in the [Calareth no-moved-stone rule](../rules/calareth-no-moved-stone-rule.md); the Aumeri are not Calareth and keep no ribbon porch.

Open: how the blue is "kept." Nessa uses the phrase; the saga never explains it.
