# The Echo at the Gate

## Premise

Calen Brink arrives in Veloria during echo-rise season and, at the DEB Gate, hears something no load-balance trial ever prepared them for: the stone remembering a bridge that no longer exists.

## Story

Calen had walked six days from Brindle March with a notebook full of crossing weights and a pocket of copper VEL marks converted at the last honest board. They knew Veloria would be large. They did not know it would be **layered**—that the same sunrise could look medieval on one street and glass-bright two rings inward, as if the city were teaching you a lesson with every step.

At the DEB Gate, they copied the local custom without thinking: two fingers to the stone frame, a breath, then through.

The echo hit like a bell struck underwater.

Not sound—**pressure**. Grief and triumph braided so tight Calen could not tell which was older. For one vertiginous instant they stood on a span that was not there, over a unity that had no name in any Brindle trial record. Then the gate was only gate again, and a porter asked if they were ill.

"No," Calen said, and meant it. "I'm listening."

The first ring swallowed them whole. Market bells. Bread smoke. A boy counting marks into his mother's palm while, above everything, the Sky Drifters moved in pale clusters like figures cut from cloud-light. Calen looked up because a woman in travel-gray told them to—"look up before you look in"—and felt the sky echo faintly against the gate-echo, as if the city were answering itself.

By midday Calen had seen the second ring's cleaner lines and the third ring's service drones carrying crate-stacks between towers. Wonder stacked on wonder until their chest hurt with it—not fear. **Capacity**. The world was larger than fairness rituals had ever implied, and some part of them had been waiting for that largeness without knowing.

Near the basin slope, where outer commerce thinned into road camps and flower pots on window sills, Calen stopped for tea. The seller locked her window at dusk and said, almost kindly, "You thought Dias was one place?"

"I did this morning."

"It's one world. That is not the same thing."

That night Calen slept above a workshop and woke to a harmonic thrum that seemed to come from every direction at once—the same thrum travelers mention in *First Day in Veloria*, though Calen had not yet read that account. They wrote one line in the waxed notebook before the ink could blur:

*The gate remembers a bridge. The sky remembers something else. I am not ready. I am here anyway.*

At first light they saw Harold pass on the long run, circling the city in a loop no single day could finish—a human rhythm against Veloria's impossible scale. Calen did not speak to him yet. They only thought: **some people map a world by motion. I map it by what refuses to stay silent.**

On the second morning, a market steward with road dust on her boots and laughter in her voice introduced herself as Tamsin Vor, asked what Calen was writing, and did not flinch at the answer.

"Echo-readers show up in echo-rise," she said. "Stay in the basin a few days. The outer rings are kinder to new ears."

It was not a promise. It was an invitation—and in Dias, invitations are often the first form of belonging.

Calen stayed.
