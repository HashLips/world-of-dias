# Overheard at the Bell

## Premise

The whisper reaches The Peel and Bell. Bram's drinks do strange small work. The room builds a story about the hull larger than Kett's sight, and none of the cups opens a way out of the realm.

## Story

The Peel and Bell had a bucket of peels by the door and a bell that meant the room should become one room. Kett Rind arrived with shed-sweet still on her scarf and the particular thirst of a person about to be bad at privacy.

Bram Keel saw it before she ordered. His bad knee had opinions about drama. "If you want it quiet," he said, "you are in the wrong thirst."

"Hullwater," she said.

He poured the clear cold one. Pear skin. No pulp. She swallowed, and the next sentence left her mouth already overheard, a breath early, at the table by the window and at the bench under the board.

"Fruit went into Mercer's hull at the lent field," the room heard, in her voice and slightly ahead of it. "Something came out that rang when it bumped, and it was not fruit-shaped, and they carried it anyway."

Puck Hennet, who had come in from the oxen with dust in his ears, set his bowl down. He had ordered Ox-Patience because Nia had made him promise not to pick a fight with a rumor until the rumor picked him. The drink slowed his blink. It did not slow his dislike.

"Planes," he said, thick and calm. "I knew the air would start lying about crates."

"I saw the crate," Kett said. The Hullwater was done with her. The rest was ordinary mouth, which was still plenty. "I didn't see inside."

That honesty should have shrunk the story. It fed it. A sorter who would not invent the center was a sorter you could trust with the edges. Within a quarter hour the edges had grown hats: the crate was a body, the crate was a weapon, the crate was a Softfruit so perfect it had become illegal. Nia Toll, chalk still under a nail, knocked her cup once on the wood.

"It is a count," she said. "Don't make it a ghost until a ghost pays the toll. And if anyone here drank their feet quiet so they could sneak out and 'check the shed,' Bram, I will assume Redmile Quiet and I will assume stupidity."

Bram lifted the dark sip he had refused a man ten minutes earlier. "He asked. I gave him small beer and a look. Quiet feet are how people get called raid for the price of a joke."

Someone who wanted beauty instead of fear ordered Gilding Bitters. The room golded. Bruises looked like sunset. Third-ring bulbs pretending to be oil-lamps became, briefly, the basin's best hour. Eyes watered. The gold left. The story did not. A third-ring coat at the end of the counter, too clean for the bench, asked whether the drink was "resonant transit." Bram's face did the work of a slammed door.

"It is bitters," he said. "You will see yellow and you will cry like a person who cut an onion. You will still be in F432. If you wanted a ferry you can go buy a story from a fool. I sell cups."

Puck, against his own patience, ordered Sky-Salt. Foam collapsed. Salt finished. His chin lifted because the neck insisted, and he saw, through the high window, a pale cluster far above Veloria, steady, unlanded.

"Drifters," he said. "Not for hire. Don't put them in your crate story. They don't come down to carry fruit or men or excuses."

The room looked up, animal and brief. Nothing descended. The cluster did not rearrange itself into an answer. Kett felt the shame of having started a thing that now ate other things. She also felt the mean relief of no longer being the only mouth. Bell-Milk went around on a dare. A carter's voice rang a half-step sharp for the better part of an hour, telling the hull tale until his throat hurt and the tale had put on boots.

Bram did not ring the bell. The room had already become one room without his help. On the Peel Bell Board the chalk cup waited, empty of this particular place. Not yet. Gossip was still a drink. It had not yet been made to stand up and spell a field.
