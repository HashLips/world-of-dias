# Valhop Origins and Spread

## Name

Valhop Origins and Spread. On the benches this is "where the Hare came from," and it is never asked only once.

## Overview

**Valhop Origins and Spread** is the street history of how **Valhop** (*the Hare*) grew from a dice toss in the dust into the folding board game played across Nauw, Wabet, and Sorel. It records the stages in which the rules were added, the road it travelled, and who claims what.

What it is: a recent, living-memory history. Valhop belongs to the lived **Present**, not to the Prime Age, the Settling Present, or the Long Gate era. F432 people count time in family lines rather than numbered years (see Known Eras and Markers), and Valhop is counted the same way: it is about **three generations old**. The oldest back-benchers learned it with boxes and creatures but no hare; their parents remember the scratched box; their grandparents only ever tossed for the last fruit. It is younger than the Eastbound Fruit Road's waystations, younger than Fruit-Road Mother Wen's sharing-knife law, and much younger than the Long Gate crossing.

What it is not: an ancient rite, a resonant discovery, or a game any office invented. Nobody holds a founding record. The stages below are what players broadly agree on; the names and places attached to each stage are **disputed**, and the disputes are part of the fun.

## Beliefs and Values

- **The game grew by being argued with.** Every rule is remembered as the answer to a quarrel: a die under a cart, a die that never left the box, a swap that ended a game in a sulk.
- **Nobody owns it.** Each region claims one piece and admits, when pressed, that the other pieces came from somewhere else.
- **A rethrow is older than the board.** Players like to say the first rule of Valhop was "throw it again," and that everything else was added later to need it less often.

## Practices and Traditions

### Where it began

The common account places the first games at a **waystation on the Eastbound Fruit Road**, most often named as **Fruit-Road Waystation Seven**. Convoys between Wabet's orchards and Nauw's markets rest there in shade and soup-steam, and a rest at a fruit-road waystation is long, because stewards time it to heat and bruising rather than to a clock. Bored drivers, keepers, and loaders with pockets full of fruit pits had time to fill and one recurring question: who gets the last fruit in the bowl, and who pours the first round of Waystation Soup-Carrot.

The answer was to toss a pit-die on the ground. Highest number won the fruit; lowest poured the soup.

### Stages of the rules

| Stage | What was added | The quarrel it settled |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. The ground toss** | Two fruit-pit dice, sanded square, tossed on packed earth. High wins. | Who gets the last fruit; who pours. |
| **2. The bump rule** | A die touched by anything after it leaves the hand is thrown again. | Dice rolled under carts, into troughs, and against draft-pony hooves. |
| **3. The dust box** | A square scratched in the dirt; the die must be **dropped** from above and **touch the box first**, or it scores nothing. | Long sideways throws that ended in the trough. Dropping kept dice near the bowl. |
| **4. The minus box** | A die that **stays** in the box scores minus its number. | Players who learned to drop dead-straight and never leave the box. The saying was "a die that never leaves home has seen nothing." |
| **5. The cloth** | Boxes drawn in charcoal on crate sacking, so the game could ride on a tailboard. | Dust boxes scuffed out by every boot and wheel. |
| **6. Creature blocks** | Smaller boxes around the big one, each named for an animal the convoys knew, with a multiplier: frog, moth, cat, bear, and wolf. Higher number, smaller block. | Drivers wanted a reason to aim. The bear got 20 as a joke, because a Soft-Bear on the road is a rare, lucky sight. |
| **7. Rocks and rows** | Two rows of circles and a waxed score rock each; a round's winner moves one circle; first to the end wins. | Single tosses were over too fast to fill a soup's simmer. Seven round wins is about one good pot. |
| **8. The hare and the pebble** | The hatched hare block (-x) and the Switcheroo pebble that lets its taker swap one round's scores. | A game that was all aim and no mercy. The hare "gives way," and hands you a second chance for it. |
| **9. The last-round limit** | The pebble cannot be used in the round that ends the game. | Too many last-round swaps ended in sulks rather than poured bowls. |
| **10. The folding board** | A block-printed sheet with one common face, folded once across a blank gap, tied, and carried in a pouch with die, rock, and pebble. | Charcoal cloths smudged, and nobody's blocks were the same size twice. |

The sun at the top and the side names **Team Sun** and **Team Moon** arrived with the rows. Two rows needed names, and a crescent and a rayed disc were the easiest marks to scratch at the ends of a line. They remain the game's marks only and say nothing about the sky.

### How it spread

Valhop travelled at wagon pace. On the Eastbound Fruit Road a good day is about **25 km**, and Veloria City lies about **1,400 km** from Wabet's orchard approaches (see F432 span and travel distances). A game invented at a waystation reached the Nauw markets the way fruit does: one convoy, one season, one bench at a time.

- **East to Nauw.** Drivers taught it at every stop between the waystations and the Basin. It reached the back benches of **Softfruit Table Hall** in Veloria, where the loser's first bowl met the hall's stacked clay bowls and stayed there.
- **West into Wabet.** Returning convoys carried it home to the orchards and the **Verdant Choir Meadows**, where it went quiet: scores on fingers, mouthed sayings, soft claps.
- **South to Sorel.** Two roads carried it. Fruit runners returning empty went west and south through the **Westfold Toll** into **Hearthvale** (Sorel and Wabet lie about 1,300 km apart by those approaches). People leaving Nauw for a second beginning came down through **Long Gate** with a die in a pocket and nothing else that was theirs. Both met at **Secondfire**, where the cooling waits at Kiln Laughter Yard gave the game a permanent home.

Local dice followed local material: fruit pits stayed in Nauw because that is where the game started; Wabet turned to quiet timber offcuts; Sorel pressed dice from kiln clay.

Printed boards spread by price as much as by road. Kraft-paper prints were cheap enough to give away and light enough to fold into a pocket, so they went everywhere a die did. Cloth boards, which cost more, followed more slowly, and are still the minority on most benches.

## Lore

### Who is credited, and who argues

No single inventor is named, and every claim has a counter-claim.

- **Waystation keepers** say the dust box was first scratched at Seven with the tip of a sharing-knife. Rival keepers point out that waystations get renumbered when a washout moves the road, so the game may have started at a Seven that is now somebody else's Six. Nobody has settled who does the renumbering, and Seven's keepers will not discuss it during a game.
- **Nauw back-benchers** say Valhop was only "dice with manners" until the hare arrived, and that the hare was a Nauw idea. The hare block reads as a Softlane hare yielding the road, and *Hare-kind* is old Nauw Market Slang for "go gentle." The street name *the Hare* dates from this stage, which suits the claim. An off-duty Softfruit Ladle Host is sometimes credited with the last-round limit. Since the Host is a rotating role, every Host since has accepted the credit.
- **Wabet players** say the creatures are Wabet creatures (the choir-frog and the Soft-Bear above all), so the board is a Wabet board with Nauw noise added.
- **Sorel players** say it was a game until Secondfire made it a **board**. Secondfire block-cutters, used to seals and stamps, are said to have carved the first common face so every board could match. Veloria copy rooms also claim the first print. The marked backs of Sorel boards are usually offered as evidence; Veloria answers that a busy back proves enthusiasm, not invention.
- **Veloria's copy rooms**, when they make their claim, usually point to **Mirel Fairproof**, a Lineborn copy-room junior turned block-cutter whose fair copy of the rules became the standard face most people play on today. Mirel herself claims only the first *fair* print, not the first print, and says that if Secondfire cut the face first, she has copied it faithfully. Neither side counts this as settling anything.
- **Convoy toasts** sometimes credit Fruit-Road Mother Wen herself. This is impossible, since Wen is generations older than the game, and everyone singing it knows. It is a toast, not a claim.

When two claimants meet, the custom is to settle it with a game. The loser concedes the point until the next game, which is why no claim has ever stayed settled.

### Why the dispute stays friendly

Each region's claim covers a piece the others still use every day. Nauw keeps the hare, Wabet the creatures, Sorel the board, the road the box. Arguing about it is a way of saying the game is shared.

## Kindness practice

When a newcomer asks where Valhop came from, give them every region's version, then hand them a die.

## Hard edge

Using the origin quarrel to sneer at another region's players, or claiming a founding right to decide anyone else's rules. The street answer is the oldest rule: throw it again.
