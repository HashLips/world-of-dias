# Static Moss

## Name

Static Moss

## Overview

**Static Moss** is a bristling moss that thickens near residual static and conduit glass in Veloria, including edges of **Calibration Row**. People use it as a weather tell for “something is still humming,” not as a precise instrument. In the Calibration Saga it reacts when the residual calibration seam refuses to close—and later when post-escape static drift moves through pipes.

## Description

The moss grows in thin mats on brick, conduit housings, and the cool outside faces of workshop glass. At rest it looks ordinary and gray-green. Near active residual charge the tips lift and stiffen, as if the wall were listening. Row hands call that bristling “static weather.” Operators may call the same patch noise and file past it.

Static moss is common along third-to-core approaches and outside alcoves where instruments run long. When Orrin Veld’s **Spectrum Frame** leaves a residual calibration seam with porch-light precision, moss on the conduit outside the workshop bristles while he tries incomplete power-down. The moss does not create the seam, invite the Caliburn Echoform, or graph background bands F120 / F380 / F610 / F840 (or disputed F960). It only shows that careful looking left a charge in the building.

After the vault latch fails and the Caliburn escapes into Veloria load-noise, some mats stay restless near anonymous drop routes and queue ledges. Folk blame wrong ticks on the moss; sober craftspeople say the moss is reporting, not causing. It is not a ferry marker and not a Frequency Zero clue.

## Local Use

- Watched before opening shutters or plugging heel-cap contacts: heavy bristle means wait, wipe glass, or move the tea elsewhere.
- Avoided as bedding or tea; bristled tips irritate skin and taste like dust.
- Joked about as “the wall’s eyebrows” during late mutter echoes and post-escape static drift.
- Sometimes scraped from glass before serious graphing so filament does not catch; scraping is manners, not exorcism.
- Never sold as proof that an Echoform is present—bristle can mean ordinary load weather.
