# The Last Fruit at Seven

## Name

The Last Fruit at Seven. Also told as "the pony that invented Valhop."

## Summary

The street legend of the first Valhop throw: two convoy hands at Fruit-Road Waystation Seven reach for the last fruit in the public bowl, a keeper settles it with fruit-pit dice, and a draft-pony's hoof starts the game's oldest rule. It teaches that the game exists to keep a small want from splitting a table.

What it is: a recent, much-retold rumor about the first afternoon of a game about three generations old. What it is not: a record. Nobody wrote it down, the names have worn off, and every region tells it with its own furniture. The plainer history is **Valhop Origins and Spread**.

## Myth Text

It was the end of a long rest at Seven, in the heavy part of a Fruitcarry afternoon, and there was one fruit left in the public bowl.

It was not a Lucky Fruit. Tellers are firm about that, because everyone has heard what a perfect fruit can do to a convoy, and this was an ordinary one with a bruise on its shoulder. It was simply the last. Two hands reached for it at once: a loader from the Wabet end and a driver from the Nauw end. Neither wanted to let go first, and neither wanted to be the one who held on.

The keeper that afternoon, whose name every teller has lost, did not want a shouting match over a bruised fruit at a station that shouts ethics before prices. So the keeper took two fruit pits from the drying tray, squared ones that children had been scratching dots into to pass the time, and said: "High number takes the fruit. Low number pours the soup."

The loader threw first. The pit rolled under the trough. The driver threw second. The pit bounced off the hoof of a dozing draft-pony, which woke, stepped on it, and looked at everyone as though they had been rude.

"Again," said the keeper. "Anything that touches it after your hand, you throw again."

That, the tellers say, was the first rule of Valhop.

To keep the next throw out of the trough, the keeper scratched a square in the dust with the tip of the station's sharing-knife and said the pit had to land in the square first. The loader dropped a five into the square. The driver dropped a two. The loader won the fruit.

Then the keeper held out the sharing-knife, and the whole station laughed, because at Seven the person who cuts a fruit offers the first slice outward. The loader cut the fruit and handed the first half to the driver. The driver, beaten and fed, poured the soup for everyone.

They played again the next day, because the rest was long and the square was still in the dust. By the end of the week someone had decided that a pit which never left the square should cost you, and someone else had drawn a frog.

### Regional versions

- **Nauw tellers** make the driver the hero, and insist that the first thing the driver said after pouring was "Hare-kind," go gentle, because a game you lose kindly is a game people will sit down to again.
- **Wabet tellers** tell the whole thing in a hush. In their version the choir-frogs stop singing while the pits are in the air and start again when the fruit is cut, and the loader passes the half with both hands and without a word.
- **Sorel tellers** add a third person who was not there: someone who wandered in afterwards, heard the story, and carved the first proper board so the square would stop blowing away. Sorel will not say who. Sorel says that is the point.
- **Convoy songs** sometimes hand the sharing-knife to Fruit-Road Mother Wen. Everyone singing knows Wen is far older than the game. They sing it anyway, because a good knife deserves a good name.

### The lesson

The prize was never the fruit. The winner's prize was the right to give the first slice away, and the loser's forfeit was feeding everyone. Players still say it when someone takes a game too seriously: "Remember Seven. You're throwing for who gets to share."

## Notes

Status stays **rumor**: street folklore, not a dated record. The waystation's number is part of the joke: rival keepers note that waystations are renumbered when washouts move the road, so the first game may have been at a station now called something else. That question stays open. The first-bowl custom and the bump rethrow in [`Valhop rules`](../rules/valhop-rules.md) are read as descendants of this tale.
